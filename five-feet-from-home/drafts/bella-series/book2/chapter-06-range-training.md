# Chapter 6: Range Training

## Book 2: "The Rain in Spain"
### Part Three: The Transformation

---

The morning mist still clung to the trees when Bella walked out to the range.

Hawk was already there—checking targets, setting up drills with the same precision he used for everything. He looked up as she approached and gave her the faintest nod.

---

Bella was learning what tactical attire meant.

Gone were the silk blouses and tailored trousers. Today she wore BSS-issued range gear: cargo pants in a muted olive that had more pockets than she knew what to do with, a fitted black moisture-wicking shirt with the small BSS logo on the chest, and boots—actual boots, tactical ones that laced up to mid-calf and made her feel like she was pretending to be someone else.

Her hair was pulled back in a tight French braid, the way Kendra had taught her. "Keep it out of your face and off your neck," Kendra had said. "You'll thank me when you're sweating."

She was already sweating.

Hawk, by contrast, looked completely at home. He wore what he always wore to the range: broken-in tactical pants in khaki, a simple black t-shirt that stretched over shoulders that still looked capable of anything, and boots that had clearly seen actual combat. His sleeves were pushed up to his elbows, revealing forearms marked with old scars. A ball cap shaded his eyes—faded green, probably from his Ranger days.

He moved like his clothes were an extension of his body. Bella still felt like she was wearing a costume.

But less every day.

---

"Fire pit rules," he said, handing her ear protection and a set of magazines. "How are you doing?"

Bella smiled faintly. "Sore from last time. But steady."

"And how can I support you?"

She surprised herself with the firmness of her answer: "By not going easy on me."

Hawk's grin was quick, wolfish. "Now we're getting somewhere."

They started simple. Five rounds at 25 meters. Grip, stance, breath. Bella flinched at the recoil, adjusted, reset. Hawk corrected her gently but firmly—stepping behind her to shift her elbows, guiding her shoulders into position.

"Don't muscle it," he reminded her. "This isn't about force. It's about rhythm. Same as life."

As the sun climbed, they moved further back. Fifty meters. A hundred. Hawk taught her how to read the wind, how to time her shots between her own heartbeats, how to make patience a weapon.

"Most people rush the trigger because they're afraid of missing," he said as he reloaded a magazine. "But the odds don't favor the one who panics. The odds favor the one who waits for the opening. That's chess. That's combat. That's life."

Bella took her time on the next shot. Breathed in, exhaled, squeezed slow. The round landed clean.

Her smile was small but radiant. "Hit."

"Damn right you did." Hawk's voice carried the quiet pride of a father watching his kid succeed.

They stayed through the afternoon. Moving drills—walking while keeping sights aligned. Transitions—pistol to rifle, rifle back to pistol. He never barked orders; he explained, demonstrated, stepped back and let her try.

Between drills, they talked. Sometimes about nothing—food, movies, old stories. Sometimes about everything—angles, decision-making under pressure, how to balance odds and instinct.

At one point, after a string of clean shots, Bella lowered her weapon and laughed—the sound bubbling out unguarded. Hawk chuckled low, shaking his head.

"Feels good, doesn't it?" he said.

"Yeah," Bella admitted. "Like I'm not just watching the board anymore. Like I'm finally making moves."

"That's because you are." He lit a cigar as the sun dipped lower. "And daughter? You're doing better than you think."

By the time they packed up, Bella's arms ached and her clothes smelled of smoke and gunpowder. Her shots weren't perfect, but they were steady.

She'd learned to hold. To breathe. To trust herself, one round at a time.
