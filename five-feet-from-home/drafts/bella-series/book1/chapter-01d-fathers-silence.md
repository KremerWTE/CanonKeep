# Chapter 1d: Father's Silence

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part One: Foundations

---

Her father spoke in systems.

Where her mother communicated in warmth and intuition, Anthony Rossi communicated in organization. The bookstore was his kingdom, and he ruled it with careful, quiet precision—every shelf alphabetized, every display strategically placed, every special order tracked in a ledger he'd kept since before computers made such things obsolete.

"There's a logic to everything, Bella," he told her when she was ten, teaching her to shelve new arrivals. "You just have to find it."

He wore the same thing every day: pressed khakis, a button-down shirt (blue on weekdays, white on weekends), leather shoes polished to a shine that seemed excessive for a small-town bookstore. His reading glasses lived on a chain around his neck—tortoiseshell frames that made him look like the professor he'd once dreamed of becoming, before life got in the way.

She never knew the full story. Just fragments. A scholarship to Penn State, lost when his father got sick. A year of community college, abandoned when the family needed him to work. The bookstore inheritance he'd accepted because there was no other choice, the dream of academia filed away in the same mental drawer where he kept everything too painful to examine.

"Never depend on anyone else's timeline," he told her once, reshaping a display of new releases with the focus of a surgeon. "Build your own systems. Control what you can control. The rest is just noise."

---

Anthony Rossi was not a warm man.

He loved his children—Bella never doubted that. But his love was expressed in correction rather than affection, in critique rather than compliment. A-minus grades were met with questions about what went wrong, not praise for what went right. Perfect scores were acknowledged with a nod and the expectation that they'd be repeated.

"Your brothers understand this," he said when Bella complained about the pressure. "They don't whine about high standards. They rise to meet them."

She was twelve, crying over a book report that had received a B+. It was the first grade below an A she'd ever gotten.

"I'm not whining—"

"You're upset that you didn't succeed. That's whining. Figure out where you went wrong. Fix it. Move on."

He wasn't cruel. He was just incapable of offering comfort without also offering a lesson. Comfort, in his mind, was the lesson. Fix the problem, and the bad feelings would fix themselves.

It took Bella years to understand that this was his love language—the only one he knew. He'd grown up in a house where emotions were weakness, where problems were solved through action rather than discussion. He was raising her the way he'd been raised, genuinely believing it was the best thing he could do for her.

But understanding didn't make it hurt less.

---

The bookstore was where they connected.

Not through words—Anthony Rossi could go hours without speaking, perfectly content in his world of spine labels and inventory lists. But through the work. Through the quiet satisfaction of a task completed well.

He taught her the Dewey Decimal System when she was eight. By ten, she could shelve faster than any of his part-time employees. By fourteen, she was managing special orders while he handled vendor calls, the two of them moving through the space in synchronized silence.

"You have a good mind for patterns," he said one afternoon—the closest thing to praise he'd ever offered. "You see how things connect. That's rare."

She'd treasured those words for years. Held them close when the silence felt too heavy, when his corrections stung too sharply.

He saw something in her. Even if he never said it directly.

And in his own stilted way, he'd given her a gift: the ability to find order in chaos, to see systems where others saw confusion, to approach problems analytically rather than emotionally.

It would serve her well at BSS. Hawk would recognize it immediately—the strategic mind, the pattern recognition, the capacity to see the board while everyone else was distracted by individual pieces.

She didn't know it then. But her father, in his silent, limited way, had been preparing her for something much bigger than a small-town bookstore.

---

The last conversation they had before she left for college was about inventory.

"I've updated the system," she told him, handing over a folder of spreadsheets she'd spent the summer creating. "It should make quarterly ordering easier."

He flipped through the pages slowly, his face unreadable.

"This is good work," he said finally.

She waited for more. A goodbye, maybe. An acknowledgment that she was leaving, that things would be different now.

"Call your mother when you get there," he added. "She'll worry."

That was it. He turned back to his shelves, and Bella walked out to the car where her mother was waiting, eyes red from crying.

She didn't realize until years later that he'd framed those spreadsheets. Hung them in the back office, behind the door where customers wouldn't see. A shrine to the daughter who'd understood his language when no one else bothered to try.

He'd never told her. But she found them, after he retired. After the bookstore closed. After all the words they never said became permanent silence.

Some people love loudly. Anthony Rossi loved in systems.

And Bella, eventually, learned to read that too.
