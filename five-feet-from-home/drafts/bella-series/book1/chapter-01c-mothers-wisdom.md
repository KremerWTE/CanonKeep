# Chapter 1c: Mother's Wisdom

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part One: Foundations

---

"Watch their hands, not their mouths."

Bella was fourteen the first time her mother said it. They were behind the cafe counter, the morning rush finally dying down, and a man had just left without tipping despite spending twenty minutes flirting with her mother over his cappuccino.

"He seemed nice," Bella said.

"He seemed nice because he wanted something." Her mother wiped down the espresso machine, her movements efficient from years of practice. "But watch what people do, not what they say. His wallet never came out, did it? He wanted attention, not connection. Big difference."

It was the first of many lessons.

Her mother—Maria Rossi, née Martinez—had been running the cafe since before Bella could remember. She'd married into the family business at twenty-two, pregnant with Marcus, and had turned a struggling coffee shop into the heart of their small Pennsylvania town through sheer force of will and an almost supernatural ability to make people feel welcome.

"Everyone who walks through that door is carrying something," her mother would say. "Your job isn't to fix it. Your job is to see it, acknowledge it, and give them a moment where they don't have to carry it alone."

Bella learned to read the regulars. Mr. Patterson came in every morning at 6:15, ordered black coffee, and sat in the corner booth with his newspaper—he was grieving, had been since his wife passed three years ago, and the routine was his anchor. The Henderson twins always ordered the most complicated drinks on the menu and tipped extravagantly—they were lonely, desperate for any interaction that felt personal. Dr. Malik came in on Wednesdays, never made eye contact, and always looked like he hadn't slept—something at the hospital was wearing him down, something he couldn't talk about.

"You're good at this," her mother said one afternoon, watching Bella navigate a difficult customer with practiced grace. "Better than your brothers ever were."

It was the closest thing to a compliment her mother gave easily.

"The boys were always too eager to fix things," her mother continued. "Marcus wanted to argue, Daniel wanted to compete. But you—you just listen. You see what people need before they know they need it. That's a gift, Bella. Don't waste it."

---

The lessons weren't always about customers.

"You're going to want to give yourself away," her mother said one evening, after Bella had spent an hour helping a group of teenagers who'd left their table covered in napkin shreds and paid in loose change. "You're going to think that being helpful is the same as being valuable. It's not."

Bella was sixteen, tired, her apron stained with coffee grounds. "I don't understand."

Her mother sighed, sat down across from her in a rare moment of stillness. She was wearing her usual—a burgundy blouse that matched her lipstick, a gold chain her grandmother had given her, the small pearl studs that never left her ears. Even after twelve hours on her feet, she looked put together. That was its own kind of lesson.

"There's a difference between service and servitude," her mother said. "Service is a choice. It's offering what you have because you want to share it. Servitude is letting people take from you because you don't believe you deserve to keep anything for yourself."

She reached across the table, took Bella's hand.

"I see how you are with your brothers. With your father. With everyone in this town. You're always giving, always helping, always making yourself smaller so others can be bigger. That's servitude, Bella. And it will hollow you out if you let it."

"Then what am I supposed to do?"

"Learn the difference." Her mother squeezed her hand. "Help when you choose to. But never forget that you're allowed to choose."

Bella didn't understand then. It would take years—decades—before those words made sense.

But she remembered them. When the finance firm took and took and never gave back. When the sorority sisters thanked her for fixing everything and never invited her to the inner circle. When she spent her life being useful and never felt valued.

She remembered, and eventually, she started choosing.
