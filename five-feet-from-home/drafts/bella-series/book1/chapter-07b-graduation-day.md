# Chapter 7b: Graduation Day

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part Two: Boston College Years

---

Graduation morning dawned gray and humid, threatening rain that never quite came.

Bella stood in front of her closet—nearly empty now, most of her things packed into cardboard boxes stacked against the wall—and felt the strange vertigo of an ending.

Under the graduation gown, it didn't matter what she wore. But it mattered to her. This was the last time she'd dress as a Boston College student. The last time she'd be this version of herself.

She chose carefully: a simple white shift dress that her mother had sent from home ("For your big day, sweetheart"), nude heels that she'd finally invested in after years of borrowing, a thin gold bracelet that had been a gift from Marcus on her eighteenth birthday. Small pearl earrings. Hair blown out straight, professional, ready for what came next.

When she looked in the mirror, she saw someone who had survived. Not thrived, not conquered, but survived. Four years of being invisible. Four years of being useful. Four years of learning to read rooms and fix problems and fade into backgrounds.

*I made it,* she thought. *I don't know where I'm going, but I made it this far.*

---

The ceremony was held in Alumni Stadium.

Bella sat between strangers—the alphabetical seating chart separated her from everyone she knew—and watched the procession of speakers say the things that speakers always said at graduations. The future is yours. Be bold. Make a difference.

She wondered how many people in the crowd believed any of it.

---

When her row finally shuffled toward the stage, Bella's heart was pounding.

She crossed the platform, shook hands she wouldn't remember, accepted a diploma that felt lighter than four years should weigh. The photographer snapped a photo. Someone called the next name. She walked down the other side and it was over.

Just like that.

All those nights studying. All those events she'd fixed. All those moments of loneliness and self-doubt and quiet determination. They compressed into a single handshake, a single photograph, a single piece of paper that said she'd earned the right to leave.

---

After the ceremony, she found her family in the crowd.

Her mother was crying—the good kind, the proud kind—and her father was doing that stoic thing where his eyes were suspiciously bright but he refused to acknowledge it. Marcus was there with Catherine, looking polished and slightly bored. Daniel had actually shown up, which surprised everyone.

"We're so proud of you, sweetheart," her mother said, pulling her into a hug that smelled like home. "Our baby girl. A college graduate."

"Thanks, Mom."

Her father squeezed her shoulder. "You did good, Bells."

It was simple. It was enough.

---

Tessa found her later, mascara slightly smudged, grinning like a maniac.

"We did it!" She grabbed Bella's hands and spun her around, drawing stares from nearby families. "We actually freaking did it!"

"I know."

"Four years! Four whole years! And now we're—" Tessa's face crumpled slightly. "We're going different directions, aren't we?"

Bella felt the squeeze in her chest. Tessa was heading to New York. Bella was staying in Boston, at least for now, trying to build a career that would help support her struggling parents.

"Different cities," Bella said. "Not different directions. Not really."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

They hugged in the middle of the crowd—two girls who'd met as strangers and become each other's lifelines—and neither one wanted to let go.

---

That night, her family took her to dinner at a restaurant that felt too expensive for their budget.

Bella watched her parents pretend they weren't worried about the bill. She watched Marcus talk about his latest case. She watched Daniel check his phone every thirty seconds. She watched and cataloged and filed it all away.

This was her family. Imperfect, complicated, trying their best.

She was leaving them behind, in a way. Moving into a future that felt uncertain and terrifying.

But she was also carrying them with her. The lessons from the cafe. The quiet of the bookstore. The warmth her mother gave freely. The precision her father taught by example.

She was a Rossi. Whatever came next, that wouldn't change.
