# Chapter 11c: The Decision

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part Four: The Bridge to BSS

---

The meeting with Addie lasted two hours.

By the end of it, Bella's entire understanding of her future had shifted.

---

"What you're describing," Bella said slowly, "isn't really consulting."

Addie smiled over the rim of her ceramic cup. "What is it, then?"

"It's..." Bella searched for the right word. "Fixing. On a larger scale. With resources I can't even imagine."

"That's one way to put it."

"What's the other way?"

Addie leaned back in her chair. The coffee shop was quiet at this hour—professionals at work, students in class, nobody to overhear.

"We solve problems that can't be solved through normal channels. We protect people who need protecting. We manage crises that would destroy reputations, families, companies, lives." She paused. "We do it quietly. We do it well. And we do it with a network of women who understand that power isn't always visible."

Bella's heart was pounding. "The Wives Club."

Addie's eyebrows rose slightly. "You've heard of it."

"Whispers. From a friend. Years ago."

"Vee Sinclair, I assume." At Bella's surprised look, Addie smiled. "We pay attention. It's what we do."

---

The offer was simple in structure, terrifying in scope.

Join BSS as an analyst. Learn the systems, the networks, the way the organization operated. Prove herself through work, through reliability, through the same skills that had made her indispensable everywhere else.

But this time, with mentorship. With purpose. With a path that led somewhere.

"I want you specifically," Addie said. "Not because you're the smartest—there are plenty of smart people. Not because you're the most experienced—you're not. But because you have something rare."

"What's that?"

"Instinct. You read rooms the way some people read text. You see patterns before they become visible. You solve problems before people realize they have them." Addie held her gaze. "And you do it without needing credit. Without needing recognition. Just because it needs doing."

Bella felt something shift in her chest. "You make it sound noble."

"It is noble. It's also exhausting, and sometimes thankless, and occasionally dangerous. But if you're the person I think you are—it might also be exactly what you're meant to do."

---

That night, Bella made lists.

Reasons to stay:
- Stability
- Known quantities
- Family needs money
- Fear of the unknown

Reasons to go:
- Everything else

---

She called her mother.

"There's an opportunity," Bella said. "In North Carolina. A different kind of work."

"Tell me about it."

So she did. Not the classified parts—she instinctively knew there were classified parts—but the essence. The crisis management. The network of women. The mentorship.

Her mother was quiet for a long moment.

"You sound excited," she finally said.

"I'm terrified."

"Those aren't mutually exclusive." A pause. "You know what I've never told you?"

"What?"

"When you were little, I used to watch you in the cafe. You'd see a customer having a hard day, and you'd bring them an extra cookie without being asked. You'd notice when the sugar containers were empty and refill them before I could. You were five years old, and you were already taking care of everyone around you."

Bella felt tears prick at her eyes. "Mom..."

"You've been taking care of this family for so long. Your father and I—we've relied on you. Your brothers have relied on you. And we haven't always said thank you."

"You don't have to—"

"Let me finish." Her mother's voice was firm. "You deserve to take care of yourself for once. To chase something that's just yours. And if this is it... go. We'll figure out the rest."

"But the money—"

"Is not your responsibility. We managed before you started sending it, and we'll manage after. You're not a credit card, sweetheart. You're our daughter. And daughters are supposed to fly."

---

She resigned the next Friday.

Her supervisor looked confused, then annoyed, then professionally neutral.

"Charlotte?" he said, like the city was a foreign country. "What kind of opportunity?"

"A private consulting firm. Crisis management."

"Hmm." He drummed his fingers on his desk. "Well. Good luck, I suppose."

That was it. Three years of late nights and rescued projects and quiet excellence, and her departure merited a two-minute conversation and a handshake that felt like an afterthought.

She packed her desk into a single box and walked out without looking back.
