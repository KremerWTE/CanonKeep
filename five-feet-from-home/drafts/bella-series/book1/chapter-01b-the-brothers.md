# Chapter 1b: The Brothers

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part One: Foundations

---

Marcus was five years older than Bella. Daniel was three.

In the hierarchy of the Rossi household, they were the suns around which everything orbited. Marcus, the firstborn, had their father's quiet intensity and their mother's social grace—a combination that made him terrifyingly competent at everything he tried. Valedictorian. Captain of the debate team. Scholarship to Georgetown, then law school at Columbia. By the time Bella was learning to make lattes, Marcus was clerking for a federal judge.

Daniel was different—louder, more physical, with a laugh that filled rooms and a competitive streak that bordered on obsessive. He'd been the athlete Marcus never was, all-state in soccer and baseball, recruited to play Division I before a knee injury senior year redirected him toward business. He recovered, adapted, excelled. That was what Rossi men did.

Bella watched them from the edges of family dinners, listened to their accomplishments announced like headlines. "Marcus made partner." "Daniel closed his first major deal." "Your brothers are doing so well," relatives would say, patting her hand like she was an afterthought. "And how's school, dear?"

She was fine. She was always fine.

The thing about being the youngest, the only girl, the quiet one—nobody expected much, and nobody was disappointed. Her brothers had cleared such a high bar that anything Bella did felt like a footnote. Good grades? Of course—she was a Rossi. Helpful at the business? Expected. Nothing she achieved was ever quite achievement; it was just her being what she should be.

Marcus came home for Thanksgiving her junior year of high school, engaged to a woman named Catherine who wore cashmere like a second skin and had never worked a service job in her life. Daniel brought his girlfriend—a different one than last year, blonder and louder and wearing a designer dress to a turkey dinner.

Bella wore jeans and an old sweater, helped her mother in the kitchen, and listened to the men talk about markets and cases and futures.

"You should think about law school," Marcus told her over pumpkin pie. "You're smart enough. Dad would love it."

"Or business," Daniel added. "Finance is booming. I could get you an internship."

They meant well. They always meant well. But they never asked what she wanted. They assumed she'd want what they had—the degrees, the prestige, the visible success. They couldn't imagine that she might want something else entirely.

She didn't know what that something else was yet. But she knew it wasn't this—sitting at the edge of the table, wearing her brother's hand-me-down confidence, waiting to be told who to become.

---

Marcus still called her "Bells" like she was twelve. He'd ruffle her hair when he visited, ask about her grades, give unsolicited advice about her future like he'd cornered the market on success.

Daniel was warmer but more absent—bear hugs when he remembered, months of silence when he didn't. He'd married young, divorced younger, remarried to someone Bella had met exactly twice. His life was a blur of deals and flights and whatever city he was closing in this week.

They loved her. She never doubted that.

But love wasn't the same as seeing. And her brothers had never really seen her—not the analytical mind behind the helpful hands, not the quiet ambition buried under years of being overlooked. They saw their baby sister, the one who'd always be a few steps behind, and they loved that version of her without ever asking if there was more.

There was more. There had always been more.

She just didn't know how to prove it yet.
