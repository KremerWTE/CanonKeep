# Chapter 2c: Summer Before College

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part One: Foundations

---

The last summer at home tasted like espresso and endings.

June bled into July bled into August, each day a little shorter than the last, each shift at the cafe weighted with unspoken goodbyes. Bella memorized the things she'd taken for granted—the way morning light hit the front windows, the creak of the floorboards behind the counter, the smell of her father's cologne lingering in the bookstore doorway.

She was leaving. For the first time in her life, she was leaving.

"You'll come back for Thanksgiving," her mother said, folding clothes into Bella's suitcase with aggressive efficiency. "And Christmas. And spring break, probably, unless you have other plans—"

"Mom."

"—and summer, definitely summer, we'll need you for summer—"

"Mom." Bella put her hand over her mother's, stilling the frantic folding. "I'm going to Boston, not Mars."

Her mother's eyes were wet. "I know. I know that. I just..."

She didn't finish. She didn't have to.

Maria Rossi had poured eighteen years into this daughter—the quiet one, the capable one, the one who'd learned to read people and manage crises and make herself indispensable. And now that daughter was leaving, and there was nothing to do but let her go.

"You're going to be wonderful," her mother whispered. "You're already wonderful. Just... remember that, okay? When it gets hard. When you're surrounded by people who seem like they have it all figured out. Remember that you're enough. Exactly as you are."

Bella nodded, not trusting her voice.

She wouldn't remember, not really. Not for years. But the words would be there, waiting, for the moment she was finally ready to hear them.

---

Her father handled the departure differently.

No tears. No speeches. Just a week of unusual presence—appearing at the cafe during her shifts, finding excuses to work alongside her in the bookstore, offering stilted comments about weather and inventory that were really saying something else entirely.

The night before she left, he called her into his office.

"I have something for you."

He handed her a small box. Inside was a leather notebook, the cover worn soft from years of handling. She recognized it immediately—it was the ledger he'd kept since taking over the bookstore, the one filled with his meticulous handwriting and careful records.

"I don't understand," she said. "This is yours."

"It was mine. Now it's yours." He cleared his throat, uncomfortable with the weight of the moment. "I'm not good at... saying things. You know that. But I want you to know that everything I taught you—the systems, the patterns, the way to see order in chaos—that wasn't just about books. That was about life. About how to navigate a world that doesn't always make sense."

He looked at her, and for a moment, the walls came down.

"You're going to go far, Isabella. Farther than your brothers, farther than I ever could. Not because you're smarter, though you are. But because you see things. You see people. And that's a gift I could never give you—you were born with it."

Bella clutched the notebook to her chest, tears streaming down her face.

"Dad—"

"Just promise me you won't waste it. Don't let anyone convince you that you're less than what you are. Don't disappear into someone else's vision of your life." He paused, struggling with the words. "You're a Rossi. We don't disappear."

It was the most he'd ever said to her. The most he would ever say.

She hugged him—awkwardly, because physical affection wasn't something they did—and felt his arms come around her slowly, like he was remembering how.

"Thank you," she whispered.

"Call your mother," he said gruffly, pulling away. "She'll worry."

It was his way of saying I love you. After eighteen years, she finally understood.

---

The drive to Boston took six hours.

Her mother talked the entire way—about meal plans and laundry services and which buildings were which on the campus map she'd printed out three times just to be safe. Her father drove in silence, hands steady on the wheel, eyes fixed on the road ahead.

Bella sat in the backseat surrounded by boxes, watching Pennsylvania give way to New York give way to Massachusetts, each state line a threshold she couldn't un-cross.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Megan: Miss you already! Call me when you get there!

She typed back a heart emoji and didn't mean it. Megan had been a friend of convenience, not connection. She wouldn't miss Bella, not really. And Bella wouldn't miss her either.

That was the thing about leaving: it clarified what actually mattered.

---

They arrived at Boston College in late afternoon, the campus golden in the August light.

Her dorm room was small—a concrete box with two beds, two desks, and a window that looked out on a parking lot. Her roommate hadn't arrived yet. Her name was Tessa, according to the housing assignment, and she was from somewhere in Connecticut.

"This is nice," her mother said, determinedly cheerful as she made Bella's bed with hospital corners. "Cozy. You'll make it your own."

Her father carried boxes in silence, stacking them with the same precision he brought to everything.

By the time they finished, the sun was setting. They stood in the small room—Bella and her parents, the three of them suddenly uncertain what came next.

"Well," her father said.

"Well," her mother echoed.

"I'll be fine," Bella said. "I promise."

Her mother pulled her into a fierce hug. "I know you will. That's not why I'm crying."

"Then why?"

"Because you're going to be more than fine. You're going to be magnificent. And I'm going to miss watching it happen up close."

They left as the streetlights flickered on. Bella stood at her window, watching their car disappear around the corner, and felt the full weight of being alone for the first time in her life.

It was terrifying.

It was also, she realized, exactly what she needed.
