# Chapter 4c: Breaking Point with David

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part Two: Boston College Years

---

The breaking point came on a Tuesday.

There was nothing special about the day. No anniversary, no argument, no dramatic revelation. Just a Tuesday in April, rain streaking the windows of David's apartment, and Bella suddenly certain that she couldn't do this anymore.

"Hey." David looked up from his casebook, reading glasses slightly askew. "You okay? You've been staring at the wall for ten minutes."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You haven't been fine for weeks." He closed the book, gave her his full attention. "Talk to me, Bella. Whatever it is, we can figure it out."

That was the problem. He always wanted to figure it out. He always believed that problems had solutions, that love was enough, that she was worth the effort of understanding.

She wasn't. She couldn't be. Because if she let herself believe she was worth it, she would have to accept that losing him was possible—and that was a pain she couldn't survive.

Better to end it now. On her terms. Before he realized what she already knew.

"I think we should break up," she said.

---

David didn't yell. He didn't cry. He just looked at her with those dark, serious eyes and asked the worst possible question:

"Why?"

"We want different things."

"What things? Tell me specifically what we want differently."

She couldn't. She didn't have a real answer, just a feeling—a crushing certainty that this was too good, too safe, too much like everything she didn't deserve.

"I just... I can't do this."

"Can't do what? Love someone who loves you back?" His voice was gentle, not accusatory, and that made it worse. "Bella, I've spent eight months watching you run. From me, from yourself, from anything that feels too real. And I've tried to be patient, tried to give you space, but—"

"You can't fix me." The words came out sharper than she intended. "This isn't something you can figure out with logic and persistence. I'm broken. I've always been broken. And if I stay, I'll just break you too."

David was quiet for a long moment.

"You're not broken," he said finally. "You're scared. There's a difference."

"What difference does it make?"

"It makes every difference. Broken means you can't be fixed. Scared means you haven't tried yet." He stood, crossed to where she sat on the edge of his couch. "I'm not asking you to be perfect, Bella. I'm asking you to try. To trust me. To believe, just for a second, that you might be worthy of the love I'm trying to give you."

She wanted to. God, she wanted to.

But the voice was louder than his. The voice that had whispered since childhood: *You're not enough. You never will be. Everyone who gets close will eventually see the truth and leave.*

If she left first, at least she controlled the narrative.

"I can't," she whispered. "I'm sorry. I just... can't."

---

The breakup was clean. That was the worst part.

David didn't beg. He didn't make her feel guilty. He just looked at her with something like resignation and said, "I hope you find whatever you're looking for, Bella. I hope you let yourself have it when you do."

She cried that night. Tessa held her on the floor of their dorm room while she sobbed into a pillow, unable to explain why she was grieving something she'd chosen to destroy.

"You could call him," Tessa said gently. "Tell him you made a mistake."

"It wasn't a mistake."

"Then why are you crying like your heart is broken?"

Bella didn't have an answer. She just knew that this pain—the pain of losing David—was somehow safer than the alternative. At least this pain was hers. At least she'd chosen it.

Being loved was terrifying. Being left was worse.

So she left first. Every time.

---

She saw David once more, six months later.

He was at a campus coffee shop with a woman Bella didn't recognize—pretty, laughing, leaning into him like she belonged there. They looked happy. They looked like a couple who trusted each other.

Bella watched from across the room, her own coffee growing cold in her hands.

She'd done the right thing. She'd freed him to find someone who could actually accept love, who didn't come with a lifetime of baggage and self-doubt. He was better off without her.

That was the story she told herself.

It was easier than admitting she'd thrown away something real because she was too afraid to believe she deserved it.

---

Years later, sitting by the fire pit with Hawk, she would finally understand what she'd done.

"Self-sabotage," Hawk called it. "The defense mechanism of people who've never been properly loved. You destroy things before they can destroy you. Classic move."

"It felt necessary at the time."

"It always does." He puffed his cigar, watching the flames. "The question is whether you're still doing it. Whether you're going to keep running, or whether you're finally ready to stay."

She'd thought about David that night. About the patient, kind man who'd seen her worth and been sent away for his trouble.

She couldn't undo that. But she could choose differently, next time.

If there was a next time.
