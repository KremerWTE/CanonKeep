# Chapter 8: The Finance Grind

## Book 1: "Wouldn't It Be Loverly"
### Part Three: Boston Professional Years

---

The finance firm occupied three floors of a glass tower in downtown Boston. Bella's desk was on the second floor, wedged between a window she could barely see and a conference room that seemed perpetually booked.

She was an analyst. The job description said things like "financial modeling" and "client portfolio management," but mostly it meant long hours, high pressure, and a culture that felt like a constant competition she hadn't signed up for.

---

The dress code wasn't written down, but it was understood.

For men: dark suits, white or blue shirts, conservative ties. Variations allowed on Fridays.

For women: pencil skirts or tailored trousers, blouses that weren't too feminine, blazers that matched their pants. Heels required (though no one ever said so out loud). Conservative jewelry. Professional hair—styled, but not styled. Put-together, but not attention-seeking.

Bella built her work wardrobe piece by piece, each item chosen for survival rather than expression. She owned three blazers: black, navy, and charcoal gray. She owned five blouses in rotation: three white, one light blue, one soft pink for when she wanted to seem "approachable." She wore the same black heels every day until they wore through, then bought identical replacements.

Her jewelry was minimal—small gold studs in her ears, a simple watch with a leather band, no necklaces (they tangled with conference call headsets). Her hair stayed in low buns or sleek ponytails, nothing that would distract from her competence.

She learned to dress like the women who were succeeding: polished enough to be taken seriously, modest enough not to threaten anyone. Never too fashionable. Never too plain. The narrow corridor of acceptable femininity in finance was one she navigated every morning in front of her closet mirror.

The men wore whatever they wanted within the suit-and-tie framework. The women performed an invisible calculus every single day.

Bella became very good at the calculus. She just wished she didn't have to be.

---

The office was male-dominated in the way that finance offices always were—suits and handshakes and after-work drinks where women were invited but not really welcome. Bella learned to navigate it the same way she'd navigated everything else: by being useful.

She stayed late when projects ran over. She caught errors before they reached clients. She smoothed over conflicts between departments, the same way she'd smoothed over sorority drama. Her managers relied on her. Her colleagues respected her.

But respect wasn't the same as recognition.

"You're a team player," her supervisor told her during her annual review, which sounded like a compliment until she realized it was also an excuse not to promote her. Team players stayed where they were. They didn't push for more.

Bella didn't push.

She told herself the job was practical. The salary was decent. She was building skills, gaining experience, proving she could survive in a world that didn't care whether she succeeded or failed.

But the culture wore on her. The transactional relationships. The way people treated each other like chess pieces to be moved or sacrificed. The constant undercurrent of ambition that felt hollow, like everyone was climbing a ladder they'd never reach the top of.

She was good at the work. She just didn't believe in it.

Meanwhile, back home, her parents were struggling.

COVID had hit the café and bookstore hard. Foot traffic disappeared overnight. Savings dwindled. Her father called less often, but when he did, she could hear the exhaustion in his voice.

"We're managing," he said, which meant they weren't.

Bella sent money when she could. Not enough to fix anything, but enough to feel like she was doing something. Helping. The way she always helped.

She wasn't chasing glory. She was just trying to keep the people she loved from falling.
