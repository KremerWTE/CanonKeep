# Chapter 22: The D.C. Assignment

## Book 3: "I Could Have Danced All Night"
### Part Five: Becoming

---

Washington was different from Charlotte.

Charlotte was new money and old families learning to coexist. D.C. was power—raw, visible, constantly shifting. The city itself seemed to hum with ambition.

"You'll be here for a week," Harper explained during the briefing. "Shadow assignment. Our D.C. team handles things differently than Charlotte. You need to understand the landscape."

"Why me?"

"Because you're ready to see how the sausage gets made at the federal level." Harper's smile was knowing. "And because you need to learn that not every city operates the way Charlotte does."

---

The BSS D.C. office was smaller than Charlotte headquarters—a brownstone near Dupont Circle, anonymous from the outside, secure and sophisticated within.

The team lead was a woman named Victoria Chen—mid-forties, silver-streaked hair, with the bearing of someone who'd spent decades navigating bureaucracies.

"You're Addie's project," Victoria said, not unkindly.

"I'm her mentee."

"In this world, that's the same thing." Victoria gestured for Bella to follow. "Let me show you how we work."

---

D.C. work was different.

Charlotte handled corporate crises, family protection, the private concerns of wealthy clients. D.C. handled the intersection of money and government—lobbying firms with secrets, politicians with skeletons, agencies with leaks.

"We don't fix things here," Victoria explained. "We prevent them. By the time something becomes a crisis, it's usually too late. Our job is to see problems before they develop."

"How do you do that?"

"Relationships. Intelligence. Pattern recognition." Victoria pulled up a screen showing a web of connections. "See this? A lobbying firm. They represent a pharmaceutical company. The company has a drug in FDA review. Three senators on the relevant committee have received campaign contributions from the firm."

"That's legal."

"Completely legal. Also completely predictable." Victoria tapped another node in the web. "This journalist has been asking questions. She's connecting dots. In six weeks, she'll publish a story about the appearance of corruption. By then, our client—the pharmaceutical company—needs to be positioned to respond."

"So we're doing damage control before there's damage."

"We're doing narrative management before there's a narrative." Victoria smiled. "Welcome to Washington."

---

Bella spent the week learning.

She sat in on meetings with legislative aides, learning the language of the Hill. She attended receptions where deals were made over champagne and vague promises. She watched Victoria navigate a room full of people who all wanted something from each other.

"Everyone here is transactional," Victoria said, during a quiet moment. "Charlotte has heart. D.C. has interest. You need to understand both."

"Which one is better?"

"Neither. They're just different games." Victoria sipped her wine. "Charlotte builds families. D.C. builds coalitions. Both are necessary. Both can be ugly up close."

---

The highlight of the trip was a meeting with a senator's chief of staff.

The senator was moderate, well-respected, and had just announced a presidential exploratory committee. The chief of staff—a sharp-eyed woman named Sarah—was managing the delicate transition from legislature to campaign.

"We need discretion," Sarah said. "The Senator has... situations from his past. Nothing illegal. But optics."

"What kind of situations?"

"The usual. Campaign finance irregularities from twenty years ago. An affair that ended badly. A son who has a drug problem." Sarah's voice was clinical. "Nothing that would destroy him. But everything together could create a narrative."

"You want us to manage the narrative before it forms."

"I want you to make sure it never forms at all."

---

Bella worked with the D.C. team on the initial assessment.

The campaign finance issues were manageable—old enough to be explained as mistakes of youth, addressed with appropriate contrition. The affair was more complicated, but the woman had moved on and had no interest in revisiting it publicly. The son was the wild card—still struggling, still capable of making headlines.

"We can manage the first two," Victoria concluded. "The son requires ongoing attention."

"What does that mean?" Bella asked.

"Monitoring. Support. Making sure he has resources if he needs them—and making sure any relapses happen out of sight." Victoria's expression was neutral. "It sounds cold. But protecting someone's privacy isn't the same as covering up wrongdoing."

"Isn't it a fine line?"

"Everything in this city is a fine line." Victoria met her eyes. "That's why we're careful. That's why we document. That's why we only work with clients whose actual behavior can withstand scrutiny."

---

On her last night in D.C., Bella walked the Mall alone.

The monuments glowed in the darkness—Lincoln sitting in contemplation, Jefferson surrounded by his words, the Washington Monument piercing the sky. The people who'd shaped America, frozen in stone.

She thought about power. About the ways it was used—for good, for ill, for maintaining systems that helped some and hurt others.

BSS operated in the shadows of power. They didn't make policy or pass laws. But they shaped the environment in which decisions were made. They protected some people, which meant not protecting others. They managed narratives, which meant controlling information.

Was that good? Was that necessary? Was it something she could believe in?

She didn't have clear answers. But she was starting to understand the questions more deeply.

---

"How was D.C.?" Hawk asked when she returned.

"Eye-opening." Bella sat at the fire pit, accepting the whiskey he offered. "I saw how the sausage gets made."

"And?"

"And I understand why you warned me about power." She stared into the flames. "It's not about good people and bad people. It's about what systems require. What the work demands."

"Does that bother you?"

"It makes me think harder about what I'm willing to do. And what I'm not."

Hawk nodded slowly. "That's the right response. The people who don't question—they're the dangerous ones. Keep asking. Keep evaluating. The moment you stop, you've lost yourself."

"How do you know when you've crossed a line?"

"You don't always know in the moment. But you feel it afterward." His eyes were distant. "I've crossed lines, daughter. In my past. Things I had to do, or thought I had to do. Some of them still haunt me."

"What do you do with that?"

"I try to be better. I try to make different choices now." He met her gaze. "And I try to help people like you understand the stakes before you're in too deep."
