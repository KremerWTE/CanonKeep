# Chapter 12: Going Home

## Book 3: "I Could Have Danced All Night"
### Part Three: The Transformation

---

She hadn't been home in nine months.

It wasn't intentional—or maybe it was. Charlotte had consumed her. The compound, the work, the family she was building there. Her biological family felt like another life, another version of herself that she'd outgrown without meaning to.

But her mother's voice on the phone was worried, and Bella couldn't ignore that anymore.

"The cafe's doing better," her mother said. "Your father even smiled at a customer last week. But we miss you, sweetheart. It's been so long."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Just come visit. Please?"

---

The drive took six hours.

Bella had considered flying, but she needed the time—the slow transition between her new life and her old one. The familiar landscapes of the Northeast rising around her, the exits she recognized from childhood road trips, the way the air smelled different as she crossed into familiar territory.

Her parents' house looked smaller than she remembered.

Not physically—it was the same clapboard colonial it had always been, with the same faded shutters and the same overgrown garden her mother never had time to properly tend. But it felt smaller. Like she'd grown, and the space hadn't grown with her.

Her mother was waiting on the porch.

---

"Look at you." Her mother held her at arm's length, studying her. "You look different."

"Good different or bad different?"

"Just... different. Confident. Like you finally believe in yourself." Her mother's eyes were bright. "I always knew you had it in you."

Bella hugged her tighter than usual.

---

Her father was in the bookstore, as always.

He looked up when she entered, and for a moment his expression was the same careful neutrality he'd worn her whole life. Then something softened.

"Bells." He came around the counter and hugged her—brief, awkward, but real. "Good to see you."

"Good to see you too, Dad."

"Your mother's been counting the days. Literally. She has a calendar in the kitchen."

"I should have come sooner."

"You came when you could." He stepped back, examining her the way he'd examine a rare book—looking for signs of quality, of wear, of worth. "You're doing well."

It wasn't a question, but Bella answered anyway. "I'm doing really well."

"Good." He nodded once. "That's good."

---

The cafe was bustling when she stopped by the next morning.

Her mother moved behind the counter with the same practiced grace she'd always had, but there were changes. New equipment. New menu items. A line that stretched to the door.

"Business is up thirty percent," her mother said, during a brief lull. "Since we rebranded. New logo, new website, new everything."

"The money I sent?"

"Some of it. The rest was loans—but good loans, the kind we can actually pay back." Her mother smiled. "You saved us, Bella. You know that, right? When things were darkest, you stepped up."

"I just did what family does."

"No. Your brothers did what they do—which is nothing." Her mother's voice was sharp with old frustration. "You did more. You always do more."

---

She saw Marcus briefly—dinner at a restaurant he chose, expensive and impersonal.

"So you're still doing that consulting thing," he said, as if she hadn't explained it three times already.

"Crisis management. Yes."

"Right. And that's going well?"

"It's going very well."

He nodded, unconvinced. "Well. Good for you."

Bella didn't explain further. She'd learned long ago that Marcus only heard what fit his picture of her—the little sister who needed guidance, who hadn't quite figured out her life, who was always a few steps behind.

Let him think that. She knew the truth.

---

Daniel didn't come home during her visit.

He was "traveling for work"—the excuse he'd used for years to avoid family gatherings. Bella didn't mind. She'd made her peace with Daniel's absence long ago.

But she noticed her parents' disappointment, carefully hidden beneath polite excuses.

"He's very busy," her mother said.

"I know."

"His new business is really taking off."

"I'm sure."

Some patterns never changed.

---

On her last night home, she sat with her parents in the living room.

The TV was on, volume low—some old movie they'd watched together when she was young. Her father was reading. Her mother was knitting. The familiar rhythms of a family evening she'd known her entire life.

"I have something to tell you," Bella said.

They looked up.

"I've been... adopted. Kind of." She struggled to find the right words. "There are people at my job—a couple—who've become like second parents to me. They call me daughter. They signed a book 'Mom and Dad.' It's..."

She trailed off, not sure how to explain.

Her mother set down her knitting. "Tell us about them."

So Bella did. She told them about Hawk and the fire pit, about Addie and the pendant, about being chosen for who she was rather than what she could do.

When she finished, there was a long silence.

Then her mother said: "Good."

"Good?"

"We love you, Bella. We always will. But we haven't always..." She paused, searching. "We haven't always been good at showing it. At making you feel seen. At giving you what you needed."

Her father spoke then, quiet but certain: "You deserve people who choose you. We're glad you found them."

Bella's eyes filled with tears.

"That doesn't mean you're not our daughter," her mother added quickly. "You'll always be our daughter. But you can have more than one family. More than one home."

"I know."

"Good." Her mother picked up her knitting again. "Now tell us more about this Hawk person. He sounds formidable."
