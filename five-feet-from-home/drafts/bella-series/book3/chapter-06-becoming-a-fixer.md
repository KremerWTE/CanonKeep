# Chapter 6: Becoming a Fixer

## Book 3: "I Could Have Danced All Night"
### Part Two: Integration

---

The promotion wasn't announced.

One day Bella was an analyst with access. The next day, people started calling her with problems.

"Kendra said you could help," a voice on the phone said—one of the East Coast clients, someone she'd never spoken to directly. "We have a situation."

Bella pulled up her notepad. "Tell me everything."

---

The fixer's job was different from the analyst's job.

Analysts gathered information. Organized data. Identified patterns. The work was cerebral, confined to screens and spreadsheets and the careful logic of probability.

Fixers solved problems.

They took the patterns analysts found and translated them into action. They made phone calls, built relationships, navigated social dynamics that couldn't be reduced to algorithms. They understood that some situations required a human touch that no amount of data could replace.

"The role has three requirements," Addie explained during one of their mentorship sessions. "First: you have to see the problem clearly, without getting emotionally tangled in it. Second: you have to understand people—what motivates them, what scares them, what they need. Third: you have to be willing to get your hands dirty when necessary."

"Dirty how?"

"Not literally. But fixers do the work nobody else wants to do. The uncomfortable conversations. The delicate negotiations. The moments where you have to tell powerful people things they don't want to hear." Addie's smile was knowing. "Sound familiar?"

It did. It sounded like everything Bella had been doing her entire life—fixing her sorority's crises, smoothing over conflicts at the finance firm, solving problems without anyone noticing her hand in it.

The difference was that now she'd be noticed. Now she'd have authority. Now people would know her name.

---

Her first case as a fixer came through Kendra's team.

A client—a hedge fund manager with connections to three different BSS partners—was being blackmailed. Someone had photos from a weekend in Monaco that his wife didn't know about. The blackmailer wanted money. The client wanted the problem to disappear.

"We don't make problems disappear," Kendra said during the briefing. "We manage them. Figure out who has leverage and redirect it."

Bella spent three days on the case.

She traced the photos to their source—a woman the client had met at a casino, who'd been paid by a competitor to create kompromat. She identified the competitor's motivation (a deal the client had beaten him to, six months prior). She documented everything, built a counter-leverage position, and presented options.

"We can expose the competitor's hand," Bella told the client via video call. "Make it clear that any publication of those photos will result in revelations about his own... extracurricular activities."

"You found something on him?"

"I found enough." Bella kept her voice neutral. "The question is whether you want to use it actively or passively. Active means we leak something small, demonstrate capability, establish deterrence. Passive means we let him know we could leak, then wait."

The client chose passive. The blackmail stopped. The case closed.

---

"You handled that well," Kendra said afterward.

"I just followed the playbook."

"The playbook doesn't teach judgment. It doesn't teach how to read a client's risk tolerance in real time. It doesn't teach when to push and when to hold." Kendra's expression was approving. "You have instincts. That's not something we can train."

"Hawk says the same thing."

"Hawk's usually right." A pause. "How do you feel? First real case, first successful resolution."

Bella thought about it. The hedge fund manager was, objectively, not a good person. He'd cheated on his wife, made poor decisions, and now was using BSS to clean up his mess. In a just world, maybe he'd face consequences.

But the world wasn't just. And BSS wasn't in the justice business.

"I feel effective," Bella said finally. "I don't know if I feel good about it."

Kendra nodded slowly. "That's the right answer. The day you feel good about all of it is the day you've lost something important."

---

The cases kept coming.

A corporate client whose VP was leaking information to the press. A family office whose patriarch was being manipulated by a younger girlfriend. A non-profit whose board member was embezzling and couldn't be exposed without damaging the organization's mission.

Each case was different. Each solution required navigation—legal, social, sometimes ethical.

Bella learned to compartmentalize. To separate the work from the judgment. To solve problems without becoming invested in the people who'd created them.

"You're getting good at this," Addie observed, reviewing Bella's case logs. "Almost too good."

"What do you mean?"

"You're efficient. Clean. But sometimes I wonder if you're too distant." Addie's gaze was penetrating. "Fixers need empathy. Not for the clients—they can take care of themselves. But for the people caught in the crossfire. The wives who don't know. The employees who'll lose jobs. The collateral damage."

"I see them."

"Do you? Or do you see patterns?"

The question haunted Bella for weeks.

---

The answer came during a case involving a senator's chief of staff.

The chief of staff was gay—not publicly, not even privately to his employer. Someone had figured it out and was threatening exposure unless he provided inside information on legislation.

"Simple solution," Bella said during the initial briefing. "Counter-leverage. Find something on the blackmailer and neutralize."

"What about the chief of staff?" Addie asked.

"What about him?"

"He's the victim here. His career is on the line. His identity is being weaponized against him. How does he feel?"

Bella paused. She'd been so focused on the pattern—blackmail, counter-leverage, resolution—that she hadn't thought about the person at the center.

"I should talk to him," she said slowly. "Not just interview him for information. Actually talk to him."

"Yes," Addie said. "You should."

---

The conversation changed everything.

The chief of staff's name was Marcus. He was forty-two, had worked in politics for twenty years, and had never told anyone outside his family about his sexuality.

"I thought I could keep it separate," he told Bella during their meeting. "Career and personal life. Two boxes that never touched. I was wrong."

"What do you want? Not what protects your career—what do *you* want?"

Marcus was quiet for a long moment. "I want to stop being afraid. I want to tell my boss on my own terms, not because someone else forced my hand. I want to control my own story."

Bella nodded. "Then let's give you that control."

They still neutralized the blackmailer. They still protected Marcus's career. But they also created a plan for Marcus to come out—on his timeline, in his way, with support structures in place.

When the case closed, Marcus sent her a handwritten note: *Thank you for seeing me, not just my problem.*

Bella kept the note in her desk drawer. A reminder that patterns were people.
