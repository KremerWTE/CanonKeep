# Chapter 11 – Afterburn

*Source: book1_all_sources.txt lines 3131-3216 (expanded)*

---

The first Monday in his new role didn't feel like any Monday he'd had before.

No predawn flight to catch. No overnight bag waiting at the foot of the bed like a loyal dog anticipating its master's next journey. No inbox already stacked with messages marked "urgent" in the subject line, each one a small fire demanding to be stamped out before breakfast.

Instead, Jasper woke to the sound of Elena's breathing beside him—soft, steady, the rhythm of someone healing. He lay there for a moment, watching the early light filter through the curtains, catching the dust motes that drifted lazily through the air. It was strange, this stillness. Like a muscle he'd forgotten he had, slowly remembering how to relax.

He got up quietly, showered without checking his phone, and made coffee in the kitchen while the house still slept. The ritual felt different without the countdown clock running in his head—no departure time, no flight to catch, no driver waiting in the driveway with the engine running.

Grace appeared in the kitchen doorway, still in her pajamas, her hair a mess of sleep-tangled curls.

"You're still here," she said, like it was a scientific observation that required verification.

"Still here," he confirmed.

She nodded, apparently satisfied, and climbed onto her stool for breakfast. He made her scrambled eggs—Elena's recipe, though he never got the texture quite right—and they ate together in comfortable silence.

When the school bus rumbled up to the curb, Jasper walked her to the door.

"Have a good day," he said.

"You too, Dad." She paused on the steps. "Are you going to be here when I get home?"

"I'll be here."

She smiled—a quick, genuine thing—and then she was gone, backpack bouncing as she jogged toward the bus.

---

After dropping his name at the Capitol district security checkpoint, Jasper drove through streets he'd traveled a hundred times before—but always on the way to somewhere else. Today, Washington didn't feel like a layover. It felt like a destination.

The Senator's office was efficient in that understated way Washington sometimes managed when no cameras were around. Wood-paneled hallways, muted conversation, the quiet hum of people doing important work without needing to announce it. A young staffer—the same David Chen who'd called with the offer—met him in the lobby and walked him through the security protocols, the building layout, the cafeteria that served surprisingly decent coffee.

"Senator Kirkland sends her regards," Chen said, handing over a security badge. "She'll be back from her overseas trip next week. In the meantime, she wanted you to get oriented with the team."

The team waited in a glass-walled conference room on the third floor. Three people rose when Jasper entered—two men and one woman, all wearing the particular expression of professionals who'd seen enough crises to be skeptical of new leadership but were too experienced to let it show.

Jasper shook each hand in turn.

Marcus Webb, the former FEMA deputy—a heavyset man with graying temples and the calm demeanor of someone who'd coordinated disaster response for thirty years. His grip was firm, his eyes assessing.

Dr. Sarah Okonkwo, the energy-sector analyst—originally from Nigeria, educated at MIT, with a specialization in grid infrastructure vulnerabilities. She wore frameless glasses and spoke with precise economy.

And Colin Frasier, the cyber-response lead—younger than the others, with the restless energy of someone who'd spent too many years in NSA before getting fed up with bureaucracy. His handshake was brief, his attention already drifting to the tablet in his other hand.

Four people, one mission—to stop the next disaster before it had a chance to make the news.

"Welcome to the task force," Marcus said, taking his seat. "Ready to talk about what's keeping us up at night?"

---

It wasn't glamorous.

There were no flashbulbs, no midnight flights to far-off boardrooms. No CEOs waiting nervously for his arrival, no reporters tracking his every move. The conference room had a view of a parking garage, and the coffee came from a machine that had seen better decades.

But when the first situation report hit the table—a series of suspicious outages at a West Coast logistics hub—Jasper felt something spark in his chest.

Not the old adrenaline. Not the familiar rush of being the man everyone turned to when things went wrong.

Something steadier. Purpose without the drag of ego.

He listened as Sarah walked them through the data: three facilities, all owned by the same company, all experiencing unexplained power fluctuations over the past two weeks. Could be equipment failure. Could be coincidence. But the pattern suggested something more deliberate.

"The FBI's not interested yet," Colin said, pulling up satellite imagery on the wall screen. "No crime, no jurisdiction. But if we wait until there's a crime, we're playing catch-up."

"What do you need?" Jasper asked.

"Access. Private sector cooperation. Someone who can pick up a phone and get people talking without going through six months of interagency protocols."

Marcus leaned back in his chair, watching Jasper. "That's why you're here. The Senator thinks you can bridge the gap—government caution, private sector speed."

Jasper looked at the map on the screen—three red pins marking potential vulnerabilities, a web of supply chains connecting them to half the country. He thought about all the crisis rooms he'd sat in, all the fires he'd walked into when things were already burning.

"Let's make some calls," he said.

---

By three o'clock, he was home.

The house was quiet—Elena resting upstairs, the afternoon sun stretching long shadows across the living room floor. He loosened his tie and hung his jacket on the hook by the door, still getting used to the novelty of a workday that ended before dinner.

Grace was at the kitchen table, surrounded by LEGO pieces spread across a newspaper. Her tongue poked out in concentration as she fitted bricks together, building something taller than her usual projects.

"What's that?" Jasper asked, pulling out a chair beside her.

"A lighthouse," she said without looking up.

"A lighthouse." He watched her work—the methodical sorting of pieces, the careful precision of her small fingers. "What's the light for?"

"So people don't crash into the rocks," she said simply.

The answer landed somewhere deep in his chest—a truth spoken without knowing it was true.

---

*Flashback — Six Months Ago*

In his old office on K Street, he'd kept a framed photograph on the wall behind his desk. A wildfire at night—bright orange flames against the black sky, captured from a helicopter during a crisis he'd helped navigate years ago.

The photo showed the fire at its peak, consuming everything in its path. Beautiful and terrible. A reminder of the stakes, of the cost of failure.

Clients had commented on it sometimes. "Quite the image," they'd say, and Jasper would explain that it represented what they were all fighting—the chaos that could swallow companies, careers, entire industries if left unchecked.

He'd told himself the photo was a reminder of why his work mattered.

Now, watching Grace build her lighthouse—brick by careful brick—he wondered if he'd been wrong all along.

Maybe his whole career had been about running into the fire. Charging toward the smoke, thriving on the heat, measuring his worth by how close to the flames he could stand.

But what people needed wasn't just someone to fight the fire after it started.

They needed someone to keep them from getting there in the first place.

Grace held up the lighthouse, examining it from different angles. "It's not done yet," she said. "I need to add the light part."

"Where does the light come from?"

She shrugged, already sorting through pieces for something that would work. "I'll figure it out."

---

After dinner—pasta with store-bought sauce, the best Jasper could manage—Elena felt well enough to sit outside. They settled into the wooden chairs on the porch, the evening air warm and still, carrying the smell of cut grass from somewhere down the street.

Fireflies traced erratic patterns in the gathering dark, their lights blinking like morse code in a language only they understood. A neighbor's dog barked twice and fell silent. Somewhere far away, a plane crossed the sky, its lights winking as it descended toward the airport.

"Do you miss it?" Elena asked.

Jasper didn't answer right away. He watched the fireflies, counted their pulses, let the question settle.

"Parts of it," he admitted finally. "The intensity. The feeling of being right in the middle of something that mattered." He paused. "But not enough to trade this."

"This?"

"Being home. Being here. Being the guy who's present instead of the guy who shows up afterward with a plan to fix whatever broke while he was gone."

Elena rested her head on his shoulder. Her hair smelled like the lavender shampoo she'd used for as long as he could remember—a scent that meant home, that meant her, that meant everything he'd almost lost.

"Then you made the right call," she said.

He didn't say it out loud, but he knew she was right.

The fires would always be there, somewhere in the distance. Crises waiting to be born. Disasters brewing in boardrooms and server farms and supply chains that stretched around the world. There would always be someone who needed the kind of help he'd spent twenty years learning to provide.

But he couldn't be everywhere. Couldn't fight every fire. Couldn't save every company, fix every system, stop every catastrophe before it started.

What he could do was be here. Present. Available. Five feet from the people who mattered most.

---

Later, lying in bed with the windows open to the summer night, Jasper listened to the sounds of the house settling around him. The creak of old joists, the hum of the air conditioner, Elena's breathing slowly deepening into sleep.

His phone sat on the nightstand, silent. No urgent messages. No midnight calls demanding his presence on the other side of the world.

Tomorrow he'd go back to the task force. There would be more situation reports, more red pins on maps, more problems that needed solving before they became disasters. He'd make calls, build bridges, work the angles that came naturally to him after two decades in the business.

But tonight, he was home.

The five feet around him were enough.
