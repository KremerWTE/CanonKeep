# Chapter 26 – Mason's Warning

*Source: book1_all_sources.txt lines 14150-14219 (expanded)*

---

The bar was dim, lit by amber sconces that cast warm shadows across the worn leather booths and polished wood tables. The kind of place that had been serving the same whiskey to the same kind of men for fifty years—lawyers, fixers, people who dealt in problems that never made the papers.

Mason Reilly leaned back in his seat, charcoal sport coat over a navy sweater, his bourbon glass turning slow circles on the table. He had that particular ease of a man who'd learned long ago that looking relaxed was itself a form of power. His hair was slightly tousled, his jaw carrying a day's worth of stubble, his eyes sharp despite the late hour.

Jasper sat across from him in a white oxford shirt with sleeves rolled to the elbows, his dive watch visible under the cuff. He looked like he'd been running on caffeine and adrenaline for too long—which, Mason knew, was probably the truth.

"JB, you look like hell," Mason said, raising his glass in mock salute. "Let me guess—another 'urgent' client fire?"

"Something like that." Jasper flagged the bartender for another round. "Nothing I can't handle."

Mason snorted, the sound carrying more affection than contempt. "You can handle it. That's never been the question. The problem is you think handling it is the same as winning."

---

They'd known each other since Duke, back when Jasper was a walk-on trying to earn his spot on the lacrosse team and Mason was already the kind of guy who knew everyone and everything. They'd bonded over late-night study sessions and early-morning practices, over cheap beer and cheaper pizza, over the particular hunger of young men who'd come from nothing and intended to leave their marks on the world.

Mason had gone to UVA Law after graduation, then pivoted into the murky world of political fixing—the kind of work that happened in back rooms and off the record, where success was measured not by headlines but by problems that quietly disappeared. He was good at it. Maybe too good. But he'd also learned when to walk away, when to draw lines, when to remember that the game wasn't worth playing if you forgot what you were playing for.

Jasper had taken a different path—corporate crisis management, building BSS into something formidable—but they'd stayed close. The kind of close that survived distance and divergent lives, that let you pick up a conversation exactly where you'd left it even if months had passed.

"That's rich," Jasper replied, "coming from a guy who cleans up political scandals for breakfast."

"Yeah, but here's the difference." Mason set his glass down with deliberate care. "I choose my messes. I decide which fires are worth walking into and which ones I let burn. You? You let them choose you. Every time some CEO panics or some board starts sweating, you drop everything else and run toward the flames like you've got something to prove."

Jasper took a slow sip, already feeling the jab land deeper than he wanted to admit.

---

"You've been living in a five-foot world for so long," Mason continued, his voice lower now, more serious, "you don't even see the wreckage past it."

The phrase landed with the particular weight of something named for the first time. Five-foot world. The circle of immediate focus that Jasper had built his career on—see what's right in front of you, solve what's within arm's reach, don't get distracted by the horizon.

It was a survival strategy. It was also, Mason was suggesting, a kind of blindness.

"Elena's not in that five feet," Mason said. "Grace isn't in that five feet. Hell, I'm not sure you are anymore."

"I'm providing for them—" Jasper started.

"Stop." Mason's hand came down flat on the table, not loud but firm enough to cut off the excuse before it could fully form. "That's the oldest lie guys like us tell ourselves. 'I'm providing.' 'I'm building something.' 'They'll understand when they see the house and the cars and the college funds.'"

He leaned forward, elbows on the table, eyes holding Jasper's with an intensity that brooked no deflection.

"You're not just providing, JB. You're hiding. Because it's easier to wrestle a corporate disaster than sit across from your wife when she's hurting. Easier to negotiate a billion-dollar fix than face your kid's disappointment when you miss her game. Easier to be indispensable to strangers than to show up for the people who actually love you."

---

Jasper didn't answer. He couldn't. Mason could read him better than anyone—better than Harper, better than Addison, maybe even better than Elena—and that made lying pointless.

The silence stretched between them, filled by the ambient noise of the bar: ice clinking in glasses, the low murmur of other conversations, the occasional scrape of a chair against the hardwood floor.

"I remember the guy in college," Mason said finally, his tone softening. "The one who wouldn't shut up about this girl he met in the campus café. Dark hair, eyes that stopped him cold. You skipped an internship meeting to take her to that little Italian joint off-campus. Remember?"

Jasper remembered. Of course he remembered.

---

*Flashback — Elena in Her Early 20s*

The memory surfaced with the vividness of something preserved in amber.

A small Italian restaurant with red-checked tablecloths and candles in empty wine bottles. Elena across from him, laughing at something he'd said—he couldn't remember what, only the sound of her laugh and the way it made him feel like the luckiest man in the world.

She was wearing a simple sundress, her hair loose around her shoulders, her fingers brushing his as she reached for the bread basket. He watched her more than he ate, cataloging every detail: the way she tilted her head when she was curious about something, the slight furrow of her brow when she was thinking, the warmth in her eyes when she looked at him.

They'd stayed until the restaurant closed, talking about everything and nothing, making plans they were too young to keep and promises they didn't know they'd break. When they finally walked out into the cool night air, she'd taken his hand like it was the most natural thing in the world.

He'd thought: *This is it. This is what matters.*

And then life had happened, and the "what matters" had gotten buried under deadlines and deals and the relentless pursuit of success.

---

"And I remember the look on your face in that hospital room when they handed you Grace," Mason continued, pulling Jasper back to the present. "You didn't care about deadlines or deals then. You cared about holding your family."

---

*Flashback — Grace's Birth*

Another memory, more recent but just as vivid.

The hospital room, harsh lights softened by the miracle happening in front of him. Elena exhausted but smiling, her hair plastered to her forehead, her hand still gripping his from the final push. The nurses moving with practiced efficiency, and then—

Grace.

Tiny, red-faced, screaming her arrival into the world with a fury that made him laugh despite the tears streaming down his face. They'd placed her in his arms, and he'd held her like she was made of glass and starlight, afraid to breathe too hard in case he somehow broke the spell.

His tie was loosened, his suit jacket abandoned on a chair, the phone in his pocket silent because he'd turned it off for the first time in years. The world outside—the clients, the crises, the endless demands—didn't exist. There was only this room, this woman, this child.

The weight of his daughter in his arms had felt like the most important thing he'd ever held. More important than contracts or deals or the reputation he'd spent years building. For those few hours, he'd understood something essential about what mattered.

And then the hours had passed, and the world had crept back in, and somehow that understanding had gotten lost in the noise.

---

Mason leaned back, letting the silence hit.

"JB..." His voice was quiet now, stripped of the edge it had carried earlier. "You keep chasing the fire, you're gonna come home one day and realize the house is already ash."

Jasper stared at his bourbon, watching the amber liquid catch the candlelight.

"I don't know how to stop," he admitted, the words coming out rougher than he intended.

"Yeah, you do." Mason finished his drink and stood, dropping a bill on the table. "You just don't want to. Because stopping means looking at what you've missed. And that's harder than any crisis you've ever managed."

He clapped Jasper on the shoulder, the touch both gentle and firm.

"Go home, JB. Not tomorrow. Not after the next call. Now. And actually be there when you walk through the door."

He walked out, leaving Jasper alone with his drink and his thoughts and the weight of everything he'd been avoiding.
