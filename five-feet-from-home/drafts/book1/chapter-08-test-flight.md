# Chapter 8 — Test Flight

*Source: Five Feet From Home/Chapter 8.docx*

---

Elena was discharged on a gray Friday morning, the kind that looked like it had been left soaking in coffee.

The neurologist's orders were clear: short walks, light meals, follow-up in a week.

Jasper carried the discharge folder like it was classified.

Grace insisted on sitting in the back seat with her mother for the ride home, coloring book in her lap. Jasper drove slow, one hand on the wheel, the other resting on the console like it could catch whatever might fall.

---

At home, the silence was different from the hospital's — not sterile, but fragile.

He settled Elena on the couch with a blanket, made coffee she didn't drink, then hovered until she made him stop.

"Go do something," she said.

"Like what?"

"Like test what life looks like if you're not on call for the world."

He almost laughed. Almost. "I don't know what that looks like."

"Then find out."

---

He took the advice literally.

He drove to a coffee shop he'd passed a thousand times but never entered because he was always on the way to somewhere else.

Ordered a black coffee, sat by the window with no laptop, no calls.

It was harder than it should have been. His hands kept wanting to reach for the phone. His mind kept drafting replies to emails that hadn't come in yet.

A group of college kids sat at the next table, talking over each other about some start-up idea, their enthusiasm unscarred by quarterly reports or shareholder votes. He remembered what that felt like — the rush of building something because it mattered, not because it paid.

He stayed until the coffee was cold, then forced himself to walk to the park instead of back to the car.

---

### Flashback — Six Months Ago

In a boardroom in New York, he'd sat through a twelve-hour session negotiating a merger that would keep two thousand people employed — and net his firm a mid-eight-figure retainer.

When the papers were signed, the CEO shook his hand like he'd just stopped a war.

That night, in the hotel, Elena had texted him a photo of Grace's first soccer goal. He'd been too tired to reply until morning.

By then, the moment was over.

---

The park was half-empty, damp from last night's rain. He watched a father push his daughter on a swing, the girl shrieking in delight as she kicked toward the clouds.

The simplicity of it hit him harder than the Senator's offer.

He realized he could name every CEO he'd worked for in the last decade, but not the name of Grace's best friend in school.

---

When he got home, Elena was asleep. Grace was at the kitchen table, building something complicated out of mismatched LEGO pieces.

"Is that a house?" he asked.

"Kind of," she said without looking up. "It's a house you can fix without knocking it down first."

He smiled at the choice of words. "Smart design."

"Mom says you used to fix things like that," she added, fitting two stubborn pieces together.

The comment landed like a stone in his chest.

---

That evening, he opened the Senator's packet.

The role was exactly what the aide had described: senior oversight, coordination between government and corporate leaders in crisis. A seat at the table, but not the table.

He'd be a strategist, not a field operator.

It would keep him in D.C., close enough to be home for dinner more nights than not.

The pay was a fraction of what he made now. But the real cost was something else — the erosion of the identity he'd built brick by brick, deal by deal, until it felt like armor.

---

Later, in bed, Elena traced a finger along his forearm.

"You were quiet today," she said.

"Just… test flying a different sky," he said.

"And?"

He stared at the ceiling. "Feels like I'd have to learn how to land all over again."

She turned to face him, her expression unreadable in the dark. "Maybe that's not the worst thing."

He didn't answer. But he didn't disagree.
