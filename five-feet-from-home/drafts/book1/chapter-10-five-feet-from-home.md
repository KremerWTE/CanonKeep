# Chapter 10 – Five Feet From Home

*Source: book1_all_sources.txt lines 2971-3107 (expanded)*

---

The Senator's aide called first thing Thursday morning.

Jasper was standing in the kitchen, pouring coffee into his travel mug out of habit, even though he had nowhere to travel to. The morning light slanted through the window above the sink, catching the dust motes that drifted lazily through the air. Grace sat at the counter, her cereal going soggy beside an open sketchbook, crayons scattered like fallen soldiers across the granite.

His phone buzzed against the counter. D.C. area code.

"Mr. Barrett, this is David Chen from Senator Kirkland's office. The Senator's traveling, but she wanted me to check in personally. Have you reached a decision?"

Jasper watched Grace as she drew. Her tongue poked out slightly in concentration—Elena's habit, inherited without either of them noticing. The house on the page was crooked, with a big door and no fence. Three stick figures stood in front of it, holding hands. One of them had a briefcase that was almost as big as his body.

Upstairs, he could hear Elena moving around, getting ready for her first morning out since the hospital. The floorboards creaked in the familiar pattern of her footsteps—bathroom, closet, back to bathroom. She was taking her time, which meant she was nervous but wouldn't admit it.

"Mr. Barrett?" the aide prompted.

"Yes," Jasper said, still watching his daughter draw. "I've reached a decision."

"And?"

He set down the coffee pot. Grace added a sun to her drawing—a bright yellow circle with rays extending in every direction, touching everything on the page.

"I'll take it."

---

He expected a surge of adrenaline—the usual rush that came with a big play. The quickening of his pulse, the sharpening of his focus, the mental checklist already forming: who to call first, what meetings to schedule, which relationships to leverage.

Instead, it was quieter. Like stepping off a moving train and finding the platform surprisingly still beneath his feet.

Not the thrill of stepping into a new fire, but the steadiness of stepping out of one.

The aide's voice took on the practiced warmth of someone delivering good news. "That's wonderful to hear, Mr. Barrett. The Senator will be pleased. We'll send over the formal offer letter today, and there's a briefing packet that'll need your review before the confirmation process begins. Do you have any questions at this stage?"

Jasper thought about the Hong Kong crisis he'd handed off to Riley, the client who'd threatened to find someone else if he didn't show up in person, the twenty years of building a reputation as the man who always walked into the fire first.

"No questions," he said. "I'm ready."

The aide gave the standard welcome, laid out the next steps—HR paperwork, background check updates, a preliminary call with the task force leadership—and hung up.

Grace didn't even look up from her sketchbook. "Was that work?"

Jasper set the phone down on the counter, the screen still warm against his palm. He looked at his daughter—her messy ponytail, the smear of purple crayon on her wrist, the way she hummed softly when she was concentrating.

"It was life," he said.

She glanced up then, her expression curious. "That's a weird thing to say."

"Yeah," he agreed. "I guess it is."

---

Elena appeared in the kitchen doorway, dressed in jeans and a soft gray sweater—the kind of simple outfit she used to wear on weekends before weekends became just the days Jasper wasn't traveling. Her hair was down, still slightly damp from the shower, and she'd put on a little makeup. Not much. Just enough to remind herself she was still there underneath everything.

"You look nice," Jasper said.

"I look like someone who's been in a hospital for a week," she replied, but there was a small smile tugging at the corner of her mouth. "Grace, finish your breakfast. We're walking to get coffee."

"We are?" Grace asked, already abandoning the soggy cereal.

"Your dad and I are. You're going to school." Elena caught Jasper's eye. "Unless you have somewhere else to be?"

He shook his head. "Nowhere else to be."

Something shifted in her expression—not quite belief, but the willingness to try believing. "Then help me find my walking shoes."

---

Later that morning, after Grace's bus had rumbled away down the street and Elena had spent ten minutes convincing herself she was strong enough to walk six blocks, they set out.

It was slow going. Each block measured against her energy, each step a small negotiation between what she wanted and what her body would allow. Jasper walked beside her, close enough to catch her if she stumbled, far enough to give her the dignity of doing this herself.

"You're hovering," she said, after they'd crossed the first intersection.

"I'm accompanying."

"You're hovering adjacently."

He smiled despite himself. "I'll work on it."

They passed the dry cleaner where his suits had accumulated for months at a time, the owner eventually calling to ask if he'd moved. They passed the bakery where Elena used to take Grace for Saturday morning croissants, back when Saturday mornings weren't just recovery time before Sunday departures.

She was breathing harder by the third block, but she refused to slow down. The coffee shop appeared at the end of the street like a finish line, its green awning bright in the morning sun.

"You're quieter than usual," Elena said, as they waited at the crosswalk.

"Trying it on," he replied.

"Trying what on?"

The light changed. They stepped off the curb together.

"This version of me. The one that's home before the story's over."

She smiled faintly, the expression somewhere between hope and wariness. "And? Does it fit?"

"It's going to need tailoring."

---

The coffee shop was half-empty, the morning rush already faded into the lull before lunch. They ordered at the counter—black coffee for him, a chai latte for her—and found a table by the window. The same table, Jasper realized, where he'd sat alone a few days ago, trying to remember what stillness felt like.

Outside, the world moved in its usual rush. Cars speeding past, horns punctuating the morning. A woman in a suit walked by with a phone pressed to her ear, gesturing sharply at nothing. A man in running clothes jogged past, checking his watch mid-stride.

For the first time in years, Jasper didn't feel like he was late for something.

He watched Elena cradle her cup in both hands, the steam rising to warm her face. There were shadows under her eyes that hadn't faded, a fragility to her movements that would take time to heal. But she was here. They both were.

"I took the job," he said.

She set her cup down, carefully, like she was afraid of what her hands might do if she let them move too fast. "The Senator's task force?"

"Yes."

"Stateside?"

"Stateside. D.C. based. Strategic role, not operational." He met her eyes. "I'll be home for dinner most nights. Present for the weekends. Available when things break."

She was quiet for a long moment. Outside, a bus rumbled past, its brakes squealing as it pulled to the curb.

"What about your clients?" she asked. "The ones who think they can't survive without you in the room?"

"They'll learn that they can. Or they'll find someone else." He shrugged, and the gesture felt unfamiliar—the lightness of letting something go. "Riley's ready. Addison's ready. The team I built can handle what I used to do. Maybe better."

Elena traced a pattern on the table with her fingertip. "And you're okay with that? Watching someone else put out your fires?"

---

*Flashback — First Year of Marriage*

The memory surfaced like a photograph shaken loose from an old album.

Their first apartment had been a cramped one-bedroom on the third floor of a building with temperamental heating and a super who never returned calls. The rent just barely covered each month, and some months it didn't cover at all.

Jasper had taken a small crisis job for a startup no one had heard of—a meal-kit company that had accidentally shipped boxes with salmonella-tainted chicken to three thousand customers across the Midwest. It paid almost nothing, but the hours were reasonable, and when the crisis was contained, there was no next crisis waiting in the wings.

He'd come home at six every night. They'd eat dinner at the folding table by the window, watching the sun set over the parking lot next door. Afterward, they'd move to the couch—a secondhand thing with sagging cushions and a mysterious stain on one arm—and talk.

About everything. About nothing. About the lives they wanted to build and the people they hoped to become.

"What if this is as good as it gets?" Elena had asked one night, her feet in his lap, her wine glass almost empty.

"Then it's pretty good," he'd said.

She'd laughed and thrown a pillow at him, and they'd ended up tangled together on that terrible couch, and he'd thought: *This is it. This is what matters.*

Back then, being home had felt natural. Like breathing. Like the default state of his existence.

Somewhere along the way, he'd let it feel like a concession. A fallback position. The thing you settled for when the real work was done.

He'd been wrong.

---

"I'm okay with it," Jasper said, answering Elena's question across the gulf of twenty years. "I think I've been waiting to be okay with it for a long time."

When they finished their coffee, Elena slipped her hand into his. Her fingers were cool from the ceramic cup, her grip lighter than he remembered—but it was there. She was there.

"You'll still miss it sometimes," she said.

"I know."

"And you'll still have bad days where you wonder if you did the right thing."

"Probably."

She squeezed his hand, and he felt something unknot in his chest—a tension he'd been carrying so long he'd forgotten it was there.

"But I'd rather figure that out together," she said, "than wait for you to come home from somewhere halfway across the world."

---

That night, after dinner had been eaten and homework supervised and Grace put to bed with her favorite story read twice, Jasper stepped out onto the porch.

The air was cool, carrying the first hint of autumn—that clean, sharp scent that always made him think of new beginnings, even though the year was winding down. The street was quiet except for the occasional hum of a passing car, headlights sweeping briefly across the lawn before moving on.

He sat in the old wooden chair that Elena had found at a yard sale years ago, the one he'd always meant to refinish but never had. The paint was peeling, the seat worn smooth by seasons of use he'd mostly missed.

He thought about all the rooms he'd been in. Corner offices in glass towers. War rooms lit by the blue glow of a dozen screens. Hotel suites that cost more per night than his first apartment cost per month. Conference tables where he'd sat at the head, every eye turned toward him, waiting for the next move.

He thought about all the people he'd advised. CEOs terrified of losing everything. Boards desperate to protect their legacies. Politicians whose careers hung by threads he'd learned to weave and cut with equal skill.

He thought about all the fires he'd walked into. The crises that consumed weeks, months, years of his life. The adrenaline that had felt like purpose. The victories that had felt like proof he was doing something that mattered.

And he realized that every one of them had eventually burned out. The fires, the crises, the all-consuming emergencies—they had endings. The clients moved on. The headlines faded. The people he'd saved went back to their lives and forgot his name.

---

Behind him, through the window, he could see the living room. Elena's shadow moved across the wall as she straightened the cushions on the couch. The lamp cast a warm glow over the room—over the photos on the mantle, the books on the shelf, the life they'd built in the spaces between his absences.

He heard her humming. Something soft, something old. A song from their wedding, maybe, or from one of those nights in the first apartment when the future felt infinite and close enough to touch.

That didn't have to burn out. Not if he didn't let it.

Jasper stood up from the old chair. The wood creaked beneath him, protesting and welcoming in equal measure.

He turned, stepped back inside, and closed the door.

The five feet around him were enough.
