# Chapter 18: Addison's Trial by Fire

*POV: Addison Price*

---

The Forge hummed with controlled chaos at eight a.m., but Addison Price thrived in chaos.

She stood at the head of the conference table, three screens glowing behind her with live feeds from New York, Los Angeles, and Charlotte. The NFL ownership dispute had reached critical mass overnight—leaked emails, anonymous sources, and enough billionaire ego to sink a yacht. The league wanted this contained before Sunday's primetime game, which gave them exactly seventy-two hours to turn a public relations nightmare into a footnote.

Addison had been running point on media coordination for eighteen hours straight, and she felt it like electricity in her veins.

"Harper, status on the commissioner's statement?" she asked, her voice cutting through the low murmur of strategists and analysts working their phones.

Harper looked up from her laptop, dark circles visible under her eyes but her focus still laser-sharp. "Legal approved the language. We're waiting on the owners to sign off, which is like asking cats to form a committee."

"Then we move without them." Addison turned to the screen showing Rafe Moreno in the LA office, his charming smile somehow intact despite the three a.m. hour. "Rafe, what do we have on the minority owner's financials?"

"Clean as a whistle publicly," Rafe said, "but there's a debt structure that suggests he's leveraged to the hilt. If we leak the right questions to the right reporters—"

"We don't leak," Addison interrupted. "We shape. Find me something that makes both owners look foolish for fighting. Something that makes the fans the heroes of this story."

Rafe's grin widened. "I like the way you think, boss."

She wasn't the boss—not officially. Jasper still held that title, and Harper was COO. But in moments like this, when the crisis was hot and the timeline was tight, Addison became the fulcrum on which the entire operation balanced.

It was the role she'd been born to play.

---

By noon, they'd drafted three versions of the commissioner's statement, planted strategic questions with three different sports journalists, and coordinated a whisper campaign among team owners that painted the dispute as a distraction from the game itself.

Addison stood at the whiteboard, mapping the next forty-eight hours with quick strokes of a dry-erase marker. Media hits. Stakeholder calls. Damage control contingencies. Her handwriting was sharp and precise, the kind of penmanship that suggested she'd never met a problem she couldn't solve.

"Addie."

She turned to find Claire Ashford standing in the doorway, holding two bottles of water and wearing the kind of expression that suggested she was about to deliver news Addison didn't want to hear.

"I don't have time for a wellness check," Addison said, but she took the water bottle anyway.

"Good, because I'm not here to check on your wellness." Claire stepped into the conference room and closed the door behind her. "I'm here to tell you that Harper dropped a ball."

Addison froze, the water bottle halfway to her lips. "What ball?"

"The talking points for the owners' call. She sent the wrong version—the one with the editorial comments still in the margins. One of the owners saw it and is now convinced we're mocking him."

For a split second, Addison felt the floor tilt beneath her. Harper never made mistakes like that. Harper was meticulous, precise, the kind of operator who triple-checked every semicolon.

Then she remembered something Claire had said during their first session—the one Addison had attended reluctantly, convinced that performance psychology was just therapy in a nicer suit.

*Elite performers don't fail because they lack skill. They fail because they lose the ability to recover.*

Addison set down the water bottle and pulled out her phone. "Which owner?"

"Marcus Hayward. The one who wanted to move the franchise."

"He's also the one who's most scared of looking like a villain." Addison was already typing, her fingers flying across the screen. "If we can spin this as 'internal working documents' and make it about transparency—"

"That's what Harper's trying to do right now," Claire said. "But she's spiraling. I can see it in her posture—shoulders too high, breathing too shallow. She's compensating instead of recovering."

Addison looked up. "Then we help her recover."

She found Harper in her office, staring at her laptop screen with the kind of intensity that suggested she was trying to will the mistake into nonexistence. Her hair had come loose from its usual sleek bun, and there was a coffee stain on her blazer that she hadn't seemed to notice.

"Harper."

Harper didn't look up. "I know. I screwed up. I sent the wrong file, and now Marcus Hayward thinks we're making fun of him in the margins like high schoolers passing notes."

"You made a mistake," Addison said, stepping into the office and closing the door. "You didn't screw up. There's a difference."

"Is there?" Harper's voice was sharp, brittle. "Because from where I'm sitting, I just handed a billionaire ammunition to blow up a crisis we've spent three weeks containing."

Addison pulled up a chair and sat down, forcing Harper to meet her eyes. "Do you remember what Claire said about recovery?"

"That it's more important than performance."

"Not just more important. It's what separates elite performers from burnouts." Addison leaned forward. "You made a mistake because you've been running on fumes for two weeks. That's not a character flaw—that's biology. Now we recover, and we use it."

Harper blinked. "Use it how?"

"We call Marcus Hayward directly. We tell him that the internal notes reflect our team's process—that we stress-test every narrative, including critiques that make our clients uncomfortable. We frame it as thoroughness, not mockery. And then we offer him something he wants more than revenge."

"What does he want?"

Addison smiled—the kind of smile that had earned her the nickname "War Goddess" from Hawk. "He wants to be the reasonable one. The guy who rises above. So we give him a chance to look magnanimous by accepting our 'transparent process' and moving forward."

Harper stared at her for a long moment, then exhaled slowly. "That might actually work."

"It will work," Addison said. "But only if you stop spiraling and start recovering. Which means you take five minutes to breathe, fix your hair, and remember that you're Harper fucking Whoever-You-Are, and you don't let billionaires rattle you."

A ghost of a smile crossed Harper's face. "You know my last name."

"I do. But I like the mystery." Addison stood and headed for the door, then paused. "Call him in ten minutes. I'll be on standby if you need backup."

"Addie?"

She turned.

"Thank you."

Addison nodded once, then left before the gratitude could sit too long between them. She didn't do emotional processing in the middle of a crisis. That was for later—for the car ride home, or the shower, or the rare moments when Hawk would pull her into his arms and let her finally exhale.

---

By six p.m., the crisis had been contained.

Harper had made the call to Marcus Hayward with the kind of poise that reminded everyone why she was COO. The owner had accepted the explanation, even seemed mollified by the idea that BSS took their vetting process so seriously. The commissioner's statement had gone out on schedule, framing the ownership dispute as a "passionate disagreement between stakeholders who both love the game"—the kind of PR language that said everything and nothing.

Addison stood in the parking garage, leaning against her car and scrolling through the media coverage. The narrative was holding. The crisis was cooling. They'd done it.

Her phone buzzed with a text from Kendra:

*Still alive? I saved you leftovers if you actually come home before midnight.*

Addison smiled and typed back:

*On my way. Don't let Hawk eat my share.*

Another buzz, this time from Jasper:

*Good work today. Harper told me what you did. That's what partnership looks like.*

She stared at the text for a long moment. Jasper didn't give praise lightly—every compliment was earned, weighed, meant. To say that her intervention with Harper was "what partnership looks like" wasn't just acknowledgment of her crisis management skills. It was recognition that she'd grown beyond the brilliant strategist who could solve any problem into something harder: a leader who could help others recover when they stumbled.

Claire's words echoed in her mind: *Elite performers don't fail because they lack skill. They fail because they lose the ability to recover.*

Addison had spent years perfecting her skills—strategy, media relations, crisis navigation. But today, she'd done something different. She'd helped someone else recover, and in doing so, she'd proven something to herself.

She wasn't just good in a crisis. She was sustainable.

---

The drive home took forty minutes in traffic, and Addison spent most of it decompressing—shoulders down, breathing deep, letting the adrenaline drain out of her system the way Claire had taught her.

By the time she pulled into the apartment complex where Kendra was staying temporarily while her condo was being renovated, Addison felt almost human again.

Kendra met her at the door wearing bright coral leggings and a loose tank top, her hair still damp from a shower. She looked strong and solid and real in a way that made Addison's chest tighten.

"You look like you fought a war," Kendra said, stepping aside to let Addison in.

"I did. I won." Addison dropped her bag on the couch and kicked off her heels, groaning as her feet hit the floor. "Please tell me those leftovers include something with carbs."

"Pasta, garlic bread, and tiramisu because I know you and I'm an excellent human."

Addison crossed to Kendra and kissed her—slow and deep and grateful. "You're the best human."

"I know." Kendra grinned and pulled back, studying Addison's face with the careful attention she gave her athletes. "You need to eat, shower, and sleep. In that order."

"I need to debrief with you first."

"Then we debrief over pasta."

They settled on the couch with plates of reheated penne and glasses of red wine, and Addison walked Kendra through the day—the NFL crisis, Harper's mistake, the recovery strategy that had turned a disaster into a footnote.

"You sound proud of yourself," Kendra said, twirling pasta around her fork.

"I am." Addison paused, surprised by the honesty. She wasn't usually good at naming her emotions in the moment—that required a level of presence she'd spent years avoiding. But something about today felt different. "I think I finally proved to myself that I'm not just good at managing crises. I'm good at managing people through them."

"You've always been good at that."

"No," Addison said slowly. "I've always been good at solving problems. Today I helped someone else solve theirs. That's different."

Kendra set down her fork and reached for Addison's hand, lacing their fingers together. "You're growing."

"Or I'm finally using what Claire's been teaching me."

"Same thing." Kendra's thumb traced circles on the back of Addison's hand. "You know what this means, right?"

"What?"

"It means you're sustainable. Which means you're not going to burn out and end up in a hospital bed like last year."

Addison flinched. The memory was still sharp—the Miami hotel room, the sudden collapse, the terrifying realization that her body had limits even if her will didn't.

"I'm working on it," she said quietly.

"I know you are. And I'm proud of you." Kendra leaned in and kissed her forehead. "Now finish your pasta, because we have plans."

Addison raised an eyebrow. "Plans?"

"Day trip tomorrow. You, me, and a rental car. I want to check out a potential competition venue in Asheville, and you need a break from crisis management. We'll leave at six, be back by dinner."

"Kendra, I have—"

"Three fires burning at any given time, I know. But Jasper can handle them for one day. And if he can't, that's his problem, not yours."

Addison opened her mouth to argue, then stopped. Kendra was right—she'd been running point on too many crises for too long, and the whole point of hiring Claire was to build sustainability into the team. If she couldn't take a single day off without the entire operation collapsing, then they had bigger problems than billionaire egos.

"Six a.m.?" she asked.

"I'll have coffee ready."

"Then I'm in."

---

Later that night, after Kendra had fallen asleep with her head on Addison's shoulder, Addison found herself staring at the ceiling and thinking about recovery.

She'd spent her entire adult life building armor—the sharp suits, the sharper strategies, the ability to walk into any room and command it. She'd built BSS with Jasper, turned it into one of the most elite crisis management firms in the country, and earned a reputation as someone who could solve impossible problems.

But somewhere along the way, she'd forgotten how to recover. She'd confused resilience with invincibility, sustainability with relentlessness.

Today, she'd helped Harper remember how to recover. And in doing so, she'd reminded herself.

Elite performers don't fail because they lack skill. They fail because they lose the ability to recover.

Addison pulled Kendra closer, breathed in the scent of her shampoo, and let herself rest in the knowledge that she was learning—slowly, imperfectly, but genuinely—how to be not just brilliant, but sustainable.

Tomorrow, she'd take a day off. She'd drive to Asheville with Kendra, check out a CrossFit competition venue, and remember what it felt like to exist outside the five-foot radius of the next crisis.

And when she came back, she'd be stronger for it.

Not because she'd solved another impossible problem, but because she'd learned how to let someone else hold the weight for a day.

That, she was beginning to understand, was what recovery looked like.
