# Chapter 2: The Lunch with Elena

*POV: Elena Barrett*

---

The restaurant was everything Elena loved about Charlotte's emerging food scene: exposed brick walls that spoke of the building's industrial past, Edison bulbs casting warm pools of light, and a farm-to-table menu that changed with the seasons. Harvest & Hearth had opened six months ago in what used to be a textile warehouse, and it had quickly become the place where deals were made over heirloom tomatoes and handmade pasta.

Elena arrived first, sliding into the corner booth with a view of the street. The host—a young woman with sleeve tattoos and a genuinely warm smile—brought water without being asked, and Elena took a moment to breathe.

Three months since the medical crisis. Three months since she'd collapsed at the dinner table and woken up in a hospital room to find Jasper's hand wrapped around hers and Grace's drawing taped to the wall. Three months since everything had changed.

Or started to change. She was still figuring out which.

She'd stepped back into real estate with Marissa Chen's firm, taking on two clients whose budgets started with seven figures and whose expectations were somehow even higher. She'd accepted a board position at the Davidson Foundation, a nonprofit that funded literacy programs across the Southeast. And she'd started saying yes to lunch invitations like this one—started reclaiming pieces of herself that had gotten buried under the weight of being a wife who waited and a mother who held things together.

The Wife Club Zoom from Book 1 had sparked something in her. Watching Harper talk about boundaries, hearing Maddie describe the renegotiations she'd made with Chris, listening to the other women speak truths she'd been afraid to name—it had given Elena permission to want more.

Not more from Jasper. More from herself.

Sara arrived first, sliding into the seat across from Elena with the easy grace of someone who spent three hours a day coaching Olympic lifts and burpees. She was wearing dark jeans and a fitted athletic top in charcoal gray, her hair pulled back in a low ponytail that somehow looked intentional rather than rushed.

"You're glowing," Sara said, eyeing Elena with the assessing look of someone who noticed everything and said most of it out loud.

Elena felt heat rise to her cheeks but managed to keep her voice neutral. "New skincare routine."

"Liar." Sara grinned and reached for the water glass. "You look like someone who's been sleeping well. Which means either Jasper finally learned what foreplay is, or you've been sneaking naps while Lucas is down."

"Sara!"

"What? I'm your sister. I'm allowed to be crass." She took a sip of water, her eyes sparkling with mischief. "Besides, you do look good. Different. Like you're actually here instead of just going through the motions."

Elena opened her mouth to deflect, then stopped. Sara deserved honesty. "I feel different. I don't know how to explain it. It's like I've been holding my breath for years, and I'm finally remembering how to exhale."

Sara's expression softened. "Good. You deserve to breathe, Elena. You've been suffocating for too long."

Before Elena could respond, Harper swept in with a gust of energy that seemed to follow her everywhere. She was wearing a tailored blazer in deep navy over a silk blouse the color of champagne, her hair swept back in a low bun that managed to look both professional and effortless.

"Sorry I'm late," Harper said, dropping her bag onto the booth seat and sliding in next to Sara. "The NFL thing is an absolute nightmare. Two billionaires with the emotional maturity of toddlers fighting over a toy they both claim they don't even want."

"Sounds delightful," Sara said dryly.

"It's a circus. And I'm the one trying to convince the elephants to dance." Harper caught the server's attention and ordered a glass of Sancerre without looking at the menu. "Please tell me we're not talking about work. I need at least forty-five minutes of pretending the world isn't on fire."

"We were just talking about Elena's glow," Sara offered helpfully.

Harper's eyes lit up. "Oh, I noticed. New real estate client?"

"Two, actually. And a board position."

"Look at you." Harper raised her water glass in a mock toast. "From homemaker to empire builder in three months."

"I was never just a homemaker," Elena said, but there was no heat in it. Harper didn't mean it as an insult, and they all knew it.

"No," Harper agreed, "you were the woman who held everything together while everyone else fell apart. But now you're doing it for yourself, and that's different. That's power."

The server returned to take their orders—roasted beet salad for Elena, grilled salmon for Harper, the braised short ribs for Sara. By the time the appetizers arrived—whipped ricotta with honeycomb and charred bread—the conversation had shifted from work to the easier territory of family and relationships.

"So," Sara said, dragging a piece of bread through the ricotta with the focus of someone who took food seriously, "is anyone going to address the elephant in the room?"

Harper froze with her wine glass halfway to her lips. "What elephant?"

"The one named Ethan," Sara said sweetly.

Harper set down her wine glass and exhaled. "His name is Ethan Cole. He's an architect. I met him at a gallery opening two months ago, and before you ask—no, he's not a client, and no, I'm not compromising anything at BSS by dating him."

"I wasn't going to ask that," Elena said gently. "I was going to ask if you're happy."

Harper's face did something complicated—softened and tensed at the same time, like someone learning to trust a new language. "I think so. He's different from anyone I've dated before. He doesn't care that I work eighteen-hour days. He brings me coffee at midnight and doesn't expect me to apologize for being busy. He just... shows up. And then he leaves when I need space. It's like he's fluent in a language I didn't know I was speaking."

"That's beautiful," Elena said, and meant it.

"It's also terrifying," Harper admitted. "Because if he can be that patient, that present, that willing to meet me where I am—what does it say about everyone else who couldn't? What does it say about me that I accepted less for so long?"

Sara pointed her bread at Harper like a professor with a pointer. "It says you're learning. And learning means you were strong enough to admit you didn't know everything."

"When did you get so wise?" Harper asked.

"I coach twenty-year-olds who think they're invincible and fifty-year-olds who are terrified they're not. You learn a lot about people when you're watching them struggle with a barbell."

The appetizers disappeared, and the conversation drifted to lighter topics: Sara's new programming cycle at the gym, Elena's upcoming site visit for the foundation, Harper's ongoing battle to convince the NFL owners that public litigation would destroy them both.

Then Sara leaned forward, her eyes bright with the particular enthusiasm she reserved for talking about her athletes. "You have to meet Kendra. I've mentioned her, right?"

"Only about seventeen times," Elena said, smiling.

"She's a machine. She just placed third in regionals—third, Elena. Out of hundreds of athletes. And she's coaching our evening classes now, which means I actually get to leave before nine o'clock some nights."

"What's she like?" Harper asked, genuinely curious.

Sara pulled out her phone and scrolled through her camera roll. "Here. This is from last week."

She turned the screen toward them. The video showed a woman in her late twenties performing a snatch complex—bar from the floor to overhead in one fluid motion, then dropping into a full squat and standing. Her form was perfect: shoulders stable, hips driving through, the bar moving in a straight line as if pulled by invisible wires. She had dark hair pulled back in a high ponytail, lean muscle visible in her arms and shoulders, and the kind of focus that made everything else disappear.

"She's incredible," Elena murmured, watching the way Kendra moved—economical, precise, powerful.

"She's more than incredible," Sara said. "She's also genuinely kind. She'll spend an hour coaching a beginner on their first pull-up, breaking down every cue, celebrating every millimeter of progress. And then she'll turn around and hit a PR that makes grown men weep."

"I'd like to meet her," Elena said, surprising herself.

"You will. I'm having a pool party next Saturday—nothing fancy, just the gym crew and family. You should come. Bring Grace and Lucas. Addison's coming, and I think Kendra's bringing a few of the other competitors she trains with."

"The elite athlete crew?" Harper asked.

"Exactly. Dylan, Tasha, maybe Eli if he's back from the competition in Ohio. They're all ridiculous humans who can do things with their bodies that defy physics." Sara grinned. "You'll love them. Or be terrified of them. Possibly both."

Elena felt a flicker of something she couldn't quite name—curiosity, maybe, or the edge of anticipation. "I'll be there."

"Good." Sara stole a bite of Harper's salmon, ignoring the mock-glare she received. "It'll be fun. And Grace can play with Emma. They've been asking for a playdate."

The main courses arrived, and the conversation shifted again—this time to the mechanics of Harper's relationship with Ethan, which Sara dissected with the precision of a coach analyzing a lift.

"Does he know you're not looking for marriage?" Sara asked bluntly.

"We've talked about it. He says he's fine with where we are."

"Do you believe him?"

Harper hesitated. "I think he believes it. But I also think people say things they mean in the moment, and then six months later they're surprised to find out they've changed their minds."

"Then you keep talking," Elena said. "You keep checking in. That's what Jasper and I are learning to do—not assume we know what the other person needs, but actually ask."

"How's that going?" Sara asked, her voice gentler now.

Elena took a breath. "Better. Some days are easier than others. He's trying—really trying. He's home for dinner most nights. He takes Grace to the park on Saturdays. He's delegating more at work, which I know is killing him because he's a control freak."

"Aren't you glad you married him?" Harper teased.

"Every day," Elena said, and realized she meant it. Not in the easy, uncomplicated way she'd meant it on their wedding day, but in the harder, richer way that came from choosing someone again after seeing all the ways they could fail you. "But I'm also glad I'm not just waiting for him anymore. I'm glad I have my own things."

"To our own things," Harper said, raising her wine glass.

They clinked glasses—water for Elena, wine for Harper, iced tea for Sara—and for a moment, Elena felt the truth of what the Wife Club had been trying to teach her.

She wasn't just Jasper's wife. Wasn't just Grace and Lucas's mother. She was Elena Barrett, and she was building something that belonged to her.

---

After lunch, they lingered over coffee—decaf for Elena, espresso for Harper, nothing for Sara, who claimed she was already jittery from pre-workout.

"I need to ask you something," Harper said, stirring sugar into her espresso with the focused attention she gave everything. "Maddie's organizing another Wife Club Zoom for next month. Are you in?"

Elena hesitated. The first Zoom had been revelatory, but it had also been intense—listening to women she barely knew talk about the compromises they'd made, the boundaries they'd set, the ways their marriages had survived or almost hadn't. It had felt like standing in front of a mirror that showed not just your reflection but the shadows behind it.

"I'm in," she said finally. "I think I need it."

"We all need it," Sara said. "Even those of us who aren't married to workaholics."

"You're married to the gym," Harper pointed out.

"The gym doesn't forget my birthday."

They laughed, and the moment passed, but Elena filed it away—another piece of the scaffolding she was building around herself, another source of strength that wasn't Jasper.

She loved him. She was choosing him. But she was also choosing herself, and that made all the difference.

---

On the drive home, Elena found her hand drifting to her stomach.

She wasn't showing yet—sixteen weeks, and her body was holding the secret close—but she could feel the changes. The subtle thickening of her waist. The tenderness in her breasts. The way certain smells made her nauseous and others made her ravenous.

She hadn't told Jasper yet. Not officially.

She'd told Sara in a whisper at the gym two weeks ago, needing to say it out loud to someone who would hold it gently. She'd told Harper last week in the car on the way to a foundation event, needing a second opinion on when to break the news.

The wives knew before the husband did. That felt fitting, somehow—a small rebellion, a quiet claim to autonomy.

She would tell Jasper soon. Maybe this weekend, after Grace went to bed. Maybe at Sara's pool party, when the moment felt right.

Or maybe she'd wait a little longer, holding this secret to herself the way she'd learned to hold so many things—gently, carefully, knowing that once she spoke it aloud, it would belong to both of them.

For now, it was hers.

She pulled into the driveway of Oak Watch and saw Grace's backpack hanging on the fence post—her daughter's version of leaving breadcrumbs. Inside, she could hear Lucas fussing, the particular cry that meant he was tired but fighting sleep.

Home.

She stepped out of the car, breathed in the late spring air, and let herself feel the weight of everything she was carrying—marriage, motherhood, work, this new life growing inside her.

It was heavy. But it was also hers.

And for the first time in years, that felt like enough.
