# Chapter 16: Finding Their Footing

**POV: Claire**

The morning light slanted through The Forge's industrial windows, casting long shadows across the polished concrete floor. Claire Donovan stood at the edge of the main training space, coffee in hand, watching Harper Chen work through a crisis simulation on the far side of the room. The young analyst's posture had changed over the past three weeks—less hunched, more centered. Her fingers no longer drummed anxiously against her tablet between decisions.

Progress. Real progress.

Claire had seen a lot of teams in her twenty-year career. Pentagon war rooms where generals moved armies like chess pieces. Fortune 100 boardrooms where a single miscalculation could erase billions in market value. High-stakes environments where excellence wasn't just expected—it was assumed, baseline, the minimum cost of admission.

But sustainable excellence? That was rarer than most people wanted to admit.

She'd started to wonder, somewhere around her third consecutive eighteen-hour day at a defense contractor's crisis command center, if it even existed. If maybe the whole concept was a myth, something people told themselves to justify the burnout, the divorces, the heart attacks at fifty-two.

BSS was different.

She'd known it intellectually when she signed on—knew Jasper Barrett's reputation, had done her homework on the company's culture and values. But knowing something and seeing it were two different things entirely.

Harper paused the simulation, made a note on her tablet, then reset to review her decision tree. No one had told her to do that. No one was watching except Claire, and Harper didn't know she was being observed. This was internal drive, the kind you couldn't teach or mandate.

"She's getting better."

Claire didn't turn. She'd heard Jasper approach—he moved quietly for a man his size, but The Forge's acoustics betrayed footsteps on concrete.

"She is," Claire agreed. "Watch her shoulders. Three weeks ago, they'd be up around her ears by now. Fight-or-flight response to stress triggers."

Jasper moved to stand beside her, his own coffee mug—the chipped one that said "World's Okayest Crisis Manager," a gag gift from his team—cradled in both hands. "You've been working with her on breathing techniques."

"Among other things." Claire took a sip of coffee. "But the breathing's just a tool. What matters is that she's using it. That she recognizes when she needs it."

They watched in comfortable silence as Harper worked through the scenario. It was a modified version of a real case—a biotech company facing simultaneous FDA investigation and insider trading allegations. The kind of multi-front crisis that could paralyze decision-makers if they tried to solve everything at once.

Harper didn't try to solve everything at once.

She triaged. Identified the critical path. Made a call on what could wait and what couldn't. Then she executed, clean and efficient.

"She's finding her balance," Claire said quietly. "The thing that was breaking her—that need to be perfect, to control every variable—she's learning to channel it instead of letting it control her."

"That's all you?"

"That's all her. I just showed her the door." Claire glanced at Jasper. "You built something here. Something that doesn't just burn people up and spit them out when they're used up."

Jasper's expression didn't change, but something shifted in his eyes. "We try."

"No." Claire's voice was firm. "You do. I've worked in a lot of places that tried. That had mission statements about work-life balance and sustainable performance on their walls and absolutely nothing backing them up in practice." She turned to face him fully. "You chose family over a client. Do you know how rare that is at your level?"

"It was the right call."

"It was the hard call. And it made you better." Claire held his gaze. "It made this whole operation better. Because now your people know it's not just talk. They've seen you walk the walk."

Jasper shifted his weight, uncomfortable with the praise in a way that only confirmed Claire's assessment. Leaders who got defensive about their values were usually compensating for something. Leaders who got uncomfortable when those values were acknowledged? Those were the ones who actually lived them.

"Harper's not the only one making progress," Jasper said, clearly redirecting.

"No," Claire agreed, allowing the pivot. "She's not."

---

Two hours later, Claire stood in the smaller conference room—the one they'd started calling the Crucible, because that's where they ran the worst-case scenarios—watching Addison Lee dissect a mock crisis with surgical precision.

The setup was brutal: a pharmaceutical CEO caught in a sex scandal that intersected with a product safety investigation. Salacious enough to dominate news cycles, serious enough to threaten shareholder value, complex enough that every decision created cascading consequences.

Addison had been working the scenario for forty minutes. She'd made three major strategic calls, fielded mock media inquiries from Claire's role-players, and drafted holding statements for five different stakeholder groups.

Now she stood at the whiteboard, mapping second-order effects.

"If we confirm the CEO is stepping down," Addison said, marker moving quickly across the board, "the stock takes an immediate hit. That's first-order, predictable. But second-order—board composition matters. If the Vice Chairman steps in as interim, he brings baggage from the 2019 merger. Wall Street analysts will start asking if this is a boardroom coup disguised as crisis management."

Claire leaned against the wall, arms crossed, saying nothing.

"So we need to control the succession narrative before we control the scandal narrative," Addison continued. "Which means the board has to move first, even though every instinct says address the scandal first." She turned to face Claire. "We get ahead of the boardroom politics, or they become the story within forty-eight hours."

"Show me," Claire said simply.

Addison grabbed her tablet, pulled up a timeline she'd apparently been building in parallel. "Hour zero: CEO announces voluntary leave of absence for personal matters. No details, no timeline. Hour one: Board announces formation of independent review committee—external counsel, external investigators. Hour two: Board separately announces Vice Chairman accepting temporary COO role to handle day-to-day operations, Chair remains Chair, succession planning to begin immediately with outside search firm."

"Why separate the announcements?"

"Because if we bundle them, it looks coordinated. Looks like we planned for this. That raises questions about what we knew and when." Addison's fingers flew across the tablet. "Separate announcements, tight timing—it reads as responsive, not orchestrated. Board taking appropriate action in real time."

Claire pushed off the wall, moved to the whiteboard. She studied Addison's effect map, looking for gaps. "Product safety investigation?"

"That's the separation play. CEO's personal conduct is completely unrelated to product safety protocols. We make that explicit, multiple times, multiple channels. Investigators remain in place, timeline unchanged, no interference." Addison pulled up another document. "We actually accelerate the safety review if possible. Shows we're not circling wagons, we're committed to transparency regardless of leadership transitions."

"That's a risk. What if the investigation finds something?"

"Then we find it first and control the disclosure." Addison's voice was steady. "But either way, we've established that personal scandals don't compromise product integrity. We've drawn a bright line."

Claire studied the younger woman carefully. Addison had arrived at BSS six months ago with impeccable credentials and a slightly desperate edge—the kind of hunger that came from being very good at something and knowing you could be great if you just had the right environment.

She was getting great.

"Walk me through your media strategy," Claire said. "CEO steps down, you've got every outlet from CNN to TMZ calling. What's the first forty-eight hours look like?"

Addison didn't hesitate. She pulled up a media map that would have taken most crisis managers hours to build. "Traditional business press gets priority access to the board chair for on-record statements about succession and governance. That's Wall Street Journal, Financial Times, Bloomberg. We give them the serious story, the one that matters to investors."

"And the tabloids?"

"We don't feed them, but we don't freeze them out either. Standard holding statement: personal matter, respecting privacy, committed to transparency where it concerns company operations." Addison met Claire's eyes. "We let them scream into the void for twenty-four hours. By day two, if we've executed properly, the business press has moved on to succession and governance. The tabloid cycle burns itself out when there's no new fuel."

"What if there is new fuel? What if more allegations surface?"

"Depends on the nature of the allegations." Addison pulled up a decision tree. "If it's more of the same—more affairs, more personal conduct—we hold the line. Personal matter, independent review ongoing. If it crosses into company resources, corporate funds, abuse of position—we adjust. That triggers fiduciary concerns, potentially legal exposure. Different playbook entirely."

Claire nodded slowly. "Show me the different playbook."

For the next twenty minutes, Addison walked through branching scenarios. Each potential development, each possible revelation, she'd mapped the strategic implications and drafted response frameworks. Not just first-order effects—second, third, fourth-order consequences. How each decision would cascade through stakeholder groups, regulatory environments, market dynamics.

She thought in systems.

That was the difference between good crisis managers and great ones. Good crisis managers solved the immediate problem. Great crisis managers understood that in complex systems, solving one problem without considering downstream effects usually just created bigger problems elsewhere.

Addison thought like a systems engineer. Like someone who'd spent time in environments where cascading failures could kill people.

She thought, Claire realized, like Jasper.

Maybe better.

---

Late afternoon sun turned The Forge's windows into sheets of copper light. Most of the team had left for the day—even in a culture that valued sustainable performance, crisis work had its own rhythms. Sometimes you stayed until three in the morning because the work demanded it. Sometimes you left at four because the work was done and grinding for another three hours would just make you worse tomorrow.

Claire found Jasper in his office, reviewing something on his laptop. He looked up when she knocked on the doorframe.

"Got a minute?"

"Always." Jasper closed the laptop. "How'd Addison do on the pharma scenario?"

Claire stepped into the office, closed the door behind her. "That's what I wanted to talk about."

She settled into one of the chairs across from Jasper's desk. The office was sparse—no vanity wall of framed credentials, no carefully curated bookshelf of leadership tomes. Just a few family photos, a whiteboard covered in decision frameworks, and a window overlooking Charlotte's skyline.

"She nailed it," Claire said. "Didn't just nail the immediate crisis management—she anticipated second-order effects I didn't think she'd see. Built out contingency frameworks for scenarios I didn't prime her for."

Jasper leaned back in his chair. "That's good."

"It's better than good." Claire chose her next words carefully. "Jasper, she thinks like you. The systems thinking, the cascading implications, the way she maps stakeholder dynamics across multiple time horizons—that's not standard crisis management training. That's high-level strategic operations."

"She came to us with strong credentials."

"She came to you with consulting firm credentials. Lots of frameworks, lots of theoretical knowledge." Claire shook her head. "This is different. She's not applying frameworks anymore. She's seeing the underlying patterns and building solutions from first principles."

Jasper was quiet for a moment. "You think she's ready for primary on a major case?"

"I think she's ready for more than that." Claire met his eyes. "I think she could be your number two in eighteen months. I think she could be running her own division in three years."

"That's a big jump."

"It's a realistic assessment." Claire pulled out her tablet, called up her training notes. "I've been tracking her decision-making patterns, her growth curve. She's not just learning—she's developing judgment. The kind you can't teach, that has to be cultivated."

She turned the tablet so Jasper could see the analysis. Charts tracking Addison's performance across dozens of simulations, real metrics showing accuracy of threat assessment, speed of decision-making, effectiveness of stakeholder management.

"She's in the ninety-fifth percentile across every category," Claire continued. "And her trajectory is still climbing. Most people plateau once they reach competency. Addison's accelerating."

Jasper studied the data, his expression unreadable. Claire knew him well enough by now to recognize that he was processing, running his own analysis against hers, looking for gaps or wishful thinking.

"What about Harper?" he asked finally.

"Harper's finding her balance," Claire said. "She's going to be an exceptional crisis manager. Detail-oriented, thorough, excellent under pressure once she learned to manage her own stress response. But Harper's strength is execution. She's brilliant at taking a strategy and implementing it flawlessly."

"And Addison's strength is strategy."

"Addison's strength is seeing the game board." Claire took the tablet back. "She thinks like a chess player—not just the next move, but three moves ahead, across multiple games happening simultaneously. That's rare. That's what you do."

Jasper turned his chair to look out the window at Charlotte's skyline. The city was growing, transforming, more cranes dotting the horizon every year. BSS was growing with it—more clients, more complex cases, more need for people who could handle the impossible.

"I've been thinking about succession planning," Jasper said quietly. "Not because I'm going anywhere, but because that's what responsible leadership looks like. Making sure the organization can survive and thrive without you."

Claire waited. She'd learned that Jasper thought out loud sometimes, working through implications, but he needed space to do it.

"My father built BSS on his reputation," Jasper continued. "His relationships, his expertise. When he stepped back, there were questions about whether the company could sustain that without him." He turned back to face Claire. "I don't want to build something that's dependent on me. I want to build something that grows beyond me."

"You're already doing that."

"Maybe." Jasper's expression was thoughtful. "But there's a difference between building a good team and building future leaders. One maintains what you've created. The other takes it places you never imagined."

"You think Addison could take BSS places you haven't imagined?"

"I think," Jasper said slowly, "that Addison sees things I miss. Different perspective, different generation, different way of processing information. That's valuable. That's how organizations evolve instead of calcifying."

Claire smiled. "So you agree with my assessment."

"I agree that she has extraordinary potential." Jasper pulled his laptop back open. "I also think potential and readiness are different things. We need to be careful not to promote her too fast and set her up for failure."

"Agreed. That's why I said eighteen months to number two, not eighteen weeks." Claire stood. "But we should be intentional about her development. Give her opportunities to lead, to make real decisions with real consequences. Not just simulations."

"The NFL crisis is heating up again," Jasper said. "Multiple investigations converging, league office getting nervous. It's going to get worse before it gets better."

"You want to put Addison on it?"

"I want to put her on it as secondary lead. Harper's already embedded, knows the players, has the relationships. But let Addison drive strategy while Harper drives execution." Jasper met Claire's eyes. "See how they work together. See if Addison can lead without overshadowing. That's the real test—not just being brilliant yourself, but making the people around you better."

Claire nodded slowly. "That's smart. And it gives you real data on her leadership capability, not just her individual performance."

"Plus," Jasper added, "if she crashes and burns, Harper's there to catch it before it becomes a disaster for the client."

"You think she might crash and burn?"

"I think she's twenty-nine years old and I'm proposing to give her strategic lead on a crisis involving billionaire owners, federal investigations, and about fifteen different media circuses." Jasper's voice was dry. "So yes, there's a non-zero chance she crashes and burns. But there's also a non-zero chance she becomes exactly what we need her to be—and the only way to find out is to give her the opportunity."

Claire moved toward the door, then paused. "For what it's worth, my money's on the second scenario."

"Mine too," Jasper admitted. "But I've been wrong before."

"Not often."

"Often enough to stay humble." Jasper turned back to his laptop. "Set it up with Harper first. Make sure she's comfortable with the dynamic. Last thing we need is internal friction when we're trying to manage external crisis."

"Will do." Claire opened the door, then looked back. "Jasper? What you said earlier, about building something that grows beyond you—you're already doing that. Harper's finding her balance. Addison's finding her voice. This whole team is becoming more than the sum of its parts." She paused. "That's not luck. That's leadership."

She left before he could respond, but she caught the slight smile on his face as she closed the door.

Outside, The Forge was settling into evening quiet. Harper had finished her simulation and was making notes at one of the workstations. Addison was in a conference room on a video call, probably with a client or maybe just networking—the woman never seemed to stop building relationships.

Claire had worked in a lot of organizations that talked about developing people. Talent development plans and leadership pipelines and succession frameworks, all very impressive in PowerPoint presentations.

BSS actually did it.

They took brilliant people and made them better. Not by grinding them down until they quit or burned out, but by giving them room to grow, challenges to meet, support when they stumbled.

Jasper chose family over a client, and it made him better.

He was choosing his team over easy answers, and it was making them better too.

Claire settled at her own workstation, pulled up her development plans for the quarter. She had work to do—assessments to refine, training scenarios to build, frameworks to adapt for the specific challenges BSS faced.

But for the first time in longer than she wanted to admit, she wasn't wondering if sustainable excellence was possible.

She was seeing it happen in real time.

And she was helping build it.

That, Claire thought as she dove into her work, was worth every Pentagon contract, every Fortune 100 boardroom, every prestigious engagement she'd ever walked away from.

This was the real work.

This was what mattered.
