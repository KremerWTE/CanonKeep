# Chapter 1: New Season, Old Habits

*POV: Jasper Barrett*

---

The Forge was quiet at 5:47 a.m., the way Jasper Barrett preferred it.

He sat at his desk in the corner office, the Charlotte skyline still wrapped in the blue-gray light that preceded dawn. Three monitors glowed in front of him, each one a window into a different crisis unfolding in a different time zone. The NFL ownership dispute that had been simmering for weeks was now threatening to boil over into public litigation. A global supply chain client was facing a whistleblower complaint that could unravel their entire Southeast Asian operation. And somewhere in Eastern Europe, a tech company's intellectual property had been compromised in a way that smelled less like negligence and more like espionage.

Three fires. Three different strategies. One man to coordinate all of them.

He took a sip of coffee—black, lukewarm, his third cup since he'd arrived at four—and let his eyes rest on the photo tucked into the corner of his desk. Elena and Grace at the beach last summer, a kite climbing the frame behind them. Grace was mid-laugh, her hair wild from the wind. Elena was looking at the camera, but her smile was for someone just off-frame. For him.

*Three months since Lucas's baptism,* he thought. Three months since he'd sat in that church pew and felt, for the first time in years, like he was exactly where he was supposed to be.

The medical crisis had changed everything. Elena's collapse, the hospital vigil, the moment when Grace had asked if Daddy was "on our team"—it had cracked something open in him that he'd been welding shut for a decade. He'd turned down the global expansion role. He'd restructured his days to be home for dinner most nights. He'd learned to delegate in ways that still felt uncomfortable, like wearing a suit that didn't quite fit.

But the work didn't stop. The crises didn't pause because Jasper Barrett had decided to be a better husband. If anything, the world seemed to be testing him, throwing three problems at once as if to ask: *Are you sure? Are you really sure you can do this without giving everything?*

His phone buzzed. A text from Harper, his COO:

*New hire arriving at 8. Claire Ashford. You wanted to meet her first thing.*

He'd almost forgotten. Between the NFL mess and the supply chain nightmare, he'd filed Claire's arrival in the back of his mind as a "later" problem.

But she wasn't a problem. She was a solution he'd been looking for.


---

Jasper had found Claire Ashford through Marcus Webb, a Pentagon contact who ran mental performance programs for the Joint Special Operations Command. Jasper had called him after Elena's hospitalization, not for himself—or so he'd told himself—but because he'd noticed something in his team that worried him.

They were running on adrenaline and willpower. Harper was stretching herself thin, her usually sharp edges starting to fray. Addison had been working so hard that Jasper had caught her sleeping at her desk twice in one week. Even Cole and Dean, the operators who seemed impervious to fatigue, were showing the signs of a long campaign without relief.

"You don't need a therapist," Marcus had said on the phone. "You need someone who can make elite performers sustainable. Someone who's been there—who knows what it costs to be the best and what it takes to stay there without burning out."

He'd given Jasper Claire's name.

The file had been impressive. Division I swimmer, Olympic hopeful until a shoulder injury ended her competitive career at twenty. Instead of walking away, she'd reinvented herself as an endurance athlete—four years later, she was an Ironman World Champion in Kona. Then she'd pivoted again, earning a PhD at Stanford in performance psychology, consulting for SEAL Team Six and Fortune 100 executives who ran companies like combat operations.

"She doesn't fix broken people," Marcus had said. "She makes elite performers sustainable."

That was exactly what BSS needed.

---

By eight o'clock, the office had come alive. Analysts filed in with coffee cups and laptops. The operations floor hummed with the sound of keyboards and low conversations. Jasper could hear Addison's voice down the hall, firm and precise as she walked someone through a timeline.

He stepped out of his office just as Harper appeared at the elevator bay, escorting a woman Jasper recognized from her file photo. Claire Ashford was mid-thirties, with the kind of posture that suggested she could hold a plank for an hour without breaking a sweat. Her hair was pulled back in a practical ponytail, and she carried herself with the quiet confidence of someone who had nothing to prove but everything to offer.

"Jasper," Harper said, "this is Claire."

Claire extended her hand. Her grip was firm, her gaze direct. "Mr. Barrett. Thank you for the opportunity."

"Just Jasper," he said. "We don't stand on ceremony here. We stand on results."

"Fair enough." She glanced around the floor, taking in the controlled chaos with an assessing eye. "I've worked with operators before. SEALs, Green Berets, fighter pilots. I've worked with CEOs who thought sleep was a weakness and rest was for retirement. But I've never seen a corporate crisis firm quite like this."

"That's because there isn't one quite like this," Jasper said. "We're in the business of saving companies from themselves. That takes a particular kind of person. And those people have a tendency to burn out if someone isn't watching the canary in the coal mine."

"So I'm the canary-watcher."

"You're the person who makes sure the canary never has to sing."

Claire nodded slowly. "I like that. Most people don't call for help until the canary's already dead."

"That's because most people don't think they're fragile. My team isn't fragile—they're exceptional. But exceptional doesn't mean invincible, and I learned that the hard way."

He didn't elaborate. He didn't need to. The way Claire looked at him suggested she already knew—or could guess—what had forced that lesson home.

"Where do you want me to start?" she asked.

Jasper considered. The NFL situation was consuming Harper's attention, and she'd been working seventeen-hour days for two weeks straight. Addison was handling the supply chain crisis with her usual precision, but even she had limits.

"Start with observation," he said. "Sit in on meetings. Watch how people work. Get a sense of the rhythm before you try to change the tempo."

Claire smiled—the first genuine expression he'd seen from her. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about a lot of things lately." He glanced toward his office, where the three monitors were still glowing with their separate emergencies. "Some of them are even productive."


---

By noon, the NFL situation had escalated from simmering to boiling.

The ownership dispute had started as a private disagreement between two billionaires with more money than sense. One wanted to move the franchise to a new city. The other had built his identity around keeping it where it was. For months, they'd traded legal briefs and passive-aggressive comments at league meetings.

Now, one of them had gone to the press.

Jasper stood at the whiteboard in the main conference room, mapping the media landscape with quick strokes of a black marker. Harper sat at the table with a laptop open, feeding him information as it came in. Addison was on a call in the corner, her voice low and controlled.

"The Post has a source saying the minority owner is preparing to sue," Harper reported. "They're framing it as a hostile takeover attempt."

"Which owner planted that story?" Jasper asked without turning around.

"The one who wants to stay. He's trying to make the mover look like a villain."

"Then the mover will respond with his own leak by dinner. Probably the Journal." Jasper stepped back from the whiteboard. "We need to get ahead of the cycle. Who do we represent?"

"Neither one," Harper said. "We're working for the league. They want this contained before it becomes a national embarrassment."

"Then we don't pick sides. We shape the narrative around league stability, fan interests, legacy. Make both owners look petty if they keep fighting."

Harper nodded, already typing notes. "I'll prep the talking points for the commissioner's office."

Jasper turned to Addison as she ended her call. "Supply chain?"

"Contained for now," she said. "The whistleblower is credible but not connected to media. We have a window to control the internal investigation before it goes external."

"Good. Keep it tight." He looked at the whiteboard, then at the two women who had become his most trusted partners in the years since he'd built BSS. They were both showing the strain—Harper's usual crisp edges were softening with fatigue, and Addison had a tension in her shoulders that spoke to too many hours in too small a space.

"When's the last time either of you slept more than five hours?"

They exchanged glances.

"That long?" Jasper shook his head. "We just hired someone to fix this. Don't make her job harder by proving her right on day one."

Harper smiled, but it didn't reach her eyes. "Says the man who was here at four a.m."

"I'm working on it," he said, and was surprised to find he meant it.


---

Late that afternoon, Jasper found Claire in the small conference room off the main floor, reviewing personnel files with the intensity of a detective studying crime scene photos.

"First impressions?" he asked, leaning against the doorframe.

She looked up. "Your team is extraordinary. Also exhausted. Harper is running on pure willpower—she's lost about ten percent of her reaction time compared to baseline, which tells me she's not sleeping or she's carrying something heavy she's not talking about. Addison is compensating for everyone else's capacity gaps, which works until it doesn't. And you..."

"Me?"

Claire closed the file in front of her. "You've made some changes recently. I can see it in the way people talk about you versus the way they respond to you. There's a gap there—like they're not sure if the new version is permanent."

Jasper didn't answer for a long moment. "Is it that obvious?"

"Only to someone who's looking for it." She stood and walked to the window, gazing out at the late afternoon light. "You want to know what I learned from the SEALs? The ones who survive long careers aren't the ones who never get hurt. They're the ones who learn from getting hurt. They change their tactics, adjust their tempo, build in recovery. The ones who keep doing the same thing that almost killed them? They don't make it."

"And you think I'm one of the ones who's learning."

"I think you're trying." She turned to face him. "But trying isn't the same as succeeding. And your team is watching to see which way it goes."

Jasper thought about Elena, about Grace, about the promise he'd made to himself in that hospital room. About the phone calls he now took in the car instead of the airport. About the dinners he'd attended, even when he knew the work was piling up behind him.

"I'm not going to pretend I have it figured out," he said. "But I'm not going back to what I was."

Claire studied him for a moment, then nodded. "Then let's make sure the people around you don't go there either."

---

That evening, Jasper left The Forge at 6:30—early by his old standards, a small victory by his new ones.

The drive home took him past the hospital where Elena had spent those terrifying nights, past the church where Lucas had been baptized, past the CrossFit gym where Sara trained and where, according to Elena, a new trainer named Kendra had been making waves.

He pulled into the driveway of Oak Watch just as the sky was turning orange. Grace met him at the door, her hair in a crooked ponytail that suggested she'd done it herself.

"Daddy! You're home before dark!"

He swept her up into a hug, breathing in the familiar scent of strawberries and pencil shavings. "I told you I would be."

"You say that a lot," she said, but she was grinning.

Inside, Elena was in the kitchen, Lucas in his bouncer, kicking at the mobile overhead with the single-minded focus of a three-month-old. She looked up when Jasper entered, and for a moment, he saw the shadow of the woman who'd collapsed at the dinner table—tired, worried, uncertain if he'd really changed.

Then she smiled, and the shadow passed.

"You're early," she said.

"I'm trying." He crossed to her, kissed her temple, then her lips. "Trying counts for something, right?"

"It counts for everything." She handed him a wooden spoon. "Now make yourself useful. The risotto needs stirring."

He took the spoon and stepped to the stove, falling into the rhythm of something ordinary, something essential. Grace was chattering about school. Lucas was cooing at his mobile. Elena was humming a song he half-recognized from a decade ago.

And for the first time that day, Jasper Barrett felt like he was exactly where the five feet around him wanted him to be.

But in the back of his mind, the three fires still burned. Tomorrow, he'd have to face them again. Tomorrow, the choice between work and family would test him in ways he couldn't predict.

Tonight, though, he stirred the risotto, and that was enough.

---

*End of Chapter 1*
