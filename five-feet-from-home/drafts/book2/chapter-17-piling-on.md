# Chapter 17: Piling On

**POV: Jasper Barrett**

The call from Marcus Wellington came at 6:47 AM, which meant it was already afternoon in Kinshasa and whatever had gone wrong was probably catastrophic.

"Tell me it's not a coup," Jasper said by way of greeting, phone wedged between his shoulder and ear while he navigated morning traffic on I-277.

"It's not a coup." Marcus's voice carried that particular flavor of stress that came from watching carefully laid plans disintegrate in real time. "It's three factions that all think they're about to execute a coup, which is somehow worse."

Jasper signaled for the exit toward The Forge, mind already shifting gears. The mining consortium case in Central Africa had been simmering for weeks—unstable government, competing interests, billions in rare earth minerals that half a dozen countries wanted access to. BSS had been hired to help the consortium navigate the political landscape and maintain operations through the transition period.

Navigating a three-way coup attempt wasn't exactly in the original scope of work.

"How long until something breaks?" Jasper asked.

"Twelve hours, maybe less. Army chief thinks he has support from the French. Interior minister thinks he has support from the Chinese. And the Vice President thinks he has support from both, which suggests someone's intelligence is very wrong." Marcus paused. "Our clients are exposed across all three factions. When this goes loud, they're going to be on the wrong side of at least two and possibly all three."

"Recommendations?"

"Get out. Pull back to neighboring countries, wait for the dust to settle, negotiate with whoever's left standing."

Jasper pulled into The Forge's parking garage, killed the engine. "That's the safe play."

"That's the smart play," Marcus countered. "Jasper, if we're still on the ground when shooting starts, we're not crisis managers anymore. We're hostages."

"If we pull out, the consortium loses their position. Six months of relationship building, compromised. Competitors move in before the smoke clears." Jasper grabbed his briefcase, headed for the elevator. "What's the window for extracting non-essential personnel while maintaining operational presence?"

He could practically hear Marcus thinking through the logistics. "Seventy-two hours. Maybe ninety-six if we're lucky and the factions keep posturing instead of moving."

"Then we've got time to work the problem." Jasper stepped into the elevator. "I'll brief the team, see if we can't find a fourth option that doesn't involve abandoning ship or getting caught in crossfire."

"There's always a fourth option with you," Marcus said, but there was something like relief in his voice. "I'll send the full situation report. Jasper—be smart about this. No heroics."

"No heroics," Jasper agreed. "Just crisis management."

He hung up as the elevator opened onto The Forge's main floor, where he discovered that the universe had decided 6:47 AM was just the opening act.

Harper Chen stood at the situation board, phone pressed to her ear, her expression suggesting someone was delivering very bad news very quickly. Addison Lee was already at her workstation despite the early hour, typing with the intensity of someone who'd been working for a while, not someone who just arrived.

And Claire Donovan sat in the conference room with a laptop and what looked like a very serious video call happening.

Jasper's phone buzzed. Text from Claire: "War room in 10. Bring coffee."

---

Ten minutes later, Jasper settled into the conference room with his third coffee of the morning and the growing certainty that this was going to be one of those days where the impossible pile-on just kept getting worse.

Claire closed her laptop. "How bad is Africa?"

"Possible three-way coup within twelve hours. Our client's exposed across all factions." Jasper took a long drink of coffee. "Marcus wants to pull out. I told him we'd find option four."

"Of course you did." Claire turned to Harper and Addison, who'd joined them in the conference room. "The NFL situation just escalated. Federal investigation expanded to include three more players, two agents, and the team owner's nephew. League office is panicking, our client is threatening to fire everyone including us, and there's a press conference scheduled for this afternoon that could turn into a complete disaster."

Harper pulled up a document on the main screen. "The owner wants to go aggressive—deny everything, attack the investigators, claim it's a witch hunt. Legal counsel is advising the exact opposite. Communications team is divided. And the head coach just announced he's 'standing by his players' without clearing it with anyone."

"So we've got no unified strategy, multiple power centers giving conflicting guidance, and a ticking clock until someone says something stupid in public." Jasper studied the timeline. "Outstanding. What else?"

Addison raised her hand slightly, which in another context might have been funny—they weren't in school—but the gesture carried a certain tentative quality that suggested she wasn't sure if her contribution was welcome.

"The tech COO situation," Addison said. "The harassment investigation? It just got significantly more complicated."

Jasper had almost forgotten about that one. Silicon Valley executive, allegations of harassment and toxic workplace culture, company trying to navigate the crisis without torpedoing a pending acquisition. It had seemed relatively straightforward three days ago.

"Define significantly more complicated," Claire said.

"Two more women came forward overnight. One of them is the daughter of a major investor. The other one documented everything—emails, texts, recorded conversations." Addison pulled up her notes. "And the acquiring company just put the deal on formal hold pending resolution of the investigation."

Jasper set down his coffee cup with perhaps more force than strictly necessary. "So we've got a potential coup, a federal investigation into NFL corruption, and a tech acquisition falling apart. Anything else want to pile on this morning?"

His phone buzzed. Text from his father: "Need you to call. Family matter. Not urgent but important."

Jasper typed back: "In war room. Will call soon."

He looked up to find Claire watching him with that particular expression that suggested she'd seen the exchange and had opinions about it.

"We're going to run this like a war room training day," Claire announced. "All hands, all cases, full coordination. Jasper, you've got strategic oversight. Harper, Addison—you're running point on NFL and tech respectively. I'm your coach, your resource, and your check on bad decisions."

"What about Africa?" Harper asked.

"I'll handle Africa with Marcus," Jasper said. "But the principles are the same across all three situations—multiple stakeholders with conflicting interests, time pressure, high stakes, and about fifteen different ways this can go catastrophically wrong." He stood, moved to the whiteboard. "So let's start by mapping what we know versus what we think we know versus what we're guessing at."

For the next forty-five minutes, they built situation assessments. Facts in one column, assumptions in another, intelligence gaps in a third. It was a discipline Jasper had learned in military operations and refined through hundreds of crisis engagements—you couldn't solve a problem you didn't understand, and you didn't understand a problem until you knew where your knowledge ended and your assumptions began.

The NFL case was a mess of competing narratives. What they knew for certain: federal investigation, multiple targets, evidence of some kind of financial irregularities. What they assumed: the irregularities involved gambling or game-fixing. What they were guessing at: the scope, the key players, the timeline for indictments.

"We're flying blind on too many variables," Harper said, frustration clear in her voice. "Legal won't share investigation details, the client won't be straight with us about exposure, and every time we think we've mapped the players, someone new pops up."

"Then we work the problem in layers," Addison said quietly. Both Harper and Claire turned to look at her. Addison met their gazes steadily. "We can't control what we don't know. But we can build response frameworks that work regardless of what we learn. Modular crisis management—core principles that adapt to new information instead of complete strategies that break when assumptions fail."

Jasper felt something shift in the room. This was the moment Claire had been talking about—Addison finding her voice, stepping into leadership, offering strategic thinking instead of just execution.

"Show us," he said simply.

Addison moved to the whiteboard, grabbed a marker. "Core principle one: separate the client from the conduct under investigation. We can't defend actions—we don't know what happened, we're not lawyers. But we can defend the organization's commitment to cooperation, transparency, and appropriate consequences if wrongdoing is found."

She sketched a quick framework. "Core principle two: control the internal narrative before the external narrative. Right now, the owner, legal, communications, and coaching staff are all sending different messages. That's the real crisis—not the investigation, but the internal chaos. We fix that first."

"How?" Harper asked.

"We tell them they don't have a choice." Addison's voice was calm but firm. "Right now, everyone thinks they have equal authority to drive strategy. They don't. Someone has to have final decision-making authority, and everyone else has to subordinate their preferences to that authority. That's not crisis management—that's basic organizational discipline."

Claire leaned back in her chair, a slight smile on her face. Jasper recognized the expression. That was Claire watching someone level up in real time.

"Who gets final authority?" Harper pressed. "The owner's not going to subordinate to anyone. That's the whole problem."

"Then we reframe the question." Addison turned to face Harper. "The owner gets final authority on what the organization's values are and what outcomes he wants. Legal gets final authority on legal strategy and risk assessment. Communications gets final authority on message development and delivery. And we get final authority on coordinating all of it into coherent crisis response."

She drew connecting lines on the whiteboard. "Everyone owns their domain. No one owns everything. And we're the integration layer that makes sure the legal strategy doesn't contradict the communications strategy doesn't contradict the organizational values."

Jasper studied the framework, looking for gaps. "What if legal strategy and communications strategy are fundamentally incompatible?"

"Then we surface the conflict explicitly and force a decision." Addison didn't hesitate. "We present both approaches, show the trade-offs, and make the owner choose. What we don't do is let them run parallel strategies that will eventually crash into each other in public."

Harper was nodding slowly. "That could work. If we can get them to accept the framework."

"We don't ask them to accept it," Addison said. "We tell them this is how we operate. If they want BSS to manage their crisis, this is the operating model. If they want to keep running chaos, they can find another firm." She met Jasper's eyes. "Right?"

There it was. The real test. Not just strategic thinking, but the confidence to assert boundaries, to walk away from a client if necessary to maintain professional integrity.

"Right," Jasper confirmed. "We're crisis managers, not crisis enablers. If they won't let us do our job properly, we can't help them."

Relief flickered across Addison's face, there and gone in a moment. She turned back to the whiteboard, started building out the next layer of the framework.

Claire caught Jasper's eye, gave a tiny nod. This was what she'd been talking about. Not just potential—realized capability. Addison wasn't asking permission anymore. She was claiming her authority and checking if Jasper would back her.

He would.

---

By noon, the war room had expanded to include three more BSS analysts, two contract specialists, and Marcus Wellington video-conferenced in from Kinshasa. The Forge's main conference room looked like something out of a thriller movie—multiple screens showing different crisis situations, people working phones and laptops, coffee mugs and energy drinks scattered across every flat surface.

Organized chaos. Jasper's natural habitat.

"Africa update," Marcus said from the main screen. "French are backing out—their intelligence says the army chief doesn't have the support he thinks he has. Chinese are doubling down on the interior minister. Vice President just gave a speech that was either brilliant positioning or complete delusion, I honestly can't tell."

"What's our client's exposure?" Jasper asked.

"If the interior minister wins, we're golden—he's been our primary contact, we've got good relationships. If the Vice President wins, we're okay—he's neutral on foreign investment, just wants stability. If the army chief wins..." Marcus grimaced. "He's on record calling the mining consortium 'neo-colonial exploitation.' We're not golden."

"Odds on each scenario?"

"Forty percent interior minister, thirty-five percent Vice President, twenty-five percent army chief. With a five percent margin for complete chaos where nobody wins and the whole thing devolves into civil conflict."

Jasper did the math. Seventy-five percent chance of acceptable outcomes. Twenty-five percent chance of disaster. "Not the worst odds we've worked with."

"No, but the disaster scenario is really disastrous," Marcus countered. "If the army chief takes over, our people aren't just losing business relationships—they're potentially at physical risk. He's been making nationalist noises that could turn into hostage situations very quickly."

Across the room, Jasper heard Addison on a video call with the tech company's board. Her voice was steady, professional, making points with the kind of authority that didn't invite argument. Harper was next to her, feeding information and tracking action items with the precision of an air traffic controller.

They were working together. Not just coexisting, but actually collaborating—Addison's strategic vision combined with Harper's operational excellence. Exactly what he'd hoped for when he set up the partnership.

"Marcus, start contingency planning for emergency extraction," Jasper said. "I want transport options, safe routes, fallback positions. But don't execute yet. We're not abandoning position unless we have to."

"Copy that. I'll have options to you by end of day."

The video call ended. Jasper turned to find Kendra Morrison standing in the doorway. His executive assistant looked uncharacteristically uncertain, which was notable because Kendra's default state was supremely confident.

"You need something?" Jasper asked.

"The NFL owner is on line three. He's... not happy." Kendra's expression suggested that was an understatement. "He's demanding to speak with you directly, says he won't talk to 'junior associates' anymore."

Jasper glanced at Harper, who'd clearly heard. She gave a slight shrug that communicated resignation mixed with irritation.

"Tell him I'll call him back in thirty minutes," Jasper said. "Right now I'm managing a potential coup, and that takes priority."

Kendra's eyebrows rose fractionally. "I'll tell him you're on another urgent client matter."

"No. Tell him I'm managing a potential coup. Exact words." Jasper turned back to his laptop. "He needs to understand that the world doesn't revolve around his crisis, and we've got other clients who need attention."

"That's... going to make him angrier."

"Probably. But it'll also recalibrate his expectations." Jasper met Kendra's eyes. "If he fires us because I made him wait thirty minutes, we're better off without him. A client who won't let us manage our own operations isn't a client we can help."

Kendra nodded slowly. "I'll deliver the message."

She left. Claire looked up from her own laptop. "You're risking the engagement."

"I'm setting boundaries." Jasper pulled up the NFL case file. "Addison's right—we can't help clients who won't let us do our jobs. If he can't accept that we have other priorities, then we're just expensive babysitters, not crisis managers."

"Fair point." Claire returned to her screen. "For what it's worth, I think you're right. But that doesn't mean it won't blow up in your face."

"Story of my life," Jasper muttered.

The next two hours were a masterclass in controlled chaos. Marcus sent extraction plans that needed review. The tech company's acquisition team wanted a video conference to discuss timeline for resolving the harassment investigation. The NFL owner did indeed fire them—then called back twenty minutes later to un-fire them after his lawyer apparently explained that no other firm would take the case at this stage.

Through it all, Jasper watched his team operate.

Harper managed the NFL chaos with grim determination, building communication protocols and stakeholder maps while simultaneously keeping the client from self-destructing. She'd learned to manage her stress response—when things got overwhelming, Jasper saw her pause, breathe, reset. Claire's training paying dividends.

Addison ran the tech case like a conductor leading an orchestra. Multiple moving pieces, dozens of stakeholders, constantly shifting variables. She didn't panic. Didn't freeze. Just processed information, made decisions, adapted when new information changed the landscape.

And Claire moved between them like a coach on a sidelines, offering guidance, asking questions, pushing them to think harder about second-order effects and stakeholder psychology.

It was working. The team was actually functioning like a team, not just a collection of individual contributors.

Around 3 PM, during a brief lull between crisis conference calls, Kendra appeared in the doorway again.

"The board call for the tech case is starting in five minutes," she said. Then, almost as an afterthought: "I've been listening to how Addison runs her stakeholder calls."

Everyone looked up. Kendra didn't usually offer operational commentary—her domain was administration and coordination, not crisis strategy.

"And?" Claire prompted.

"She does this thing where she recaps what everyone said before she responds. Makes sure she understood their concerns correctly." Kendra leaned against the doorframe. "It slows down the conversation just enough that people stop talking past each other. They start actually listening instead of just waiting for their turn to talk."

It was a small observation. The kind of thing someone might not notice if they weren't paying attention.

But Jasper had learned over the years that the best insights often came from unexpected sources. People who weren't crisis managers but who spent their time observing how people communicated, how organizations functioned.

"That's a good catch," Addison said, looking at Kendra with genuine interest. "Does it seem like it's working? Like people are actually hearing each other?"

Kendra considered. "Yeah. The last call with the board, they were fighting less and problem-solving more. Could be coincidence, but..." She shrugged.

"It's not coincidence," Claire said. "That's active listening technique—acknowledge before advancing. It's a core de-escalation skill." She looked at Addison. "You're doing it instinctively?"

"I guess?" Addison frowned. "I just noticed that when people feel heard, they're less defensive. And when they're less defensive, they make better decisions."

Harper was nodding. "I should do that more. Half my problems with the NFL owner are that he doesn't think anyone's listening to his concerns."

"Everyone's concerns are valid," Jasper said. "Even when they're wrong. Especially when they're wrong—invalid concerns usually come from fear, and fear makes people irrational. You can't logic someone out of fear. You have to acknowledge it first."

Kendra pushed off the doorframe. "Your board call starts in three minutes. Conference room two is set up." She paused. "For what it's worth, watching you all work today—this is impressive. I've been here four years, and I've never seen the team operate quite like this."

She left before anyone could respond.

Claire broke the silence. "She's right. This is different."

"Different how?" Harper asked.

"You're thinking like a team instead of a collection of specialists." Claire gestured at the whiteboards, the situation maps, the coordination documents spread across multiple screens. "You're not just managing individual cases—you're managing the intersection of cases, the resource allocation, the strategic prioritization. That's senior leadership capability."

Jasper felt the weight of that observation. Claire wasn't just talking about tactical execution. She was talking about organizational maturity, the difference between a good consulting firm and an great one.

"We've still got three active crises and about forty-seven ways this day can end badly," Jasper pointed out.

"True." Claire's smile was slight but genuine. "But you're handling it. All of it. And nobody's panicking, nobody's breaking, nobody's cutting corners because they're overwhelmed." She looked at each of them in turn. "That's not luck. That's culture. That's what happens when you build a team that can handle the impossible."

Addison's phone buzzed. "Board call starting."

"Go," Jasper said. "Harper, you're backup—monitor the call, jump in if Addison needs support or if technical details need clarification."

They moved toward conference room two. Jasper turned back to his own laptop, pulled up the Africa situation. Marcus had sent three extraction scenarios, each with risk assessments and resource requirements.

His phone buzzed. Another text from his father: "Still need that call. When you have time."

Jasper typed: "After 6. This is a bad day."

Response came immediately: "They're all bad days in your line of work. Call when you can."

Fair point.

Claire settled into the chair next to him. "Your dad?"

"Wants to talk. Says it's not urgent but important, which is dad-code for 'urgent but I don't want to stress you out.'" Jasper rubbed his eyes. "I'll call him tonight."

"Family matters," Claire said quietly. "That's what makes this work. The fact that you won't sacrifice family for clients, won't sacrifice your team for profits, won't sacrifice your principles for easy answers." She nodded toward conference room two, where Addison and Harper were visible through the glass, settling in for their board call. "They see that. They know you mean it. That's why they can operate at this level—because they trust the foundation."

Jasper didn't respond immediately. He thought about his father, about the call he needed to make, about the choice he'd made months ago to prioritize Lily's needs over a client's demands.

It had felt like failure at the time. Like he was letting people down, breaking commitments, proving that he couldn't handle the pressure.

But Claire was right. It had made him better. Made the whole team better. Because it established that they were building something sustainable, not just burning bright and fast until everyone crashed.

"The coup is going to break in the next few hours," Jasper said, redirecting to safer territory. "Marcus needs a decision on positioning—do we try to influence the outcome, or do we just protect our client's position regardless of who wins?"

"What do you want to do?"

"I want to fix the whole country, establish stable democratic governance, and ensure equitable distribution of mineral wealth." Jasper's voice was dry. "But since that's not in our scope of work, I'll settle for getting our client through this without anyone getting killed or imprisoned."

"Then that's your answer." Claire pulled up the extraction plans on her own screen. "We're not nation-builders. We're crisis managers. We control what we can control, we mitigate what we can't, and we don't confuse the two categories."

It was a good framework. Clean, clear, actionable.

Through the glass of conference room two, Jasper watched Addison present to the tech company's board. Her posture was confident, her gestures measured. Harper sat beside her, laptop open, ready to support.

They were going to be okay. All of them.

The crises would resolve—they always did, one way or another. Some would end well, some would end badly, most would end somewhere in between with mixed outcomes and compromises.

But the team would survive it. Would grow from it.

That, Jasper thought, was the real measure of success. Not whether you won every battle, but whether you built something that could survive the losses and come back stronger.

His phone rang. Marcus.

"Africa's moving," Marcus said without preamble. "Interior minister just arrested the army chief. Vice President is calling for calm. French are officially neutral, Chinese are quiet. This might actually resolve peacefully."

"Might?"

"Sixty-forty odds. But better than this morning's numbers." Marcus sounded cautiously optimistic. "I'm holding extraction plans in reserve but standing down from active prep. We'll monitor overnight, reassess in twelve hours."

"Good work." Jasper made notes in the case file. "Keep me posted if anything changes."

He hung up, updated the situation board. One crisis trending toward resolution. Two still active and volatile.

Not bad for a day that started with three simultaneous disasters.

Claire was watching him. "You do this thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"You compartmentalize. Africa resolves, you update the board, make your notes, move to the next problem. No celebration, no relief. Just on to the next crisis." She tilted her head slightly. "Does it ever end? The crisis treadmill?"

Jasper considered the question seriously. "Not really. There's always another crisis. Another client in trouble, another impossible situation." He looked at the team—Addison and Harper in their board call, analysts working phones and laptops, Kendra coordinating logistics. "But that's the job. That's what we signed up for."

"And it doesn't burn you out?"

"It would," Jasper admitted. "If this was all there was. But it's not." He thought about Lily, about his father, about the call he'd make tonight. "The work matters. The team matters. The principles matter. As long as those things stay true, the treadmill is sustainable."

"Sustainable excellence," Claire murmured.

"Sustainable excellence," Jasper echoed.

Through the glass, Addison was wrapping up the board call. Harper was gathering materials, making notes. They'd handled it. Whatever the board had thrown at them, they'd managed it.

Jasper's phone buzzed. Text from the NFL owner: "Need update. Call me."

He looked at Claire. "Back to the treadmill."

"Back to the treadmill," she agreed.

But she was smiling. And so was he.

Because this was the work that mattered.

And they were building something that would last.
