# Chapter 6: Stacking the Deck

*POV: Jasper Barrett*

---

The Forge had never felt more like a war room.

Jasper stood at the center of the main conference space, three monitors arrayed behind him like command screens in a tactical operations center. On the left: a live feed of sports media coverage showing two NFL owners trading barbs on competing networks. In the middle: a map of West Africa with red zones marking instability. On the right: a cascade of social media posts, each one threatening to turn the NFL dispute from a private squabble into a league-wide embarrassment.

It was 7:15 a.m. on a Thursday, and the fires were already burning out of control.

"They went public," Harper said from the table, her laptop open, her expression grim. "Both of them. Coordinated media blitz at six a.m. Eastern."

Jasper didn't look away from the monitors. "Who broke first?"

"Technically? The minority owner. But the majority owner's team had a counter-statement ready within ninety seconds. This was planned."

"Then it's not a breakdown. It's a strategy."

Addison appeared in the doorway, her dark hair pulled back in a sharp bun, a tablet in her hands. She moved with the kind of controlled efficiency that suggested she'd been awake for hours. "The league office is on line two. Commissioner's legal team wants a call at eight. Chris Cole is on his way here—he'll arrive in twenty minutes."

Jasper turned to face them both. Harper looked tired but steady, her usual crisp professionalism holding despite the seventeen-hour days she'd been working for two weeks straight. Addison looked sharper than ever, but he could see the tension in her shoulders, the way her jaw was set just a fraction too tight.

He'd learned to read the signs. Claire had taught him that much.

"Bring Addison into the strategy session," he said to Harper. "I want her in the room when Chris arrives."

Harper glanced at Addison, then back to Jasper. "You sure? This is high-stakes client management. She's never—"

"She's ready," Jasper said. "And we need the bandwidth. You're stretched too thin, and I can't be in two places at once."

Addison's expression didn't change, but he saw the flicker of something—surprise, maybe, or gratitude—cross her face before she masked it.

"Understood," she said quietly.

"Good. Harper, pull the media timeline. I want to know who talked to which reporter and when. Addison, get me the ownership agreements—every clause, every option, every out. If there's a legal pressure point, I want to know about it before the lawyers do."

They moved without hesitation, falling into the rhythm of crisis response like operators running a mission brief. Jasper watched them for a moment, then turned back to the monitors.

On the left screen, the minority owner was on ESPN, speaking in clipped, controlled sentences about "franchise legacy" and "community trust." On a competing network, the majority owner was countering with language about "financial sustainability" and "long-term vision."

It was a public relations knife fight dressed up as a philosophical debate.

Jasper's phone buzzed. A text from Caroline Westbrook, the finance professor who had become one of his most valuable strategic advisors:

*This isn't about the team. It's about ego. Neither one will back down unless someone forces them to.*

He typed back:

*Then we force them.*

---

By the time Chris Cole arrived, Jasper had the bones of a strategy mapped on the whiteboard.

The quarterback looked every inch the franchise face: tall, composed, dressed in a tailored polo and jeans that somehow managed to look both casual and professional. But his eyes were tight with stress, and when he shook Jasper's hand, his grip was harder than usual.

"This is a nightmare," Chris said without preamble. "I've got reporters calling my agent, my wife, my pastor. They want me to pick a side."

"Then don't," Jasper said.

Chris blinked. "What?"

"Don't pick a side. Make them pick you."

Harper leaned forward from her seat at the table. "The league doesn't want this in the news. It makes them look unstable, and instability costs money. If we position you as the voice of reason—the player who cares about the team, not the boardroom politics—we put pressure on both owners to settle this quietly."

Addison slid a folder across the table toward Chris. "We've drafted a statement for you. It doesn't name names. It doesn't take sides. It talks about the fans, the city, the legacy of the franchise. It makes both owners look petty if they keep fighting."

Chris opened the folder and scanned the first page. His expression shifted from skepticism to something closer to relief. "This... yeah. This is exactly what I needed."

"Good," Jasper said. "Because we're going to use it as leverage. The commissioner's office wants this contained. The owners want you as an asset. You're going to tell them both that you'll release this statement—or stay silent—depending on how they handle the next forty-eight hours."

Chris looked up. "You want me to threaten them?"

"I want you to remind them that you have a voice," Jasper said. "And that voice is worth more to them than their egos."

Harper smiled—the kind of sharp, satisfied expression she got when a plan started to come together. "We'll coordinate with the league office. They'll apply pressure from their end. You apply it from yours. Between the two, we force a settlement."

Chris exhaled slowly, then nodded. "All right. Let's do it."

---

The call with the commissioner's legal team went exactly as Jasper expected: tense, cautious, and ultimately productive.

Harper handled the media strategy, walking the league through how they could frame a settlement as "collaborative leadership" rather than capitulation. Jasper focused on the leverage points—the ways the league could use Chris's silence or statement to force the owners back to the negotiating table.

Addison sat beside him, taking notes with the kind of precision that suggested she was absorbing every word, every tactic, every shift in tone. When the league's general counsel asked a question about contractual obligations, she pulled up the relevant clause before Jasper even had to ask.

"Sharp," Harper murmured after the call ended. "She's learning fast."

"She always was," Jasper said. "We just didn't give her the room to show it."

By mid-morning, the strategy was in motion. Chris released a carefully worded statement through his agent, one that praised the franchise's history and called for "unity and focus on the game." The league office issued a statement minutes later, echoing Chris's language and calling for "constructive dialogue."

The media cycle shifted. Instead of covering the feud, outlets started covering the response—the player's plea, the league's intervention, the growing pressure on the owners to settle.

It wasn't a resolution. But it was momentum.

---

The Africa situation was less manageable.

At noon, Jasper pulled up the encrypted briefing Cole had sent from Lagos. The coup they'd contained three months ago had spawned a new crisis: a power vacuum, shifting alliances, and a fragile interim government that was already showing cracks.

"Western partner needs extraction cover again," Cole's message read. "This time it's three executives and their families. Window closes in seventy-two hours."

Jasper stared at the screen, his jaw tight.

This was the kind of operation that required boots on the ground, back-channel negotiations, and the kind of risk that made even seasoned operators nervous. It was also the kind of work that had nearly cost him his family once before.

Harper appeared in the doorway. "You're thinking about going, aren't you?"

"I'm thinking Cole and Dean can't do this alone."

"They've done harder."

"Not with families involved."

Harper crossed her arms. "Jasper, you promised Elena you wouldn't chase every fire. You promised yourself."

He looked at her, then at the encrypted message on his screen. "This isn't chasing a fire, Harper. This is making sure the people we trust don't burn."

She didn't answer for a long moment. Then she sighed. "If you go, you take Addison with you. She needs the field experience, and you need someone who can manage logistics while you're managing the operation."

"Addison?" Jasper frowned. "She's never done extraction work."

"Then it's time she learned. You said she was ready. Prove it."

---

By the time Jasper called Addison into his office, the decision was already made.

She stood in front of his desk, her expression carefully neutral, her hands clasped in front of her.

"How's your passport?" Jasper asked.

Her eyes widened slightly. "Up to date. Why?"

"Because we're going to Africa. Cole and Dean need backup on an extraction. It's high-risk, time-sensitive, and outside your usual scope of work. But I need someone I can trust to manage the logistics, coordinate with local assets, and keep the operation running smoothly while I'm in the field."

Addison didn't hesitate. "When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow night. We'll fly into Lagos, link up with Cole and Dean, and move into position. Seventy-two-hour window, maybe less."

"What do I need to know?"

Jasper opened a folder on his desk and slid it toward her. "Everything's in here. Read it tonight. Memorize the key players, the timeline, the contingencies. We brief at six a.m. tomorrow."

She picked up the folder, her fingers steady. "I won't let you down."

"I know," Jasper said. And he meant it.

---

That evening, Jasper left The Forge at 7:30—later than he'd promised Elena, but earlier than his old habits would have allowed.

The drive home took him past the hospital where Elena had collapsed, past the church where they'd baptized Lucas, past the gym where Grace took her swim lessons. The city was lit up against the dusk, familiar and grounding.

He pulled into the driveway of Oak Watch just as the last light was fading from the sky. Inside, he could hear Grace's voice, high and bright, talking to Elena about something that had happened at school.

Elena met him at the door, Lucas on her hip, her expression unreadable.

"You're late," she said quietly.

"I know. I'm sorry."

She studied him for a moment, then stepped aside to let him in. "Grace has been asking for you all evening."

"I'll go find her."

He started toward the living room, but Elena's voice stopped him.

"Jasper."

He turned.

"Whatever you're about to tell me," she said, "just say it."

He took a breath. "I have to go to Africa. Cole and Dean need backup on an extraction. I leave tomorrow night."

Elena's jaw tightened. "How long?"

"Three days. Maybe four."

"And you couldn't say no."

"I could have," he said. "But I didn't want to."

She looked at him for a long moment, her eyes searching his face. Then she nodded, once. "Don't make me regret believing you've changed."

"I won't," he said.

But as he walked into the living room to find Grace, he wondered if he was making a promise he could keep—or stacking a deck that was already rigged against him.

---

Later that night, after Grace was asleep and Lucas was settled in his crib, Jasper sat in the study with his laptop open, reviewing the Africa briefing one more time.

Elena appeared in the doorway, two mugs of tea in her hands. She set one on his desk without a word, then settled into the chair across from him.

"You're bringing Addison," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Harper insisted. She thinks Addison needs the field experience."

"And you agree?"

"I do."

Elena sipped her tea, her gaze steady. "Just don't forget who's waiting for you when you get back."

"I won't," Jasper said. "I promise."

She stood, crossed to him, and kissed the top of his head. "Good. Because I'm holding you to it."

When she left, the study felt emptier than it should have. Jasper looked at the photo on his desk—Elena and Grace at the beach, the kite climbing the frame behind them—and felt the weight of the choice he'd just made.

He was trying to be two people at once: the crisis manager who never backed down, and the husband and father who had promised to stay close.

So far, he was managing. But the cracks were starting to show, and he knew it was only a matter of time before something broke.

On the other side of the city, Harper sat in her apartment, a glass of wine in her hand and her phone on the table beside her.

Ethan had texted her three times that evening. She'd answered once, with a distracted "Sorry, work crazy. Talk tomorrow."

It was the kind of response she used to send without thinking. Now, it felt like a lie.

She picked up her phone and stared at the screen, then set it back down.

Tomorrow, she told herself. She'd call him tomorrow.

But tomorrow always seemed to slip away, and Harper was starting to realize she was letting it.
