# Chapter 14: The Fixer's Eye

*POV: Claire Ashford*

---

Claire had seen operators break in a thousand different ways.

Some went loud—explosive, dramatic, spectacular. Others went quiet, like a slow leak in a tire you didn't notice until the car was riding on rims. She'd seen SEALs who could survive anything the enemy threw at them collapse under the weight of their own expectations. She'd seen fighter pilots who could handle twelve-hour missions lose it over a late dinner reservation.

But she'd never seen anyone quite like Addison Price.

Claire had been at The Forge for two weeks now, observing. Jasper had been clear about her role: watch, assess, understand the rhythm before trying to change it. So she'd sat in on meetings, studied personnel files, tracked patterns in how people worked, when they arrived, when they left, what they ate, how they carried themselves when they thought no one was watching.

And she'd watched Addison.

The woman was brilliant—that was obvious from the first five minutes. She could read a room like a tactical map, anticipating problems three moves ahead, seeing angles that others missed entirely. She managed crises with the kind of calm precision Claire had only seen in the most elite operators she'd worked with at DEVGRU.

But she was also running herself into the ground.

It was in the small things: the second cup of coffee at 2 PM that became a third at 4 PM. The way she rolled her shoulders when she thought no one was looking, trying to work out tension that had settled there permanently. The laptop that never closed, even during lunch. The phone calls she took while walking to the bathroom because fifteen seconds of downtime was fifteen seconds wasted.

Claire had seen it before. Excellence as addiction. The high of solving the impossible problem, the validation of being the person everyone needed, the identity built so entirely around competence that rest felt like failure.

Addison was going to burn out. Not today, maybe not next month, but eventually. And when she did, it was going to be spectacular.

---

The strategy session was supposed to start at nine.

Claire arrived at 8:45, settling into the back corner of the conference room with a notebook and her observations from the past two weeks. She'd learned that the best intelligence came from watching what happened before the meeting officially started—the sidebar conversations, the body language, the unspoken hierarchies.

Addison arrived at 8:47, already on her phone, a laptop tucked under one arm and a coffee in her free hand. Her hair was pulled back in a sharp bun that probably took thirty seconds but looked like it had been styled by professionals. She was wearing a charcoal blazer over a silk blouse, the kind of outfit that said *I'm in control* even if everything else was chaos.

She glanced at Claire, offered a polite nod, then took her seat at the head of the table.

Harper arrived next, looking slightly more rumpled than usual—her blazer had a wrinkle near the hem, and her hair was swept back in a bun that was functional rather than perfect. She dropped into the chair beside Addison and immediately pulled out her own laptop.

"Two hours of sleep," Harper muttered. "Tell me this meeting is going to be worth it."

"It will be if we solve the Lagos problem before it becomes a public relations nightmare," Addison said without looking up from her screen.

"That's optimistic."

"That's the job."

Claire made a note: *Harper exhausted. Addison deflecting with task focus. Both running on fumes.*

The rest of the team filtered in over the next few minutes—junior analysts, a media strategist, Cole from operations. Jasper was last, arriving exactly at nine with the kind of timing that suggested he'd been waiting outside until the official start.

He took his seat at the opposite end of the table from Addison, and for a moment, Claire saw the dynamic clearly: Jasper was the founder, the face of the company, the one clients called when things went critical. But Addison was the engine. She was the one who made it all work.

"All right," Jasper said, glancing around the table. "Let's talk Lagos. Harper, where are we?"

Harper pulled up a presentation on the screen. "The whistleblower went public yesterday. Local media picked it up, and now we've got international outlets sniffing around. The client is panicking, which means they're making decisions without consulting us."

"What kind of decisions?" Addison asked.

"The kind that make things worse." Harper clicked to the next slide. "They issued a statement denying everything, which of course made them look guilty. They fired the whistleblower, which triggered a wrongful termination lawsuit. And they're refusing to cooperate with the internal investigation we recommended."

Jasper leaned back in his chair, his expression unreadable. "So we're putting out fires they keep lighting."

"Exactly."

Claire watched Addison. The woman hadn't looked up from her laptop, but her fingers had stopped moving on the keys. She was listening, processing, already building a strategy in her head.

"We need to get ahead of the narrative," Addison said. "If we let the media control the story, we're done. We need to frame this as a company taking accountability, not a company covering up."

"They're not taking accountability," one of the analysts pointed out. "They're denying everything."

"Then we make them." Addison finally looked up, her gaze sharp. "We draft a new statement. Full transparency. Acknowledge the allegations, commit to a third-party investigation, promise consequences if wrongdoing is found. We take the wind out of the media's sails by doing what they expect us to fight."

Harper frowned. "The client's not going to like that."

"The client doesn't have to like it. They have to survive it." Addison's tone was firm, the kind of certainty that left no room for argument. "If they keep fighting, this drags out for months and destroys their reputation. If they get ahead of it now, they control the timeline."

Jasper nodded slowly. "I agree. Harper, can you draft the statement and get it to the client by end of day?"

"Already on it," Harper said, typing notes.

The meeting continued for another forty minutes—logistics, media strategy, contingency plans if the situation escalated. Claire watched Addison the entire time.

The woman was extraordinary.

She didn't just respond to problems—she anticipated them. When Harper mentioned a potential regulatory complication, Addison had already mapped out three scenarios and their solutions. When one of the analysts raised a concern about employee morale, Addison pivoted immediately to internal communications strategy. She saw the whole board while everyone else was focused on individual pieces.

But Claire also saw the cost.

Addison's shoulders were tight, her jaw set in a way that suggested tension she was carrying long after the crisis ended. She answered questions with precision, but there was no warmth in it, no connection. She was operating in crisis mode, the same way Claire had seen operators function in combat zones—hyper-focused, relentless, and completely disconnected from anything that wasn't mission-critical.

It was effective. It was also unsustainable.

---

When the meeting ended, Addison was the first one out the door, already on her phone before she'd cleared the threshold. Claire packed up her notebook slowly, giving the room time to empty, then followed.

She found Addison in her office, laptop open, phone tucked between her ear and shoulder, typing with both hands.

Claire knocked on the doorframe.

Addison glanced up, held up one finger in a *just a minute* gesture, then finished her call. "I'll have the draft to you in an hour," she said into the phone, then hung up.

"Claire," Addison said, setting the phone down. "What can I do for you?"

"I wanted to talk about the strategy session."

Addison's expression shifted slightly—still professional, but with a hint of wariness. "Did I miss something?"

"No." Claire stepped into the office, closing the door behind her. "The opposite, actually. You didn't miss anything. You saw everything."

Addison's eyebrows rose. "I'm not sure I follow."

Claire crossed to the window, looking out at the Charlotte skyline. "I worked with DEVGRU—SEAL Team Six—for three years. I've been in rooms with some of the best operators in the world. People who could read a battlefield in seconds, who could see threats before they materialized, who could anticipate problems most people wouldn't notice until it was too late."

She turned back to Addison. "You remind me of them."

Addison didn't respond immediately. When she did, her tone was cautious. "Is that a compliment or a warning?"

"Both."

Claire moved closer, resting her hands on the back of the chair across from Addison's desk. "You see things. Most people react to fires. You smell the smoke before it starts. That's rare. That's the kind of skill that makes you invaluable."

"But?"

"But the operators I worked with? The ones who were the best at reading the battlefield? They were also the ones most likely to burn out. Because when you see everything, you feel responsible for everything. And eventually, that weight breaks you."

Addison's expression didn't change, but Claire saw the flicker of recognition in her eyes. The acknowledgment that someone had finally named the thing she'd been carrying alone.

"I'm managing," Addison said quietly.

"I know you are. That's the problem." Claire sat down, leaning forward slightly. "Managing isn't the same as thriving. You're running on fumes, Addison. I've been watching you for two weeks. You're here before anyone else, you leave after everyone else, and I'd bet money you're answering emails at midnight."

"That's the job."

"No. That's the trap." Claire's voice was gentle but firm. "Crisis management isn't about putting out fires. It's about seeing which sparks will catch and dealing with them before they become fires. You're brilliant at that with clients. But you're ignoring the sparks in your own life."

Addison was quiet for a long moment, her fingers drumming lightly on the edge of her desk. "What are you suggesting?"

"I'm suggesting we work together." Claire pulled out her notebook, flipping to a page she'd prepared that morning. "Scenario exercises. Crisis simulations. Not for clients—for you. I want to train you to see the patterns in your own life the way you see them in everyone else's."

"I don't need—"

"Yes, you do." Claire's interruption was firm but not unkind. "You're one bad week away from a collapse, Addison. Maybe it's a health crisis. Maybe it's a mistake that costs a client. Maybe it's just waking up one day and realizing you've spent ten years building a career and zero years building a life." She paused. "I've seen it happen. And I'm here to make sure it doesn't happen to you."

Addison stared at her, and for the first time since Claire had arrived at The Forge, she saw the armor crack. Just a little. Just enough.

"What would these exercises look like?" Addison asked finally.

"We start small. Scenario planning—not for clients, but for you. What does your week look like if you sleep eight hours a night? What does your calendar look like if you block off time that's non-negotiable? What does success look like if it's not measured by how many crises you solve?"

Addison's lips quirked in something that wasn't quite a smile. "That sounds like therapy."

"It's not therapy. It's tactical planning." Claire leaned back in her chair. "You're an operator, Addison. You think in scenarios, contingencies, risk mitigation. I'm just asking you to apply that same skill set to yourself."

Addison was quiet again, her gaze drifting to the window. Claire didn't push. She'd learned a long time ago that the best way to get elite performers to change was to let them come to the decision on their own.

Finally, Addison looked back at her. "Okay."

"Okay?"

"Okay." Addison's voice was steady, but there was something underneath it—relief, maybe, or hope. "Let's try it."

Claire smiled. "Good. We'll start tomorrow. Ten AM. Bring your calendar and your coffee. We're going to rebuild your week from the ground up."

Addison nodded, then hesitated. "Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Why do you do this? The performance coaching, the sustainability work. You were an elite athlete. You could've stayed in that world. Why shift to helping other people?"

Claire considered the question. It was one she'd been asked a hundred times, and the answer was always the same.

"Because I was one bad decision away from destroying myself," she said simply. "I was training so hard for the Olympics that I tore my shoulder apart. Ended my swimming career at twenty. I was devastated. But I was also relieved. Because for the first time in my life, I wasn't defined by what I could do. I had to figure out who I was when I wasn't performing."

She paused, meeting Addison's gaze. "That's the work. Not learning how to perform better. Learning how to be a person who happens to perform at a high level. There's a difference. And once you find it, everything changes."

Addison didn't respond, but Claire saw the way her expression softened, the way her shoulders dropped just slightly. It wasn't much. But it was a start.

"Ten AM tomorrow," Claire said, standing. "Don't be late."

"I'm never late."

"I know. That's also something we're going to work on."

Claire left the office, closing the door quietly behind her. She walked back through the operations floor, past analysts hunched over laptops, past Harper on a phone call that sounded like it was going poorly, past Jasper in his corner office, staring at three monitors like they held the answers to questions he hadn't asked yet.

The Forge was full of brilliant people running themselves into the ground. Claire's job was to make sure they learned to run differently.

She thought of Addison, sitting alone in her office, staring at a calendar that probably looked like a war zone. She thought of the flicker of hope she'd seen in the woman's eyes when Claire had said *we're going to rebuild your week from the ground up.*

It was a small spark. But Claire had learned a long time ago that the biggest transformations started with the smallest fires.

Tomorrow, they'd start building something sustainable. Something that wouldn't collapse under its own weight.

Tomorrow, they'd teach Addison Price how to see herself the way she saw everyone else.

And maybe—just maybe—they'd keep the canary singing a little longer.

*End of Chapter 14*
