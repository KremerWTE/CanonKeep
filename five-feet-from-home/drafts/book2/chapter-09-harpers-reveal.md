# Chapter 9: Harper's Reveal

**POV: Harper**

Harper Chen had negotiated multi-million dollar contracts, managed crises that could have destroyed companies, and once talked a hostile board out of a coup attempt using nothing but a PowerPoint deck and sheer force of will.

But planning a dinner party for four people was somehow more terrifying than all of that combined.

She stood in the kitchen of Oak Watch — the Barrett family estate — checking the timing on the braised short ribs for the third time in ten minutes. Elena had insisted on hosting, had insisted on cooking, had insisted that this whole thing would be "casual and low-key."

Harper was starting to suspect Elena's definition of "low-key" didn't match reality.

"Those ribs are perfect," Elena said, gliding into the kitchen with the kind of effortless grace that made Harper feel perpetually underdressed, even when she was literally wearing Armani. "Stop worrying."

"I'm not worrying."

"You've checked them three times in ten minutes. That's either worry or OCD, and I know you well enough to know which one it is."

Harper closed the oven door and turned to face Elena. The Barrett matriarch was in her element here — silk tunic over tailored pants, her silver hair caught in a elegant knot, moving through her kitchen like a dancer who'd memorized every step.

"What if Jasper doesn't like him?" Harper asked, and hated how small her voice sounded.

Elena stopped arranging herbs on a cutting board and looked at her — really looked at her, with those sharp eyes that saw too much. "What if he does?"

"That's... that's what I want."

"Is it?" Elena came closer, her expression gentler now. "Or is part of you hoping Jasper gives you an excuse to keep Ethan at arm's length?"

Harper opened her mouth to object, then closed it. Because Elena wasn't wrong. For six months, she'd kept Ethan carefully separated from her work life, from her family, from the parts of herself that felt most vulnerable. She'd told herself it was about protecting the relationship, about not moving too fast.

But the truth was simpler and more pathetic: she was scared.

"I haven't done this before," Harper said quietly. "The serious relationship thing. At least not since college, and that was... different."

"Different how?"

"I was different. Younger. More naive. I thought love was supposed to be this all-consuming thing that took over your life." She shook her head. "When it ended, it nearly destroyed me. I couldn't eat, couldn't focus, couldn't function. I swore I'd never let anyone have that kind of power over me again."

Elena was quiet for a moment. Then: "And Ethan?"

"Ethan is..." Harper struggled to find the words. "He's calm. Steady. When I'm with him, I don't feel like I'm losing myself. I feel like I'm finding parts of myself I forgot existed."

"That sounds lovely."

"It's terrifying."

Elena laughed — warm and genuine. "Oh, sweetheart. Welcome to the joy of falling in love at an age when you're smart enough to know what you have to lose."

The doorbell chimed.

Harper's heart jumped into her throat. "That's them."

"Breathe," Elena said. "Jasper's going to be on his best behavior because I'll murder him if he's not. And Ethan is going to be wonderful because from everything you've told me, he actually is wonderful."

"What if—"

"Harper. Breathe."

Harper took a breath. Then another. Through the kitchen window, she could see Jasper's car pulling up the drive. Of course he was early. Jasper was always early, as if being on time was a moral failing.

"I'll get the door," Elena said. "You get the wine."

Harper nodded and moved toward the wine rack with the mechanical precision that came from years of performing under pressure. She'd selected two bottles — a Burgundy that Jasper would appreciate and a Sancerre that Ethan had mentioned liking during one of their dinners. The kind of small detail that felt enormous now, weighted with meaning.

Voices in the foyer. Elena's warm welcome, Jasper's lower rumble in response. And then another voice — Ethan's, with that slight Pacific Northwest accent he'd never quite lost despite decades in California.

Harper checked her reflection in the darkened window. Black silk blouse, tailored gray pants, minimal jewelry. Professional armor disguised as casual dinner attire. She looked composed. Put together. Like someone who definitely wasn't having a minor panic attack about introducing her boyfriend to her surrogate family.

"Harper?" Elena called. "We're in the living room."

Harper grabbed the wine bottles and headed out.

The living room at Oak Watch was one of her favorite spaces — floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the valley, comfortable furniture arranged for conversation rather than display, bookshelves that held actual books people had actually read. Elena had made it feel like a home rather than a showpiece, which was its own kind of magic.

Jasper stood near the fireplace, wearing dark jeans and a charcoal henley that made him look more approachable than his usual suits. But his posture was pure protective older brother — arms crossed, shoulders squared, assessing the man who stood beside Elena.

Ethan Graves was fifty-two, tall and lean, with the kind of weathered handsomeness that came from a career spent outdoors. His salt-and-pepper hair was slightly too long, curling at his collar, and he wore khakis and a blue button-down with the ease of someone who'd never worried much about fashion. He held a bottle of wine — something expensive from Napa, Harper noted — and was smiling at something Elena had just said.

When he saw Harper, his whole face lit up.

That was the thing about Ethan that had undone her defenses, piece by piece over six months. The way he looked at her like she was the most interesting person in any room. The way his smile was never calculated or performed — it was just genuine pleasure at seeing her.

"Hey," he said, crossing to her. He kissed her cheek — a gesture carefully calibrated for the audience, neither too familiar nor too distant. "You look beautiful."

"You clean up well yourself," she managed.

"I wore the shirt you said didn't make me look like I was about to go camping."

"I never said that."

"You absolutely said that."

Harper laughed, and felt some of her tension ease. This was Ethan. Steady, self-deprecating Ethan, who could make her laugh even when she was nervous enough to vibrate out of her skin.

She turned to Jasper, who was watching this exchange with an expression she couldn't quite read. "Jasper, this is Ethan Graves. Ethan, my boss and favorite pain in the ass, Jasper Barrett."

Jasper's mouth twitched. "Favorite?"

"Don't push your luck." Harper looked between them. "Ethan's an environmental consultant. He works in renewable energy development."

"Specifically, I spend a lot of time arguing with NIMBYs about wind farms," Ethan said, extending his hand. "Jasper. Good to finally meet you. Harper talks about you constantly."

They shook hands. Harper watched Jasper's face, trying to read his assessment. But her boss had spent decades learning to keep his thoughts hidden, and he wasn't giving anything away.

"All good things, I hope," Jasper said.

"Mostly. Though she did tell me about the time you decided to handle a crisis negotiation yourself instead of letting the professionals do it, and nearly started an international incident."

Jasper's eyebrows rose. He looked at Harper. "You told him about Prague?"

"I tell him everything," Harper said, and realized as she said it that it was true. Somewhere in the last six months, Ethan had become the person she told things to. The person she trusted with the stories she didn't share with anyone else.

"Well," Jasper said, and something in his expression softened slightly. "In my defense, the situation in Prague was complicated."

"The situation in Prague was a shitshow because you have a savior complex," Harper said.

"Accurate," Elena agreed from across the room. "Wine, gentlemen?"

They settled into the rhythm of pre-dinner conversation. Elena poured wine and asked Ethan about his work with the kind of genuine interest that made people want to tell her their life stories. Ethan talked about a current project — a wind farm proposal in Eastern Oregon that was meeting resistance from local ranchers — with the easy enthusiasm of someone who genuinely loved what they did.

Harper watched Jasper watch Ethan. Her boss was doing his protective older brother routine, which would have been annoying if it wasn't also kind of sweet. He asked pointed questions about Ethan's background, his career trajectory, his thoughts on various current events. The kind of conversational interrogation that might have seemed casual to someone who didn't know Jasper well.

But Ethan handled it beautifully. He answered honestly, asked his own questions, and didn't seem even slightly intimidated by the fact that he was being vetted by one of the most formidable men in the private security industry.

At one point, Jasper mentioned a current BSS project — something about security protocols for a renewable energy client. Ethan leaned forward, suddenly more engaged.

"Is this the solar installation in Nevada?" he asked.

Jasper blinked. "How did you know about that?"

"I consulted on the environmental impact assessment. Small world." Ethan smiled. "Your team did excellent work, by the way. Most security firms treat environmental concerns as an afterthought, but your people actually integrated them into the planning from the start."

Something shifted in Jasper's expression. The interrogator posture eased slightly. "You worked with Martinez and Thompson?"

"Martinez, yeah. Thompson I only met once, but he seemed sharp. They both understood that you can't secure a site long-term if you're destroying the ecosystem around it. Security and sustainability have to work together."

And just like that, they were off — talking about overlapping projects, shared professional contacts, the intersection of security and environmental policy. Harper watched in amazement as Jasper's protective suspicion transformed into genuine engagement.

Elena caught her eye from across the room and winked.

Dinner was perfect, because of course it was. Elena had made braised short ribs with roasted root vegetables and some kind of potato gratin that should have been illegal. They ate in the formal dining room, which somehow felt intimate rather than stuffy, and the conversation flowed with the easy rhythm of people who were enjoying themselves.

Ethan told stories about his early career — working as a field researcher in Alaska, nearly getting eaten by a bear, learning to survive on nothing but instant ramen and spite. He was funny without trying too hard, self-deprecating without being falsely modest.

"So what made you shift from field research to consulting?" Jasper asked.

Ethan's expression flickered — something briefly shadowed before he smiled. "Honestly? I got tired of being away from civilization for months at a time. Field work is incredible when you're young and single and have no attachments. But as you get older..." He shrugged. "You start wanting different things. Stability. Community. A life that exists in one place rather than scattered across remote research stations."

"Do you miss it?" Elena asked.

"Sometimes. Mostly I miss the simplicity. When you're in the field, your problems are concrete. Is there food? Is there shelter? Is that thing coming toward you going to kill you?" He smiled. "Consulting is more complicated. The problems are abstract. Political. You're navigating human systems instead of natural ones."

"Which is harder?" Jasper asked.

"Human systems, hands down. Grizzly bears are straightforward. You know where you stand with a grizzly bear. People..." Ethan shook his head. "People are endlessly complicated."

"Is that why you like Harper?" Jasper asked, and there was something sharp underneath the casual tone. "Because she's complicated?"

Harper nearly choked on her wine. "Jasper."

"It's okay," Ethan said. He looked at Jasper directly, his expression open. "I like Harper because she's brilliant, funny, and one of the most genuinely capable people I've ever met. I like that she can manage an international crisis before breakfast and then spend an hour arguing with me about whether the Oxford comma is necessary. I like that she pretends to be tougher than she actually is, and I like that she lets me see the parts of herself she usually keeps hidden."

The room went quiet. Harper felt her face flush.

"Also," Ethan continued, his tone lighter now, "she laughs at my terrible jokes, which is really the foundation of any good relationship."

Elena laughed. Jasper's mouth twitched into something that might have been approval.

"Okay," Jasper said. "Okay, that's a good answer."

After dinner, Elena shooed them all out of the kitchen and insisted on handling cleanup alone. "You three go sit on the terrace. It's beautiful tonight."

They took their wine to the sprawling stone terrace that overlooked the valley. The sun had set an hour ago, and now the mountains were dark silhouettes against a sky full of stars. Harper pulled her cardigan tighter against the evening chill.

Jasper settled into one of the Adirondack chairs with the comfortable ease of someone who'd spent half his childhood here. Ethan took the chair beside Harper's, close enough that their armrests touched.

"Your mom is wonderful," Ethan said to Jasper.

"Technically my aunt," Jasper corrected. "But yeah. She is."

"Harper said you grew up here?"

"After my parents died, yeah. Elena took me in, gave me stability when everything else was chaos." Jasper looked out at the view. "This place saved my life."

It was the most personal thing Harper had heard Jasper say in years. The fact that he was saying it to Ethan felt significant.

"I'm sorry about your parents," Ethan said quietly.

"It was a long time ago." Jasper took a sip of his wine. "Harper said you lost your wife."

Harper tensed. They'd talked about this, she and Ethan, but it was still raw territory. His wife had died of cancer six years ago, and the grief had reshaped his entire life.

"Yeah," Ethan said. His voice was steady but softer now. "Six years ago. Ovarian cancer. By the time they caught it, it was too advanced."

"I'm sorry."

"Thank you." Ethan was quiet for a moment. "People always want to know if you get over it. The truth is, you don't. Not really. You just learn to carry it differently. The grief becomes part of you instead of the only thing about you."

Jasper nodded slowly. "Yeah. That sounds right."

They sat in companionable silence, three people who'd all lost something and learned to keep living anyway.

After a while, Ethan excused himself to use the bathroom. When he was gone, Harper turned to Jasper.

"Well?" she asked.

"Well what?"

"Don't play dumb. You've been vetting him all night. What's the verdict?"

Jasper was quiet for a long moment, looking out at the mountains. "He's good," he finally said. "Solid. Smart. Self-aware. Doesn't seem like he's trying to impress anyone."

"But?"

"No but. I like him." Jasper looked at her. "More importantly, he clearly adores you. And you let yourself be soft around him, which I didn't know you were capable of."

Harper felt something tight in her chest loosen. "Really?"

"Really." Jasper smiled — a genuine expression that transformed his usually guarded face. "I'm happy for you, Harp. You deserve this."

"I'm terrified," she admitted.

"Good. That means it matters." He reached over and squeezed her hand. "But you're allowed to have this. You know that, right? You're allowed to have a life outside of BSS. Outside of work. You're allowed to be happy."

Harper felt tears prick her eyes and blinked them back furiously. "When did you get so wise?"

"I've always been wise. You just usually don't listen."

She laughed, and then Ethan was back, settling into his chair with his easy smile.

"Did I miss anything important?" he asked.

"Just Jasper admitting I'm his favorite employee," Harper said.

"You're my only chief of staff, so technically you're both my favorite and least favorite."

"I'll take it."

They stayed on the terrace for another hour, talking and laughing and watching the stars emerge in the vast dark sky. Elena joined them eventually, bringing coffee and chocolate, settling into the conversation like she'd never left.

And Harper realized, sitting there with the three people who'd somehow become her family, that she'd been wrong about what scared her.

She wasn't afraid of losing herself in a relationship. She was afraid of letting herself want something this much. Of building a life that included someone else, of making space for happiness when she'd spent so long believing work was enough.

But looking at Ethan — the way he laughed at Jasper's dry humor, the way he listened to Elena's stories with genuine interest, the way his hand found hers in the darkness and held on — she thought maybe it was time to stop being afraid.

Maybe it was time to let herself have this.

When they finally said their goodbyes, Jasper pulled her aside in the foyer while Ethan thanked Elena for dinner.

"He's good for you," Jasper said quietly. "Don't overthink this, okay? Just let yourself be happy."

Harper hugged him — a quick, fierce embrace that spoke all the things she couldn't quite say. "Thank you. For being here. For approving. For... everything."

"Always," Jasper said.

In the car driving back to the city, Ethan reached over and took her hand.

"That went well," he said.

"You were amazing."

"I was nervous as hell. I think I talked about wind farms for twenty straight minutes at one point."

"Jasper loved it. He loves competent people who actually know what they're talking about."

"Does he love me enough not to have me disappeared if I hurt you?"

Harper laughed. "Probably not. So don't hurt me."

"Never," Ethan said, and the simple sincerity in his voice made her chest ache.

They drove in comfortable silence for a while, the highway unwinding before them, city lights growing closer.

"Can I tell you something?" Harper asked.

"Always."

"I've been keeping you separate. From my work, from my family, from the parts of my life that feel most important. I told myself it was about protecting what we have, about not moving too fast."

"And now?"

"Now I think I was just scared. Of wanting this too much. Of letting you matter."

Ethan was quiet for a moment. Then: "Do I? Matter?"

"Yes," Harper said. "You really, really do."

He lifted her hand to his lips and kissed it. "Good. Because you matter to me too. More than I thought I'd ever let anyone matter again."

They drove on into the night, toward the city and whatever came next, and Harper let herself feel it — all of it. The fear and the hope and the terrifying, wonderful possibility that maybe, finally, she was allowed to have this.

That she was allowed to be happy.
