# Chapter 17 – Boston Biotech

---

The Seaport Conference Center smelled like money and desperation, which Jasper Barrett had learned were usually the same thing.

He stood in the lobby, watching the revolving door spit out another wave of hedge fund analysts in charcoal suits. Behind him, Addison Price scrolled through her tablet with the kind of precision that made interns nervous and CEOs relieved.

"Academic stakeholders are arriving in fifteen," she said without looking up. "Dean from Harvard Medical, two professors from MIT. The investor call is at noon."

"Timeline?"

"Tight. Dr. Chen wants the activist off the board before quarterly earnings. He's worried the media narrative shifts if they wait."

Jasper nodded. They'd flown in at six this morning — both of them running on four hours of sleep and coffee strong enough to strip paint. It was the kind of pace that used to define his life. Now it felt like a sprint he was choosing instead of a rhythm he was locked into.

The difference mattered.

"You want academics or financials?" he asked.

Addison finally looked up, one eyebrow raised. "You're asking?"

"You've been running point on stakeholder mapping. Your call."

She studied him for a beat, the same way she always did when he gave her control instead of orders. Trust was still new territory between them — not because it was fragile, but because it had been tested so many times it had turned into something like steel.

"Academics," she said. "I'll handle the narrative. You work the investor defense."

"Done."

They moved through the glass atrium toward the private conference suites. Outside, Boston Harbor stretched gray and choppy under a March sky that couldn't decide if it was winter or spring. Inside, the crisis was clearer: BioVantage Solutions, a gene therapy startup valued at $1.2 billion, was under attack from an activist investor who wanted to gut the research division and flip the company for parts.

Dr. Michael Chen, the founder and CEO, had built the company on fifteen years of research. The activist wanted returns in fifteen months.

Jasper had seen this story before. The question was always the same: who controlled the narrative when the wolves came knocking?

The conference room was white and angular, the kind of space designed to make people feel like they were in the future. Dr. Chen stood at the head of the table, a slight man in his fifties with wire-rimmed glasses and the haunted look of someone who hadn't slept in a week.

"Mr. Barrett," Chen said, extending a hand. "Thank you for coming."

"Call me Jasper. This is Addison Price, my partner."

Chen's grip was firm but his palm was damp. Fear sweat. Jasper had learned to read it like a barometer.

"I don't know if you can fix this," Chen admitted.

"We don't fix things," Jasper said, sitting. "We position them. Right now, you're playing defense. We're going to put you back on offense."

Addison pulled up a slide deck on the wall-mounted screen. The first image was a news headline: ACTIVIST INVESTOR TARGETS BIOVANTAGE: 'PIVOT OR PERISH.'

"They're framing this as a choice between profits and science," Addison said. "That's the wrong frame. We're going to reframe it as a choice between short-term extraction and long-term innovation."

Chen blinked. "How?"

"Your academic partnerships," Jasper said. "Harvard, MIT, Johns Hopkins. Those aren't just collaborations — they're endorsements. When the dean of Harvard Medical stands up and says your research could save lives, that's not a business story. That's a mission story."

"And investors care about mission?" Chen asked, skeptical.

"The right ones do," Addison replied. "And the ones who don't will care about the reputational cost of being on the wrong side of a cancer cure."

Jasper watched her as she spoke. She didn't pause, didn't second-guess. Two years ago, she would've deferred to him in a room like this. Now she led it.

The student becoming the master.

It wasn't jealousy he felt — it was something closer to pride. And maybe a little vertigo.

The academic delegation arrived at 9:45.

Addison met them in the lobby while Jasper prepped the investor call upstairs. He could hear her voice drifting through the open door as she walked them down the hall — smooth, confident, the kind of tone that made people believe the plan was already working.

"Dr. Lawson, thank you for making the trip. I know your schedule is impossible."

"Anything for Michael," the dean replied. "He's one of our best."

Jasper smiled to himself. She'd done her homework. She always did.

By the time he joined them in the conference room, Addison had already walked them through the strategy: a coordinated media push positioning BioVantage as the future of gene therapy, with Harvard and MIT as the scientific validators. The dean was nodding. The professors were taking notes.

"What do you need from us?" Dr. Lawson asked.

"On-the-record statements supporting the research timeline," Addison said. "We'll coordinate press with your communications teams. And if the board meeting goes public, we'd like you available for follow-up quotes."

"Done," Lawson said without hesitation.

Jasper leaned back in his chair, watching. She'd built the entire coalition in twenty minutes. No hesitation, no wasted words. She'd become the kind of operator he used to be — sharp, fast, surgical.

Maybe sharper.

The investor call at noon was a different kind of battlefield.

Jasper sat across from three hedge fund managers in a room that smelled like leather and old cigars. These were the money men — the ones who'd been early backers of BioVantage and now had to decide if they'd side with the activist or the founder.

"Gentlemen," Jasper began. "Let's talk about what you're really worried about."

The lead investor, a man named Grayson with slicked-back hair and a Rolex that cost more than most cars, leaned forward. "We're worried about returns. Chen's been promising breakthroughs for five years. We've seen trials, not revenue."

"Fair," Jasper said. "But you didn't invest in BioVantage for quarterly dividends. You invested because you believed in the science."

"Science doesn't pay back investors," Grayson shot back.

"No," Jasper agreed. "But IP does. And right now, you're sitting on seventeen patents that could define the next generation of oncology treatment. If you flip the company now, you liquidate those patents to whoever bids highest. Maybe that's a pharma giant. Maybe it's a foreign competitor. Either way, you lose control of the upside."

Grayson didn't respond immediately. That was a good sign.

Jasper pressed. "The activist wants a fire sale. You want an exit. Those aren't the same thing. If you give Chen two more years and the Phase III trial succeeds, you'll 10x your position. If you sell now, you'll double it — maybe."

"What if the trial fails?" another investor asked.

"Then you're in the same place you'd be if you sold today," Jasper said. "But if it succeeds and you're not holding the bag, you'll spend the rest of your career explaining why you let someone else win the jackpot."

Grayson sat back, arms crossed. "You're good at this."

"It's what you're paying for."

By 2 p.m., the coalition was forming. The academics were drafting statements. The investors were wavering. Dr. Chen looked like he might actually sleep that night.

Jasper found Addison in the break room, staring out the window at the harbor.

"We're winning," he said.

"I know." She didn't turn around. "The activist will counter. Probably tonight."

"We'll be ready."

She nodded, still watching the water. "You ever think about what happens when we're not needed anymore?"

It was the kind of question she never used to ask. Jasper leaned against the counter, considering it.

"You think we're not needed?"

"I think we're really good at teaching other people how to do this," she said. "At some point, they won't need us in the room."

"That's the goal, isn't it?"

She turned, finally meeting his eyes. "Is it?"

Jasper didn't have an easy answer. For most of his career, being essential was the point. If clients could solve their own crises, he didn't have a job. But somewhere along the way — maybe when Elena got sick, maybe when Grace asked if he was on their team — he'd started to realize that being needed wasn't the same as being valuable.

"I think the goal is to build something that works without us," he said slowly. "And then choose when we show up."

Addison smiled, but it was small and thoughtful. "You've changed."

"So have you."

"I know." She picked up her tablet. "That's what scares me."

Before he could ask what she meant, her phone buzzed. She glanced at the screen, and her expression shifted — just a flicker, but Jasper had known her long enough to catch it.

"Hawk?" he asked.

"Yeah. Checking in."

"You should call him back."

She looked at him, surprised. "We're in the middle of—"

"We're ahead of schedule," Jasper said. "The board meeting isn't until tomorrow morning. Call him."

She hesitated, and Jasper could see the old instinct still there — the reflex to push personal things into the margins, to keep work at the center. He'd lived that way for years. He knew what it cost.

"Addison," he said quietly. "Call him."

She exhaled, nodded, and stepped into the hallway.

Jasper turned back to the window. The harbor was still gray, still choppy. But the storm wasn't coming. It was passing.

That night, they worked from the hotel business center — Addison on messaging, Jasper on financial models. Around nine, she looked up from her laptop.

"The academic statements are locked," she said. "Harvard's releasing theirs at 6 a.m."

"Good. That'll set the tone before the activists can spin."

She closed the laptop. "Jasper?"

"Yeah?"

"Thanks. For earlier. For telling me to call Hawk."

He nodded. "You would've done the same for me."

"Maybe." She smiled. "Or maybe I would've told you to suck it up and finish the deck."

"That too."

They both laughed, and for a moment the crisis felt smaller — not because it wasn't real, but because it wasn't the only thing that was.

At eleven, Jasper called Elena.

"How's Boston?" she asked.

"Cold. Productive. We're close."

"Good." He could hear Grace in the background, asking if it was Daddy. "Grace wants to know if you're coming home tomorrow."

"Probably. Board meeting's in the morning. If it goes well, I'll catch the afternoon flight."

"And if it doesn't?"

Jasper paused. Two years ago, he would've said he'd stay as long as it took. Now he knew better.

"I'll still catch the afternoon flight," he said. "We've built the plan. They can execute it."

Elena was quiet for a beat. Then: "I'm proud of you."

"Yeah?"

"Yeah."

Grace's voice broke through: "Daddy, are you saving the day?"

Jasper smiled. "Trying to, sweetheart. But I'm coming home either way."

"Promise?"

"Promise."

After he hung up, he sat in the silence of the hotel room, staring at the city lights. Boston looked different from forty floors up — smaller, more manageable. Like maybe the world didn't need him to hold it together.

Maybe it just needed him to show up, do the work, and go home.

Addison knocked on his door around midnight.

"Can't sleep?" he asked.

"Thinking," she said, stepping inside. She had her laptop under one arm, hair pulled back in a messy bun. "I ran the investor models three more times. I think we're missing something."

"What?"

"The activist's leverage. He's pushing too hard for someone who only holds eight percent. There's another player."

Jasper frowned. "You think he's got a partner?"

"Or a buyer lined up. Someone who wants the IP but doesn't want to fight for it."

"Run the ownership filings," Jasper said, already pulling up his own screen. "See who's been accumulating shares quietly."

They worked until two in the morning, cross-referencing SEC filings and shell company disclosures. By the time they found it, the coffee was cold and the sun was starting to rise over the harbor.

"There," Addison said, pointing at the screen. "Meridian Capital. They've been buying through three different LLCs. If you add it up—"

"They're at fourteen percent," Jasper finished. "Combine that with the activist, and they've got enough to force a board vote."

"So what do we do?"

Jasper sat back, thinking. The old version of him would've panicked, would've called Dr. Chen at 2 a.m. and demanded an emergency strategy session. But the new version — the one who'd learned to see past the five feet — knew that panic was just adrenaline pretending to be urgency.

"We call it out," he said. "In the board meeting. We expose Meridian before they can make their move. If the other investors know there's a shadow buyer, they'll circle the wagons."

Addison smiled. "That's good."

"It's risky."

"It's perfect."

She closed her laptop and stood. "Get some sleep, Jasper. Tomorrow's going to be long."

"You too."

She paused at the door. "You know what's funny?"

"What?"

"Two years ago, I would've been terrified to call this on my own. Now I feel like I could run this whole thing without you."

Jasper smiled. "You probably could."

"But I don't want to," she said. "That's the part that surprised me."

After she left, Jasper lay in the dark, staring at the ceiling. She was right. She was outpacing him in some areas — sharper, faster, more confident. And instead of feeling threatened, he felt relieved.

He didn't need to be the best in the room anymore.

He just needed to be in the right rooms.

The board meeting was at nine.

By 8:45, the room was packed — investors, board members, academic observers, legal counsel. Dr. Chen sat at the head of the table, looking pale but determined. The activist investor, a man named Trask with a reputation for hostile takeovers, sat across from him with the smug confidence of someone who thought he'd already won.

Jasper and Addison stood at the back, watching.

"Ready?" she whispered.

"Always."

Dr. Chen opened with a presentation on the Phase III trial data. It was strong — better than expected, actually — but Trask interrupted halfway through.

"With all due respect, Dr. Chen, timelines don't pay dividends. We've heard these promises before."

"Mr. Trask," Addison said, stepping forward. "May I?"

Trask looked annoyed, but nodded.

Addison pulled up a slide on the screen — a breakdown of recent share acquisitions by Meridian Capital.

"For those who aren't aware," she said calmly, "Meridian Capital has been quietly accumulating shares in BioVantage over the past six months. Combined with Mr. Trask's position, they now control enough votes to force a sale."

The room erupted. Investors started whispering. Board members looked confused.

Trask's face went red. "That's speculative—"

"It's public record," Addison cut in. "And if you'd like, I can walk everyone through the LLCs Meridian used to hide the purchases."

Jasper watched the room shift. The investors who'd been on the fence were now looking at Trask like he'd tried to pick their pockets. And maybe he had.

One of the hedge fund managers — Grayson — stood. "If this is true, then this isn't about returns. It's about a backroom deal."

"I assure you—" Trask began.

"Save it," Grayson said. "I'm voting with Chen."

One by one, the other investors followed. By the time the vote was called, Trask had lost.

Dr. Chen looked like he might cry.

Afterward, in the hallway, Chen shook Jasper's hand so hard it hurt.

"Thank you," he said. "I don't know how to—"

"You don't have to," Jasper said. "Just keep doing the work."

Addison stood beside him, arms crossed, watching the investors file out. She looked satisfied but not smug. Confident but not cocky.

"Good work," Jasper said.

"You too."

They walked out into the cold Boston morning, the harbor stretching gray and endless in front of them.

"You catching the afternoon flight?" Addison asked.

"Yeah. You?"

"I think I'll stay another day. Hawk's flying in tonight. I haven't seen him in two weeks."

Jasper smiled. "Good. You should."

She looked at him, something unspoken passing between them.

"We're getting better at this," she said.

"At crisis management?"

"At life."

Jasper thought about that as they walked toward the car. She was right. They were better — not because they were working less, but because they were choosing more carefully. Choosing when to fight, when to walk away, when to let someone else carry the weight.

The student becoming the master.

Not in spite of him.

Because of him.

And maybe that was the whole point.
