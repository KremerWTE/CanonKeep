# Chapter 20 – Kendra's History

---

The first thing Kendra remembered was falling.

Not the bad kind of falling — the kind where you hit the ground and break something. The good kind. The kind where you launched yourself into the air and trusted gravity to catch you.

She was five years old, in her mother's dance studio in Amarillo, Texas. The studio smelled like rosin and sweat, and the mirrors made the room feel bigger than it was. Her mother stood at the barre, calling out counts in that voice she used when she was teaching — firm but kind.

"Five, six, seven, eight—"

Kendra tried to follow along, but her legs were too short, her arms too clumsy. She kept falling out of time.

Her mother didn't get frustrated. She just knelt down, adjusted Kendra's stance, and said, "Try again."

So Kendra tried again.

And again.

And again.

That was the rhythm of her childhood: Try again. Fall. Get up. Try again.

By the time she was seven, she was good. Not just good for her age — good, period. She could do backflips off the beam, could stick landings that made the other girls jealous. Her mother signed her up for gymnastics at the local gym, and within a year, Kendra was on the junior elite track.

Her dad worked in oil services, which meant he was gone a lot — sometimes weeks at a time, chasing jobs in West Texas or New Mexico. But when he came home, he'd sit in the bleachers and watch her practice, arms crossed, smiling.

"That's my girl," he'd say afterward, ruffling her hair. "You're gonna be great."

Kendra believed him.

Because back then, she believed everything.

By twelve, Kendra was training six days a week.

School, gym, home. That was the cycle. She didn't have time for friends, didn't have time for sleepovers or mall trips or whatever normal kids did. She had routines to perfect, skills to master, competitions to win.

And she was winning.

Regional championships. State qualifiers. Nationals, where she placed seventh in the all-around. Her coach said she had a real shot at the Olympic trials if she kept it up.

"You've got the talent," Coach Marissa said. "You just need to want it."

Kendra wanted it. She wanted it so badly it scared her.

But wanting something and keeping it were different things.

She learned that the hard way.

High school was when boys started noticing her.

Not because she dressed up or wore makeup or tried. She didn't. But there was something about being good at something — being the best at something — that made people pay attention.

Marcus was first.

He was a freshman like her, tall and sweet and kind of boring. He asked her to homecoming, and she said yes because she didn't know how to say no. They dated for six months, and the whole time, Kendra felt like she was watching someone else's life.

He held her hand in the hallways. Texted her good morning. Showed up to her meets with flowers.

And she felt nothing.

"I don't get it," her best friend, Jenna, said. "He's perfect. What's the problem?"

"I don't know," Kendra admitted. "I just... don't feel it."

So she broke up with him. He cried. She felt guilty. And then she moved on.

Junior year, there was Tyler.

Tyler was different. He played soccer, had a motorcycle, made her laugh in ways Marcus never could. He was exciting. Dangerous, even.

Kendra fell hard.

For three months, it was perfect. They snuck out after practice, rode his motorcycle out to the edge of town where the lights disappeared and the stars took over. He kissed her like she was the only thing that mattered.

And then she found out he was texting another girl.

"It's not like that," Tyler said when she confronted him. "She's just a friend."

But Kendra had seen the messages. The flirting. The lies.

She broke up with him in the school parking lot, and he didn't even fight for her.

After that, Kendra stopped falling.

The injury happened during senior year.

She was practicing vault — her best event. She'd done the skill a thousand times. Roundoff, back handspring, double twist. It was muscle memory.

But this time, her foot slipped on the springboard. Just a fraction of an inch. Just enough.

She came down wrong.

The pain was instant and blinding. She heard something pop — her knee, her ligament, she didn't know — and then she was on the floor, gasping.

Coach Marissa was there in seconds. "Don't move. Don't move."

The diagnosis was a torn ACL.

Surgery. Six months of rehab. And even then, the doctor said, she might not get back to elite level.

"You're young," he said, like that made it better. "You'll recover."

But Kendra knew. Even if her knee healed, something else had broken.

The dream was over.

College was supposed to be a fresh start.

She got a full gymnastics scholarship to UCLA — not for elite training, but for the NCAA team. It was something. Not what she'd dreamed of, but something.

Her freshman year was a blur. New city, new team, new expectations. She worked hard, pushed through the pain in her knee, tried to prove she was still good enough.

But her heart wasn't in it anymore.

She missed routines she used to land in her sleep. Fell off the beam during competitions. Her teammates were supportive, but Kendra could see the disappointment in their eyes.

She wasn't great anymore. She was just good.

And good wasn't enough.

Rehab was where she found CrossFit.

The physical therapist suggested it as a way to rebuild strength. "Low impact, high intensity," he said. "Good for athletes transitioning out of sport."

Kendra hated the phrase "transitioning out." It made it sound like she'd chosen to quit.

But she went anyway.

The gym was nothing like the studios she grew up in. No mirrors, no mats, just concrete floors and barbells and people who looked like they could break her in half.

She loved it immediately.

CrossFit was different. It wasn't about perfection. It wasn't about sticking landings or nailing routines. It was about pushing yourself until you couldn't, and then pushing a little more.

It was hard and messy and painful.

And for the first time in years, Kendra felt alive.

By sophomore year, she'd quit the gymnastics team.

It wasn't an easy decision. Her coach tried to talk her out of it. Her parents were confused. But Kendra knew she couldn't keep pretending.

"I'm done," she told Coach Marissa. "I'm sorry."

"You don't have to be sorry," Marissa said. "You gave it everything. That's all anyone can ask."

Kendra wasn't sure she believed that. But she appreciated it anyway.

She threw herself into CrossFit. Competed in local tournaments. Placed well enough to get noticed. By junior year, she was training for regionals.

The gym became her relationship. The only one that didn't burn her.

Boys came and went.

There was Jake, a guy from her econ class. Sweet, smart, safe. They dated for a semester, and then he transferred schools.

There was Ryan, a CrossFit athlete she met at a competition. Intense, driven, hot as hell. They hooked up a few times, and then he ghosted her.

There was Caleb, who she almost loved. Almost.

But every time she got close, she felt the walls go up. The reflex to pull back, to protect herself, to keep things casual.

"You're scared," Jenna told her during spring break senior year. "That's all it is."

"Of what?"

"Of getting hurt again."

Kendra didn't argue. Because Jenna was right.

After Marcus, after Tyler, after every guy who'd left or lied or just faded away, Kendra had learned to keep her distance.

It was easier that way.

Safer.

After college, Kendra moved to Charlotte.

She didn't have a plan, exactly. Just a vague idea that she wanted to coach, wanted to build something. A friend from UCLA had moved to Charlotte and opened a CrossFit gym, and she said Kendra could work there while she figured things out.

So Kendra packed her car and drove east.

Charlotte was hotter than she expected. More sprawling. More expensive. But it had a rhythm she liked — fast but not frantic, busy but not overwhelming.

She got a job at the gym. Started coaching classes. Started competing again.

And slowly, she built a life.

It wasn't the life she'd imagined when she was twelve and dreaming of the Olympics. But it was hers.

That was when she met Chris Donnelly.

He walked into the gym one morning, looking like he'd never touched a barbell in his life. Tall, lean, a little awkward. He signed up for the intro class, and Kendra was his coach.

"You ever done this before?" she asked.

"Nope," he said, grinning. "But I figured it was time to stop being useless."

She liked him immediately.

Not in a romantic way — not at first. But there was something about him. He was steady. Calm. He didn't push, didn't perform, didn't try to impress her.

He just showed up.

Every class. Every week. Slowly getting stronger, slowly getting better.

And somewhere along the way, Kendra started noticing him differently.

The first time they kissed was after a Saturday morning workout.

She'd stayed late to clean up, and Chris had stayed to help. They were wiping down equipment, talking about nothing in particular, when he stopped and looked at her.

"Can I ask you something?" he said.

"Sure."

"Why are you still single?"

Kendra froze. "That's a hell of a question."

"I know," he said, smiling. "But seriously. You're smart, you're strong, you're—" He gestured vaguely. "You. Why?"

She didn't have a good answer. So she said, "Because I'm good at walls."

"Yeah," Chris said. "I noticed."

"And that doesn't bother you?"

"Not yet."

He kissed her then, slow and careful, like he was asking permission.

And Kendra, for once, didn't pull away.

They dated for a year before things got complicated.

Chris was good. Too good, maybe. He didn't push her, didn't demand more than she could give. He was patient with her walls, patient with her silences.

And Kendra started to think, Maybe this is it. Maybe this is what love looks like when it's not burning.

But then Addison came back.

Kendra had met Addison years ago, back when Addison was still climbing the ladder at Barrett Security Solutions. They'd crossed paths at a charity event — something Elena had organized — and there'd been an instant spark.

Not just attraction. Something deeper.

They'd hooked up once. Maybe twice. Kendra couldn't remember. What she remembered was the way Addison looked at her — like she saw past the walls, past the armor, past the performance.

Like she saw Kendra.

And then Addison had disappeared into her work, into her life, and Kendra had moved on.

Or tried to.

But now Addison was back. Not physically — not yet. But in Kendra's head, in her chest, in the spaces Chris didn't fill.

And Kendra didn't know what to do with that.

One night, Chris asked her point-blank: "Are you happy?"

They were sitting on his couch, watching a movie neither of them cared about.

"What kind of question is that?" Kendra said.

"An honest one."

She turned to look at him. His face was open, earnest. He wasn't picking a fight. He was asking because he wanted to know.

"I don't know," Kendra admitted. "I think so."

"You think so?"

"I don't know what happy is supposed to feel like," she said. "I know what exciting feels like. I know what safe feels like. But happy? I'm not sure."

Chris nodded slowly. "Do I make you happy?"

Kendra wanted to say yes. She wanted to tell him that he was everything she needed, that he was enough, that she wasn't still thinking about Addison.

But she couldn't lie. Not to him.

"You make me feel safe," she said. "And that's not nothing."

"But it's not happy."

"I didn't say that."

"You didn't have to."

They broke up two weeks later.

It wasn't dramatic. No screaming, no accusations. Just a quiet conversation in his kitchen where they both admitted the truth: Kendra wasn't ready. And Chris deserved someone who was.

"I'm sorry," Kendra said.

"Me too," Chris replied.

And that was it.

After Chris, Kendra threw herself back into the gym.

She coached harder, trained harder, competed harder. She made regionals that year, placed in the top ten. It should've felt like winning.

But it felt like running.

One night, she sat alone in her apartment, staring at her phone. She had Addison's number. Had never deleted it.

She typed a message: *Hey. You around?*

Deleted it. Typed again: *I've been thinking about you.*

Deleted it.

Finally, she just wrote: *Miss you.*

She stared at the message for ten minutes before deleting it too.

Because what was the point? Addison had her own life. Her own walls. They were the same, her and Addison — two people who'd learned to keep everyone at arm's length.

And maybe that's why they kept circling back to each other.

Because they understood.

Now, sitting in her apartment in Charlotte, Kendra thought about all of it.

The gymnastics studio in Amarillo. The injury that ended her dream. The boys who came easy but never stayed. The gym that became her relationship. Chris, who was steady and kind and not enough. Addison, who was chaos and fire and too much.

She didn't know what she wanted.

She just knew she was tired of walls.

Tired of protecting herself from things that might hurt. Tired of running from things that might matter.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Addison: *You free tonight?*

Kendra stared at the message. She could say no. Could keep the walls up, keep herself safe.

Or she could try again.

One more time.

She typed back: *Yeah. Come over.*

And then she waited.

Because maybe the first thing she remembered was falling.

But the second thing was getting up.

And trying again.
