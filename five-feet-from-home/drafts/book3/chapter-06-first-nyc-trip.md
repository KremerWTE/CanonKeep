# Chapter 6: First NYC Trip

**POV:** Addison
**Location:** NYC
**Book:** Book 3 - The Expansion

---

The Gulfstream levels off at thirty-eight thousand feet, and Addison finally lets herself exhale.

She's flown private before—BSS clients expect it, and Jasper's never been one to cut corners on logistics—but this is different. This isn't a support role. This isn't her sitting across from Jasper while he runs the briefing and she takes notes. This is her case. Her client. Her lead.

Jasper's sitting across the aisle, laptop open, reviewing something that has nothing to do with the hedge fund case they're flying to New York to handle. He's giving her space. Deliberately. She can feel it in the way he's positioned himself—close enough to be available, far enough to let her own the moment.

It's a strange feeling. Not unwelcome, just… new.

Addison opens the case file on her tablet, even though she's already memorized it. Kenneth Marsh, founder and managing partner of Helix Capital, accused of securities fraud by the SEC. The allegations are ugly: inflated asset values, misleading investor reports, potential Ponzi-style payouts. Marsh denies everything, claims he's being targeted for political reasons, and hired BSS three days ago after his general counsel recommended Jasper personally.

Except Jasper didn't take the case. He assigned it to Addison.

"You're ready," he'd said when he called her into his office. "And Marsh needs someone who can move fast. That's you."

She'd wanted to argue. Wanted to say that Marsh specifically asked for him. But Jasper had already made the decision, and arguing would've been a waste of both their time.

So here she is. First chair.

She scrolls through the SEC filing again, flagging the weakest points in their argument. There's a pattern here—she can see it in the transaction dates, the reporting windows, the way certain disclosures were timed. If she can isolate the pattern, she can build a counter-narrative. Show that Marsh was sloppy, not criminal. That his firm's accounting was disorganized, not fraudulent.

It's a narrow path, but it's there.

"Find something?"

She looks up. Jasper's closed his laptop and is watching her, one eyebrow raised.

"Maybe," she says. "The SEC's timeline doesn't account for Helix's internal reporting delays. If we can prove the delays were structural—baked into their systems—then the so-called 'misleading' reports weren't intentional. They were just late."

Jasper nods slowly. "That's good. What do you need to prove it?"

"Access to their internal systems. IT logs, email timestamps, workflow documentation. I'll need to sit down with their COO and their head of compliance."

"Marsh won't like that. He'll think you're digging for more problems."

"Probably," Addison agrees. "But if he wants us to win this, he'll cooperate."

Jasper's mouth quirks into a small smile. "You're not wrong."

She goes back to her tablet, but she can still feel him looking at her. It's not intrusive—Jasper's never intrusive—but it's present. Assessing.

"What?" she asks without looking up.

"Nothing," Jasper says. "Just thinking."

"About?"

"About the fact that two years ago, you would've asked me what to do next."

Addison glances at him. His expression is calm, maybe a little proud. "Two years ago, I didn't know what I was doing."

"You knew more than you thought."

She doesn't respond to that. It's probably true, but it doesn't feel true. Two years ago, she was good at execution. She could take Jasper's strategy and run with it, make it happen, manage the details. But she wasn't the one building the strategy. She wasn't the one seeing the whole board.

Now she is.

Jasper leans back in his seat, folding his arms. "You've changed, Addie."

The nickname—soft, familiar—catches her off guard. He doesn't use it often. Not at work. "Yeah?" she says, keeping her tone light. "Good changed or bad changed?"

"Good." He says it without hesitation. "You're sharper. More confident. You trust your instincts now."

"I had to," she says simply.

It's the truth. After her collapse in Miami—after the hospital, the recovery, the reckoning with the fact that she'd nearly killed herself chasing someone else's approval—she'd had to rebuild. Not just her health, but her sense of self. What she wanted. What she was capable of. Who she was when she wasn't performing for Jasper or the clients or the invisible judges in her own head.

Hawk had helped. So had Elena. And Kendra, in her own way.

But mostly, Addison had helped herself.

Jasper's quiet for a moment, then he says, "I'm glad you did."

She looks at him fully now, tablet forgotten. There's something in his voice—something that sounds almost like regret. "You didn't push me into this, Jasper. You know that, right?"

"I know," he says. "But I didn't stop it, either. I saw you running yourself into the ground, and I told myself it was your choice. That you were built for it." He shakes his head. "I was wrong."

"You weren't wrong," Addison says. "I was built for it. I just wasn't built to do it alone."

Jasper holds her gaze for a beat, then nods. "Fair enough."

The moment hangs between them, not uncomfortable but weighted. Then Jasper opens his laptop again, and the conversation shifts back into neutral territory—logistics, schedules, the hotel in Midtown where they're staying.

Addison goes back to her case file, but something in her chest has loosened.

This trip isn't just about the case. It's proof. Proof that she's not Jasper's protégé anymore. She's his partner.

And he knows it.

---

The offices of Helix Capital are on the forty-second floor of a glass tower in Midtown, all sleek surfaces and floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking the grid of Manhattan. Kenneth Marsh meets them in the conference room, flanked by his general counsel and his COO. He's in his mid-fifties, silver-haired, with the kind of polished confidence that comes from decades of making money and getting away with it.

"Mr. Barrett," Marsh says, extending a hand. "Thank you for coming."

Jasper shakes it, then gestures to Addison. "Kenneth Marsh, this is Addison Price. She's leading your case."

Marsh's smile falters for half a second—just long enough for Addison to notice—but he recovers smoothly. "Ms. Price. A pleasure."

"Mr. Marsh," Addison says, shaking his hand. Her grip is firm, her expression neutral. She's done this dance before. The client who expected Jasper and got her instead. The momentary doubt. The decision to either trust her or waste everyone's time second-guessing.

She's not interested in convincing him. Either he'll see the value, or he won't.

They sit. Addison opens her tablet and pulls up the SEC filing. "Let's start with the basics. The SEC is alleging that Helix inflated asset values in your quarterly investor reports for eighteen months. They're claiming this was intentional and designed to attract new capital while concealing losses. Is that accurate?"

Marsh frowns. "It's not that simple."

"Then make it simple," Addison says.

His general counsel—a thin, nervous man named Aaron Fielding—cuts in. "Our asset valuations were based on third-party appraisals. If those appraisals were inflated, it wasn't our fault."

Addison doesn't look at him. She keeps her eyes on Marsh. "Were the appraisals inflated?"

Marsh hesitates. "Some of them may have been… optimistic."

"That's not an answer."

Jasper, sitting beside her, says nothing. He's letting her run this. She can feel his stillness, his trust.

Marsh exhales slowly. "Fine. Yes. Some of the appraisals were higher than we expected. But we didn't commission them with the intent to deceive. We relied on the appraisers' expertise."

"Did you question the high valuations?" Addison asks.

"No."

"Why not?"

Marsh's jaw tightens. "Because we were growing. Fast. The valuations aligned with our growth trajectory."

"Or they aligned with what you wanted to believe," Addison says. She's not being cruel. She's being precise. "If we're going to defend this, Mr. Marsh, I need the truth. Not the version you've been telling yourself. The truth."

Marsh stares at her for a long moment. Then, slowly, he nods. "Alright. The truth is, we didn't look too closely because we didn't want to find a problem. We were raising capital. We needed the numbers to look good."

"That's negligence," Addison says. "Not fraud. That's what we'll argue."

Fielding blinks. "You can do that?"

"If the evidence supports it." Addison looks at the COO—a woman in her forties named Linda Ortega, who's been silent so far. "Ms. Ortega, I need access to your internal reporting systems. Email logs, workflow timestamps, anything that shows how long it took for appraisals to make it into investor reports."

Ortega glances at Marsh, who nods. "We can arrange that," she says.

"Good. I also need a list of everyone involved in the valuation process. Appraisers, analysts, compliance officers. I need to know who touched these numbers and when."

"That's a lot of people," Ortega says.

"Then you'd better get started."

Marsh leans back in his chair, folding his arms. He's watching Addison now with something close to respect. "You don't waste time, do you, Ms. Price?"

"No," Addison says. "I don't."

The meeting runs another ninety minutes. By the end, Addison has a clear picture of what happened: Marsh's firm grew too fast, relied too heavily on external appraisals, and failed to implement internal controls that might've caught the inflated numbers. It's bad management. It's reckless. But it's not fraud.

If she can prove that, they'll win.

When they finally leave the building, stepping out into the cold February air, Jasper glances at her. "That was well done."

"Thanks," Addison says. She's exhausted, but in a good way. The kind of tired that comes from doing the work and doing it well.

"You know he didn't trust you at first," Jasper says.

"I know."

"And you didn't care."

"No," Addison agrees. "I didn't."

Jasper smiles. "Good."

They walk in silence for a block, then Jasper says, "Dinner tonight? There's a place in the West Village I think you'd like."

Addison's about to say yes when her phone buzzes. She pulls it out and sees a text from Elena.

**Elena:** How's New York? Jasper behaving himself?

Addison smiles and types back:

**Addison:** So far. I'll keep you posted.

She looks up at Jasper. "Dinner sounds good. But not too late. I need to review those IT logs tonight."

"Understood," Jasper says. "I'll make a reservation for seven."

---

The restaurant is small, tucked into a corner of a cobblestone street in the West Village. It's the kind of place that doesn't advertise, doesn't need to. The food is French, the wine list is absurd, and the lighting is low enough that Addison can almost forget they're talking about work.

Almost.

"You think Marsh will hold up under deposition?" Jasper asks, cutting into his steak.

"If we prep him right, yeah," Addison says. She's working on a piece of roasted fish, delicate and perfectly seasoned. "He's not stupid. He just got cocky."

"Cocky is prosecutable," Jasper points out.

"Only if we let it look intentional. If we frame it as negligence, the SEC has a much weaker case. They'd have to prove intent, and the evidence doesn't support that."

Jasper nods. "You've thought this through."

"I have."

He sets down his fork and looks at her. "You know, two years ago, I would've been the one walking Marsh through that argument. You would've been taking notes."

"And now I'm not," Addison says.

"No," Jasper agrees. "Now you're leading. And you're better at it than I expected."

Addison raises an eyebrow. "Better than you expected? Should I be offended?"

Jasper laughs. "No. I always knew you were capable. I just didn't know you'd be this good this fast."

She takes a sip of wine, letting the compliment settle. It feels different coming from Jasper now. Less like validation, more like recognition. "I had good teachers," she says.

"You had yourself," Jasper counters. "We just gave you the tools."

The conversation shifts after that—away from the case, away from work. They talk about Elena, about Grace's upcoming recital, about Kendra's Open workout and how she placed in the top hundred. Jasper's proud of Kendra in that distant, fraternal way he has with people he respects. Addison doesn't mention that she's been texting Kendra more often lately. That there's something growing between them again, something she's not ready to name yet.

When they finish dinner and step back out into the cold, Jasper flags a cab. "You heading back to the hotel?"

"Yeah," Addison says. "I've got work to do."

He smiles. "Of course you do."

The cab pulls up, and she climbs in. Before she closes the door, Jasper leans down. "You did good today, Addie. Really good."

"Thanks," she says. And she means it.

As the cab pulls away, she watches him through the window—standing on the curb, hands in his pockets, the city lights reflecting off the glass towers behind him. For the first time in a long time, she doesn't feel like she's chasing him.

She feels like they're walking side by side.

---

Back at the hotel, Addison kicks off her heels and pulls out her laptop. The IT logs from Helix Capital are waiting in her inbox, a dense spreadsheet full of timestamps and email threads. She spends two hours combing through them, building a timeline, cross-referencing the dates with the SEC's allegations.

By the time she finishes, it's past midnight. But she has what she needs.

She closes the laptop and picks up her phone. There's a text from Hawk, sent an hour ago.

**Hawk:** How's New York?

**Addison:** Productive. Case is solid. Jasper's being… Jasper.

**Hawk:** That good or bad?

**Addison:** Good. Different, but good.

**Hawk:** You sound tired.

**Addison:** I am.

**Hawk:** Get some sleep, War Goddess. You've earned it.

She smiles at the nickname, sets the phone down, and climbs into bed. The city hums outside her window, restless and alive. She closes her eyes and lets the exhaustion pull her under.

Tomorrow, she'll keep building the case. Tomorrow, she'll prove that she's not just Jasper's right hand.

She's her own force.

And she's just getting started.

**End of Chapter 6**
