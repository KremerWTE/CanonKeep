# Chapter 14: DC Crisis

**POV: Addison Clarke**

The war room on K Street smelled like cold coffee and desperation. Addison sat at the head of a conference table designed to seat twenty, surrounded by lawyers in shirtsleeves and political consultants with thousand-yard stares. Outside the windows, Washington DC sparkled in the late afternoon sun, the Capitol dome visible in the distance like a warning.

They'd been at this for six hours. Day two of three. And they were losing.

"Let's run it again," Marcus Chen said. He was the senior partner from Covington and Burling, the white-shoe law firm Whitmore Industries kept on retainer for exactly this kind of crisis. "Congressman Barfield is going to come at you hard on the cost overruns."

"The cost overruns were due to specification changes requested by the Pentagon," Jasper said for the fifteenth time. His tie was loosened, sleeves rolled up. He looked exhausted. "We documented everything."

"Documentation doesn't matter if he makes you look incompetent on C-SPAN." Marcus pulled up a slide showing projected media coverage. "He's already leaked to the Post. Front page tomorrow: 'Defense Contractor Bilks Taxpayers for Millions.'"

"We didn't bilk anyone," Addison said quietly.

Every head in the room turned toward her. She'd been mostly silent during these prep sessions, taking notes, observing. Learning the rhythms of crisis management at this level.

But she'd seen something the lawyers were missing.

"Ms. Clarke?" Marcus's tone was professionally polite but dismissive. She was Jasper's girlfriend. His assistant. Not a player in this arena.

"Congressman Barfield isn't actually concerned about cost overruns," Addison said. "He's concerned about losing the Meridian contract to a North Carolina company instead of a California company. He has three major defense contractors in his district. This inquiry isn't about oversight. It's about protectionism."

The room went quiet.

"That's quite a theory," Marcus said.

"It's not a theory. It's in his donor list." Addison pulled out her tablet, navigated to a spreadsheet she'd built on the flight from Charlotte. "Northrop, Lockheed, and Boeing have contributed $1.7 million to his campaigns over the last six years. And all three of them bid on the Meridian contract."

"How did you—" One of the junior associates looked shocked.

"FEC disclosures. Public record." Addison turned the tablet around so everyone could see. "He's not going after us because we did anything wrong. He's going after us because we beat his donors."

Jasper was staring at her with an expression somewhere between awe and concern. They'd talked strategy during the flight, but she hadn't mentioned she'd been building a complete opposition research file on every member of the oversight committee.

Marcus leaned forward. "Even if that's true — and I'm not saying it isn't — it doesn't change our approach. We still need to defend the contract terms."

"No," Addison said. "We need to reframe the entire conversation."

"To what?"

"To national security. To manufacturing jobs in swing states. To why California-based contractors weren't competitive on timeline or innovation." She stood up, moved to the whiteboard at the front of the room. "Congressman Barfield wants to make this about fiscal responsibility. We make it about American competitiveness."

She started writing, organizing her thoughts in real-time.

"The Meridian contract is for next-generation communication systems. Cutting-edge technology. We won because we proposed something genuinely innovative — not just an incremental improvement on existing platforms." She turned back to the room. "That's the story. That's what plays on the evening news."

"The problem," Marcus said slowly, "is that Congressman Barfield controls the narrative. He sets the questions. We're playing defense."

"Not if we get ahead of it." Addison's mind was racing now, seeing the angles. "What if we brief the Armed Services Committee before the hearing? Show them the technology. Demonstrate why it matters. Let them draw their own conclusions about whether Barfield is asking the right questions."

One of the political consultants — Sarah something, Addison hadn't caught her last name — sat up straighter. "That's... actually not terrible. If we can get Senator Whitehouse on board, she could provide cover."

"Senator Whitehouse voted against the defense appropriations bill," another consultant pointed out.

"Because of waste," Addison countered. "But she's on record supporting innovation in military technology. If we position this as cutting waste through better technology, she might be persuaded."

Marcus was watching her with new attention. "You've thought about this."

"I've thought about everything." Addison met his eyes. "That's my job."

The room shifted. She felt it. The subtle recalibration as fifteen lawyers and consultants realized she wasn't just Jasper's plus-one.

"All right," Marcus said. "Let's game it out. Sarah, can you reach out to Whitehouse's office? Gauge interest in a briefing?"

"On it." Sarah was already typing on her phone.

"Addison, I want that donor analysis expanded. Every committee member. Show me patterns." Marcus turned to Jasper. "And you need to be prepared for Barfield to go personal. He's not above it."

"Personal how?" Jasper asked.

"You're thirty-three. You run a division of your father's company. He could paint you as a nepotism hire who's in over his head."

Addison felt Jasper tense beside her. This was his weak spot — the constant awareness that his position came with an asterisk. Son of the founder. Brother of the heir apparent. Never quite legitimate on his own terms.

"He could try," Addison said. "But the project metrics don't support that narrative. Jasper's division has outperformed projections for seven consecutive quarters. The Meridian bid won on technical merit — it was evaluated by Pentagon engineers, not procurement officers who could be influenced by politics."

"They'll still try to make it stick," Marcus warned.

"Let them try." Addison's voice was calm but firm. "If Congressman Barfield wants to suggest that a Rhodes Scholar with a decade of defense contracting experience is unqualified, he's welcome to make that argument on national television."

Jasper's hand found hers under the table. Squeezed once. Thank you.

They broke at seven PM. Addison's brain felt like it had been through a blender, but she had a clear task list. Expand the donor analysis. Draft talking points for the Armed Services Committee briefing. Research Senator Whitehouse's voting record on technology appropriations for the last five years.

"You were incredible in there," Jasper said as they walked back to the Willard Hotel. The evening air was muggy, distinctly DC in summer. "I didn't know you'd done all that research."

"I started on the plane. Finished it last night after you fell asleep."

"Addison." He stopped walking, turned to face her. "You don't have to do this. Any of this. I could handle—"

"I know you could handle it. But you don't have to handle it alone." She reached up, straightened his collar. "We're a team, remember?"

"This wasn't part of the deal when you agreed to date me."

"The deal," she said, "was that we'd build something together. This is what that looks like."

They continued walking. The Willard was only two blocks away, but Jasper seemed in no hurry. He was processing something, working through thoughts he wasn't quite ready to articulate.

"My mother called," he said finally. "This afternoon. Wanted to know how it was going."

"What did you tell her?"

"That you were running circles around the entire legal team." He smiled. "She wasn't surprised."

Addison thought about Elena, about the gala two weeks ago, about the careful way she'd been positioning Addison within Charlotte's power structure. "She's been very generous with her time. The mentorship."

"She sees something in you." Jasper's voice was carefully neutral. "Something she recognizes."

"What do you mean?"

"My mother spent twenty-five years being the perfect political wife. Supporting my father's career. Building relationships that benefited the company. She was brilliant at it." He paused. "But she never got credit. It was always Dad's company. Dad's network. Dad's success."

"That must have been difficult," Addison said carefully.

"I think that's why she's so invested in you. She sees a chance to do it differently. To build power that's acknowledged, not just exercised from the shadows."

They reached the hotel. The lobby was full of congressional staffers and lobbyists, the usual DC crowd. Jasper steered them toward the elevator banks.

"Does that bother you?" Addison asked. "That she's mentoring me?"

"No. Yes. I don't know." The elevator doors closed, giving them privacy. "I love that you two have connected. I love seeing you come into your own. But sometimes I wonder if you're doing this for you, or for her, or because it's what people expect."

Addison leaned against the elevator wall. It was a fair question. She'd been asking herself the same thing.

"I think," she said slowly, "it started as me trying to be helpful. Trying to support your career, make connections that would benefit the company. But somewhere along the way, it became... more than that."

"More how?"

"I realized I was good at it. The strategic thinking. The relationship building. Seeing the angles that other people miss." She met his eyes. "And I realized I liked it. Being in the room. Having influence. Making things happen."

"That doesn't make you my mother's puppet."

"No. But it does mean she recognized something I was only starting to understand about myself." The elevator stopped at their floor. "I want this, Jasper. The career. The impact. The power, if I'm being honest. I want to build something that matters."

"With me?" His voice was quiet. "Or through me?"

The question hit harder than she expected. They'd reached their room. Addison unlocked the door, walked inside, trying to formulate an answer that was both honest and kind.

"Both," she said finally. "I want to build something with you. But I also want to build something that's mine. Is that okay? Can I want both?"

Jasper sat down on the edge of the bed, loosened his tie the rest of the way. "I don't know. I'm still figuring out what I want. Whether I want to be CEO someday, or whether I'm happy being the guy who handles the complicated problems while Christopher gets the corner office."

"You could be CEO," Addison said. "If you wanted it. You're just as qualified as he is."

"But I'm not the eldest son. And in family companies like ours, that still matters." He looked up at her. "Which is why my mother's interest in you is both a gift and a complication. She's positioning you for a role that traditionally would go to Christopher's wife. Except Christopher shows no signs of getting married. And even if he did, the woman he marries might not want what Mom is offering you."

Addison sat down beside him. "What is she offering me?"

"Partner of H. The social architect of Whitmore Industries. The woman who manages the relationships, brokers the deals that can't happen in boardrooms, builds the network that makes everything else possible."

"That's your mother's role."

"For now. But she's not getting younger. And she's never trained a successor before. Never even tried." Jasper took her hand. "Until you."

They sat in silence for a moment. Through the window, Addison could see the Washington Monument lit up against the darkening sky. Power made visible. Influence carved in stone.

"What if I want it?" she asked quietly. "What if I want to be that person?"

"Then I'll support you. The same way you're supporting me." He squeezed her hand. "But I need you to be honest with me. About what you want. What you're building. Where I fit in all of it."

"You're not a stepping stone, Jasper. You're my partner."

"Even if partnering with me is what gives you access to all of this?" He gestured vaguely at the room, but she knew he meant more than that. The power. The network. The possibilities.

"Even then." She turned to face him fully. "I love you. Not your last name. Not your mother's connections. Not what you can give me access to. You."

"But you love the access too."

"Yes." She wasn't going to lie about that. "I love what I'm learning. What I'm becoming. The person I am when I'm solving problems like the one we're facing this week. Is that shallow?"

"It's honest." Jasper pulled her closer. "And I'd rather have honesty than pretense."

Her phone buzzed. Sarah, the political consultant: *Whitehouse's office is interested. Can you have the briefing materials ready by tomorrow at 2 PM?*

"Work?" Jasper asked.

"Senator Whitehouse will meet with us. But I need to prep materials tonight."

"Then we should order room service. You can work, I'll quiz you on the specs."

It wasn't romantic. It wasn't the DC getaway couples were supposed to have. But as they spread out laptops and legal pads across the hotel room desk, Addison felt more connected to Jasper than she had in weeks.

This was their language. Solving problems together. Building something larger than themselves.

Even if neither of them was entirely sure what that something would become.

---

The briefing with Senator Whitehouse's senior staff went better than expected. Addison presented the technology specs, highlighted the cost savings over existing systems, emphasized the manufacturing jobs in North Carolina and Virginia — both swing states.

The senior defense aide asked pointed questions. Addison answered all of them, pulling up data she'd memorized during the night before.

"This is impressive," the aide said finally. "I'll recommend the Senator take a meeting with Congressman Barfield before the hearing. Make sure he understands the optics of attacking genuinely innovative technology."

That was as close to a win as they were going to get.

Back in the K Street war room, Marcus looked grudgingly impressed. "You bought us space. Maybe enough to negotiate a de-escalation."

"We don't want de-escalation," Addison said. "We want vindication."

"That's risky."

"So is letting them establish a narrative that we cut corners." She pulled up her tablet again, this time showing a mock-up of a press release. "We should go on offense. Hold our own press conference. Invite the media to tour the facility where the technology is being built. Show them what American innovation looks like."

"Barfield will hate that," one of the consultants said.

"Good. Let him hate it. Let him look like the obstructionist while we're showcasing American jobs and next-generation technology."

Jasper was watching her again, that same expression from yesterday. Proud. Concerned. Recalibrating.

"You really think we can flip this?" he asked.

"I think we can reframe it. Which is the same thing." Addison stood, moved to the whiteboard. "Right now, the story is: 'Defense contractor under fire for cost overruns.' We change it to: 'Congress attacks innovative American company while China invests in next-gen technology.' Make it about competitiveness. Make it about jobs. Make it about everything except Barfield's donor relationships."

Marcus was nodding slowly. "It's aggressive."

"It's necessary."

"All right." He turned to his team. "Sarah, start drafting the press release. Tom, reach out to the Charlotte Observer and the Raleigh News. Local papers first. Then we go national." Back to Addison: "You're sure about this?"

"I'm sure about the technology. I'm sure about our position. And I'm sure that playing defense in Washington is how companies die slow deaths." She met his eyes. "We didn't do anything wrong. So let's stop acting like we did."

The room shifted again. This time, it wasn't just respect. It was recognition. She wasn't an assistant anymore. She was running this operation.

"Jasper?" Marcus asked. "It's your contract. Your call."

Jasper looked at Addison for a long moment. Then he smiled — the real smile, not the practiced one he used in business settings.

"Do it," he said. "We go on offense."

---

The press conference happened forty-eight hours later, at the Whitmore Industries facility in Durham. Sixty reporters, five cameras, and Jasper at a podium explaining why the Meridian contract represented the future of American defense technology.

Addison stood in the back, watching him command the room. He was good at this — better than he gave himself credit for. Confident but not arrogant. Technical but not inaccessible.

"The question," Jasper said in response to a reporter from Defense News, "isn't why did this cost what it cost. The question is: can we afford not to invest in technology that will keep American forces ahead of our adversaries? I'd argue we can't. And I'd argue that congressional grandstanding doesn't serve that goal."

It was a direct shot at Barfield. Bold. Calculated. Exactly what Addison had recommended.

The story that ran that night on the evening news wasn't about cost overruns. It was about innovation and American competitiveness. By morning, three senators had issued statements supporting the Meridian contract.

Congressman Barfield quietly withdrew his request for a formal hearing.

Victory.

On the flight back to Charlotte, Jasper was quiet. Addison worked through emails, already thinking three moves ahead. The Order of Malta Gala was coming up. Elena had mentioned it twice. There were people she wanted Addison to meet.

"You were made for this," Jasper said suddenly.

Addison looked up from her laptop. "For what?"

"This. Crisis management. Strategic communications. Playing the game at this level." He was watching her with an expression she couldn't quite read. "You walked into a room full of the most expensive lawyers in DC and took over. And you were right. About all of it."

"We were lucky."

"No. We were prepared. Because you prepared us." He reached across the aisle, took her hand. "I mean it. You were made for this."

The words should have felt like a compliment. They did feel like a compliment.

But they also felt like a question. Like Jasper was trying to figure out what version of their future included both of them — or whether her trajectory was already pulling them in different directions.

"We did this together," Addison said.

"We did. But you led." He squeezed her hand. "And I think you should keep leading. Whatever that looks like. Wherever it takes you."

"What if it takes me somewhere you don't want to go?"

"Then we'll figure it out. Together." He smiled, but there was something wistful in it. "I'm not going to be the guy who holds you back. Even if that means redefining what we're building."

Addison wanted to say something reassuring. Something certain. But the truth was, she didn't know what they were building anymore. Three months ago, she'd been an executive assistant trying to help her boyfriend navigate corporate politics.

Now she was running congressional strategy and being positioned for a role that didn't have a job description.

The only thing she knew for certain was that she was good at it. Better than she'd ever been at anything else.

And she wanted more.

"Elena called," Addison said. "While you were doing the interview with Bloomberg. She wants me to go with her to the Order of Malta Gala next month."

"That's a big deal. Catholic high society. Major networking opportunity."

"I know." Addison hesitated. "Is it weird? How much time I'm spending with your mother?"

"It would be weird if you weren't. She's offering you things I can't." He met her eyes. "Connections. Training. A path to influence that doesn't depend on me."

"But I don't want it to not depend on you."

"Maybe it needs to. For a while. So you can figure out what's yours and what's ours." Jasper leaned back in his seat. "I watched you this week. You were brilliant. But you were also independent. Making decisions, setting strategy. You didn't need me to validate you."

"I always need you."

"Want me, maybe. But need? I don't think so. Not anymore." He smiled. "And that's not a bad thing, Addison. That's growth."

The plane began its descent into Charlotte. Below them, the city spread out in familiar patterns. Home. Except it didn't quite feel like home anymore.

It felt like a launching pad.

Addison didn't know where she was launching to. But as she looked at Jasper — this brilliant, kind man who was giving her permission to become something neither of them fully understood yet — she knew one thing.

She was ready.

For whatever came next.
