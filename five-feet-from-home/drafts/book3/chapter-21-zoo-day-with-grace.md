# Chapter 21: Zoo Day with Grace

*POV: Addison Price*

---

The Charlotte Zoo parking lot smelled like hot asphalt and overpriced concessions, and Grace Barrett was bouncing in her car seat like she'd been mainlining sugar since breakfast.

"We're here! We're here! Aunt Addie, we're here!"

Addison Price turned off the ignition and twisted in her seat to look at the seven-year-old strapped behind her. Grace's hair was in two lopsided braids that Addison had attempted that morning—she'd watched three YouTube tutorials and still couldn't make them symmetrical. Grace didn't seem to mind. She was wearing a unicorn t-shirt, purple leggings with glitter stripes, and light-up sneakers that had been blinking frantically for the entire thirty-minute drive.

"I can see that, Bug. You ready?"

"I've been ready since I woke up." Grace was already fumbling with her seatbelt buckle. "Did you know flamingos stand on one leg to stay warm? And penguins mate for life? And elephants can remember things for fifty years?"

"I did not know all of that." Addison climbed out and opened Grace's door, helping her down. "Where'd you learn it?"

"School. And also the Discovery Channel. Mommy lets me watch it sometimes when Lucas is napping and she needs quiet time."

Addison filed that detail away—Elena needing quiet time, Elena who was still recovering from the pregnancy and the medical crisis that had nearly taken her from them. She made a mental note to check in with her sister-in-law later.

But today wasn't about Elena. It wasn't about Jasper, or BSS, or the Philadelphia hospital crisis she'd just closed, or the fact that Chris Donnelly had texted her this morning to ask how she was doing. Today was about a seven-year-old who'd asked, very seriously over breakfast two weeks ago, if Aunt Addie could take her to the zoo. Just the two of them.

Addison had said yes before she'd thought about it. Now, standing in the parking lot with Grace's small hand tucked into hers, she was glad she hadn't thought too hard.

"Okay, Bug. Where to first?"

Grace pulled a crumpled map from her pocket—she'd printed it from the zoo's website and annotated it with colored markers. "We start with the African elephants, then the giraffes, then the lions, then—"

"Wait. You have a whole plan?"

"Of course." Grace looked at her like this was obvious. "You can't just wander around a zoo, Aunt Addie. You have to have a strategy."

Addison bit back a smile. "You sound like your dad."

"Daddy says strategy is important. He says if you don't have a plan, you're just reacting." Grace consulted her map. "So. Elephants first. Come on."

She tugged Addison toward the entrance, and Addison let herself be pulled, the corporate crisis manager who'd just saved a hospital system now following the instructions of a first-grader with a color-coded zoo map.

---

The African elephant exhibit was crowded with families and school groups, but Grace wedged herself into a spot at the front railing with the determination of someone who'd been planning this for weeks.

"Look," she breathed, pointing at the largest elephant, who was using her trunk to spray water over her back. "That's Zuri. She's thirty-two years old. She came from another zoo when she was ten."

"How do you know all this?"

"I did research." Grace pressed her face closer to the railing. "Aunt Addie, do you think elephants get lonely?"

The question caught Addison off-guard. "I... I don't know. Maybe?"

"I think they do." Grace watched Zuri move slowly across the enclosure, her massive body swaying. "Because they remember things. Like people. If you remember someone and they're not there anymore, that's lonely."

Addison felt something twist in her chest. She thought about Kendra, about the years they'd spent apart before reconnecting. About the loneliness that had lived in that gap, unacknowledged until it wasn't there anymore.

"Yeah," she said quietly. "I think you're right."

Grace looked up at her, those wide Barrett eyes catching the light. "Were you ever lonely, Aunt Addie?"

"Sometimes."

"Are you still?"

Addison considered lying. Considered giving the easy answer that would move them past this moment and back to the simple joy of a zoo day. But Grace had a way of asking questions that deserved real answers.

"Not as much as I used to be."

Grace nodded, satisfied. Then she grabbed Addison's hand again. "Good. Now let's go see the giraffes. I want to see if they're as tall as Daddy says."

---

The giraffes were, in fact, as tall as Daddy said.

Grace stood beneath the viewing platform, craning her neck back so far Addison worried she'd topple over. "They're so big. How do they even walk without falling?"

"Carefully, I'd guess."

"Do you think they know they're weird-looking?"

Addison laughed—a real laugh that caught her by surprise. "What makes you think they're weird-looking?"

"Their necks are like—" Grace stretched her arms up over her head, standing on her tiptoes. "—like someone pulled them like taffy. But in a good way. Like they're special."

"I think that's exactly right. They're special because they're different."

"Like you and Aunt Kendra."

Addison blinked. "What?"

"You and Aunt Kendra are special because you're different. Not like other aunts." Grace tilted her head, studying Addison the way she studied the animals—with total focus and zero filter. "Mommy says you're brave. She says it's brave to be yourself even when people don't understand."

"Your mom said that?"

"Mm-hmm. When I asked why you and Aunt Kendra hold hands sometimes. She said love looks different for different people, like how some animals have long necks and some have long trunks." Grace turned back to the giraffes. "I think that makes sense."

Addison didn't trust herself to speak for a moment. Elena had always been supportive, but hearing it filtered through Grace—this small, perceptive person who saw the world in such clear terms—hit differently.

"Yeah, Bug," she managed. "That makes sense to me too."

"Good." Grace consulted her map. "Now we have to do the silly walk."

"The what?"

"The silly walk. We have to walk like giraffes to the next exhibit. It's part of the plan."

"That was not on the map."

"It's an implied part of the plan." Grace demonstrated, taking long, exaggerated steps with her knees lifting high. "Come on, Aunt Addie. You have to do it too."

Addison looked around. Families were watching. A teenager with a zoo volunteer shirt was trying not to laugh. The old Addison—the one who wore armor and never let anyone see her stumble—would have refused.

But Grace was looking at her with such pure expectation, such absolute certainty that Aunt Addie would say yes, that Addison found herself lifting her knees and taking long, ridiculous steps across the path.

Grace shrieked with laughter. "You look so funny!"

"I look like an idiot."

"You look like a giraffe!" Grace grabbed her hand, and they walked together in exaggerated synchronization, two ridiculous humans pretending to be long-necked herbivores, and Addison couldn't remember the last time she'd felt this light.

---

They did silly walks for every animal.

At the lions, they prowled and growled. At the monkeys, they scratched their armpits and made oo-oo-aa-aa sounds. At the bears, they lumbered and swayed. Grace insisted on authenticity, which meant Addison spent twenty minutes hopping like a kangaroo until her thighs burned.

By the time they reached the flamingos, Addison was exhausted and exhilarated in equal measure.

"Okay," Grace announced. "This is the big one."

"Why is this the big one?"

"Because flamingos stand on one leg for a really long time. So we have to see who can do it longer."

"Grace, I don't think—"

But Grace was already lifting one leg, balancing on the other with her arms out for stability. She wobbled, corrected, wobbled again.

Addison sighed and lifted her own leg. She lasted maybe ten seconds before her standing leg started shaking.

Grace made it to fifteen before she toppled sideways, landing in a heap of giggles on the grass.

"I almost had it!"

"You did great." Addison offered her a hand up. "Want to try again?"

They tried six more times. Grace's record was twenty-three seconds. Addison's was eighteen. On the seventh attempt, they both fell over at the same time and lay on the grass, staring up at the sky, laughing so hard Addison's sides hurt.

"This is so fun," Grace gasped.

"Yeah," Addison agreed. "It really is."

A shadow fell across them. Addison looked up to find a zoo employee—a kind-faced woman in her fifties—smiling down at them. "Y'all doing okay down there?"

"We're being flamingos," Grace explained, very seriously.

"I can see that. You're doing a great job."

Grace beamed. The woman moved on, and Grace sat up, brushing grass off her leggings. "Can we ride the carousel next? Please?"

"Absolutely."

---

The carousel was old-fashioned, the kind with hand-painted animals and piped-in music that sounded like it belonged in a different century. Grace stood at the entrance, eyes wide, taking in all the options.

"Which one do you want?" Addison asked.

"The unicorn." No hesitation. Grace pointed at a white horse with a golden horn and flowers painted on its flanks. "That one's magic."

"What makes it magic?"

"It just is. You can tell." Grace climbed on, gripping the pole with both hands. "What are you going to ride?"

Addison scanned the carousel. There was a noble-looking lion. A sleek black panther. A dolphin mid-leap.

And then there was the rooster.

It was painted in garish shades of red and gold, its beak open in what could only be described as an indignant squawk. It looked ridiculous. It looked like it had opinions about being a carousel animal.

"That one," Addison said, pointing.

Grace burst out laughing. "The chicken?"

"It's a rooster."

"It's so weird!"

"You said giraffes are special because they're weird."

"That's true." Grace considered this. "Okay. The rooster is special."

Addison climbed on. The rooster was smaller than she expected, and her legs dangled awkwardly. The carousel operator—a teenage boy who looked deeply bored—gave them a thumbs-up and started the music.

They went around. And around. And around.

Grace waved every time she passed the spot where Addison could see her. Addison waved back from her ridiculous rooster, and somewhere around the second rotation, she stopped caring what she looked like. This was joy. This was the whole point.

They rode it three times. By the third round, Grace was singing along to the music, making up words that didn't match the melody. Addison found herself humming too.

When they finally got off, Grace's cheeks were flushed and her braids were coming loose. "That was the best. Can we get a snack?"

"Absolutely. What do you want?"

"Ice cream. No—wait. A pretzel. No—both."

"Both it is."

They found a concession stand and sat at a picnic table in the shade, sharing a soft pretzel and two ice cream cones that melted faster than they could eat them. Grace's vanilla cone dripped onto her shirt. Addison's chocolate dripped onto her hand.

"Aunt Addie?"

"Yeah, Bug?"

"Can we get matching stuffed animals?"

Addison paused mid-lick. "You want matching stuffed animals?"

"Yeah. Like, we each get the same one. So we remember today."

Something in Addison's chest cracked open. "What kind of stuffed animal?"

Grace thought about it, ice cream forgotten. "Penguins."

"Why penguins?"

"Because they mate for life. And they take care of each other. And they're funny-looking but also really brave because they live in the cold and they don't give up."

Addison had to set down her ice cream because her hands were shaking. "Penguins," she repeated.

"Yeah." Grace looked at her, those eyes so much like Jasper's and yet entirely her own. "Is that okay?"

"That's perfect."

---

The gift shop was a chaotic wonderland of overpriced souvenirs and shrieking children. Grace navigated it like a general surveying a battlefield, rejecting the lions ("too serious"), the giraffes ("we already did that"), and the flamingos ("those are for standing, not cuddling").

She found the penguins in the back corner—plush, black-and-white, with orange feet and stitched-on smiles that made them look perpetually delighted.

"These," Grace declared, holding up two identical penguins. "These are the ones."

Addison took them to the register, paid for them without checking the price, and handed one to Grace. Grace hugged it immediately, pressing her cheek to its soft head.

"What are you going to name yours?" Addison asked as they walked back to the car.

Grace didn't hesitate. "Sir Waddles."

"Sir Waddles?"

"He's fancy. He deserves a title." Grace looked at the penguin in Addison's hands. "What about yours?"

Addison studied the penguin. It stared back at her with its stitched eyes, looking both ridiculous and oddly dignified. "Detective Penguin."

Grace's face lit up. "Because you solve mysteries!"

"Something like that."

"I love it." Grace buckled herself into her car seat, Sir Waddles secured on her lap. "Aunt Addie?"

"Yeah?"

"This is the best day ever."

Addison started the car, but she didn't pull out of the parking space right away. She turned to look at Grace, at this small person who'd somehow worked her way past every defense Addison had ever built.

"You know what, Bug? I think it might be mine too."

Grace smiled—bright and uncomplicated and so full of trust it hurt. "Can we do it again sometime?"

"Absolutely."

"Just us?"

"Just us."

"Promise?"

Addison held up her pinky. Grace linked hers through it, sealing the deal the way only seven-year-olds and people who meant it could.

"Promise."

---

They drove home with the windows down and the radio playing something upbeat and forgettable. Grace sang along, making up half the words. Sir Waddles sat on her lap. Detective Penguin sat in the passenger seat, buckled in like a tiny, dignified co-pilot.

Addison's phone buzzed with messages—probably work, probably something urgent that would pull her back into the world of crises and strategies and armor.

She ignored it.

For now, this was enough. The zoo day. The silly walks. The carousel. The matching penguins. The promise.

Grace, humming in the backseat, her voice bright and off-key and perfect.

Addison glanced at Detective Penguin. "We did good today," she said quietly.

The penguin stared back, its stitched smile unwavering.

Yeah. They did.

---

*End of Chapter 21*
