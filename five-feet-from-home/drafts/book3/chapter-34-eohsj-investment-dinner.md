# Chapter 34: EOHSJ Investment Dinner

**POV:** Addison
**Location:** NYC
**Book:** Book 3 - The Expansion

---

The restaurant is the kind of place that doesn't advertise.

No sign on the door. No website. Just a discreet bronze plaque that reads *Est. 1924* and a doorman who knows your name before you say it. Elena called it "old money's living room," and standing in the foyer with its dark wood paneling and oil paintings of saints, Addison understands why.

She's wearing a navy dress—simple, elegant, expensive—and the Maltese cross pendant Elena gave her two weeks ago. It's small, understated, but the people here will notice. They always do.

"You look perfect," Elena says beside her, adjusting the clasp on her own necklace. She's in a deep green silk dress that makes her eyes look almost gold in the low light. Poised, confident, utterly at ease.

Addison is none of those things.

"I still don't understand why I'm here," she murmurs as they follow the maître d' through a hallway lined with photographs—charity galas, papal visits, dignitaries shaking hands with men in white tie and sashes.

"Because Lady Margaret asked for you specifically," Elena says. "And when Lady Margaret asks, you show up."

"I've met her twice."

"Twice is enough."

The maître d' stops at a private dining room and opens the door. Inside, a long mahogany table is set for twelve, candles flickering in silver holders. The room smells like beeswax and expensive wine. Seven people are already seated, all in formal attire—dark suits for the men, elegant dresses for the women. The conversation pauses as Elena and Addison enter.

"Elena, darling." A woman at the head of the table stands, and Addison recognizes her immediately: Lady Margaret Ashford, British aristocracy, Dame of the Equestrian Order of the Holy Sepulchre of Jerusalem. She's in her late sixties, silver-haired, imperious, with the kind of bearing that suggests centuries of people doing exactly what she tells them.

Elena crosses to her and they exchange kisses on both cheeks—the European way, practiced and smooth.

"Margaret, thank you for including us," Elena says.

"Of course." Lady Margaret's gaze shifts to Addison. "And you brought her. Excellent."

Addison steps forward, offering her hand. "Lady Margaret."

Margaret takes it, her grip firm. "Miss Price. I'm delighted you accepted my invitation."

"I'm honored to be here."

"Sit," Margaret says, gesturing to two empty chairs midway down the table. "We're just getting started."

Addison takes her seat, Elena beside her. Across the table, a man in his fifties with steel-gray hair and a Patek Philippe watch nods at her. To her left, a woman in pearls and a cream dress offers a polite smile.

"Addison Price," Margaret says, addressing the table, "is the managing partner at Barrett Security Solutions. She's also a board member of the Phoenix Foundation and one of the most capable crisis managers in the country."

Addison keeps her expression neutral, but inwardly she's recalibrating. She didn't expect to be introduced like that. She expected to be Elena's plus-one. Background. Observing.

"Crisis management," the man across from her says. His accent is East Coast money—Connecticut, maybe. "What kind of crises?"

"Corporate, political, reputational," Addison says smoothly. "Anything that threatens an organization's ability to function."

"Recent work?"

"A senator facing an ethics investigation. A Fortune 500 company dealing with a data breach. A nonprofit navigating donor scandals."

The man nods, interested. "And you solve these problems how?"

"By controlling the narrative. Crisis management isn't about fixing what went wrong—it's about shaping how people understand what went wrong."

"Spin," the woman in pearls says, not unkindly.

"Strategy," Addison corrects. "Spin is dishonest. Strategy is intentional."

Margaret smiles. "I told you she was sharp."

The first course arrives—oysters on ice, arranged like art. Conversation shifts to safer topics: recent galas, upcoming fundraisers, the archbishop's visit to New York. Addison listens more than she speaks, watching the dynamics. This isn't a dinner. It's an audition.

---

By the third course—duck breast with blackberry reduction—the real conversation begins.

"We're here tonight," Margaret says, setting down her wine glass, "to discuss the Order's charitable priorities for the coming year. Specifically, funding for Catholic education initiatives in underserved communities."

A man at the far end of the table—younger, maybe early forties, expensive suit—leans forward. "How much are we talking?"

"Five million, distributed across twelve schools in six cities. The goal is to stabilize enrollment, update infrastructure, and provide scholarships for families who can't afford tuition."

"And the return?" the younger man asks.

"The return," Margaret says coolly, "is that we fulfill our baptismal call to serve the poor and educate the next generation of Catholics."

"That's a lovely sentiment, but sentiment doesn't build endowments."

"No," Margaret agrees. "Money builds endowments. Which is why you're here, Thomas."

Thomas—the younger man—smiles, unbothered. "I'm just saying, if we're going to commit five million, we should have metrics. Enrollment numbers. Retention rates. Graduation outcomes."

"Agreed," the woman in pearls says. "We're stewards, not ATMs."

Addison watches the exchange, fascinated. This is the intersection of faith and finance, and it's more complicated than she expected. These people aren't just writing checks—they're negotiating influence, legacy, mission.

"Miss Price," Margaret says, turning to her. "You work with nonprofits. What's your take?"

Addison sets down her fork, aware that everyone is looking at her. "Metrics are important. Donors want to know their money is being used effectively. But if you only fund what's measurable, you'll miss the things that matter most."

"Such as?" Thomas asks.

"Community. Stability. Hope. You can measure graduation rates, but you can't measure what it means for a kid to have a safe place to go every day. Or a teacher who believes in them. Or a faith community that doesn't treat them like a charity case."

Thomas tilts his head. "So we fund based on feelings?"

"No. You fund based on mission. And then you build metrics that align with that mission."

Margaret's smile is sharp and approving. "Well said."

The conversation continues, and Addison finds herself drawn in despite her nerves. She talks about the Phoenix Foundation's donor strategy, about how they balance immediate needs with long-term sustainability. She listens to a debate about whether Catholic schools should require Mass attendance or make it optional. She watches Elena navigate the conversation with the ease of someone who's done this a hundred times.

By dessert—a delicate panna cotta with espresso—Addison feels like she's passed some invisible test.

---

After dinner, the group moves to a sitting room with leather armchairs and a fireplace. Whiskey and cigars are offered. Addison declines the cigar but accepts a glass of Macallan.

Margaret sits beside her, close enough that their conversation is private.

"You did well tonight," Margaret says.

"Thank you."

"I wasn't complimenting you. I was stating a fact." She takes a sip of her whiskey. "Elena tells me you're Catholic."

"I am."

"Practicing?"

"When I can."

"That's not an answer."

Addison smiles faintly. "I go to Mass most Sundays. I pray. I'm still figuring out what it all means."

"Good. Faith without questions is just obedience."

"Is that why you invited me tonight? To see if I'm Catholic enough?"

"No. I invited you because you're capable, intelligent, and you understand how power works. The Order needs people like you."

Addison turns to look at her. "The Order?"

"The Equestrian Order of the Holy Sepulchre. We're a papal order dedicated to supporting the Christian presence in the Holy Land and serving the universal Church. We fund schools, hospitals, parishes. We work with bishops, diplomats, business leaders. It's not a social club, Miss Price. It's a mission."

"And you think I'd be a good fit?"

"I know you would."

Addison doesn't know what to say to that. She's spent her entire adult life avoiding institutions, avoiding commitments that feel like entanglements. But this—this feels different. Not like a cage. Like a door.

"Think about it," Margaret says. "We meet quarterly in New York. Occasionally in Rome. There's a discernment process—it's not a fast track. But if you're interested, I'll sponsor you."

"Why me?"

Margaret's gaze is steady, unflinching. "Because Elena trusts you. And I trust Elena."

---

Later, in the car back to their hotel, Addison stares out the window at the Manhattan skyline, still processing.

"You okay?" Elena asks.

"Margaret offered to sponsor me for the Order."

"I know. She told me she was going to."

Addison turns to her. "You knew?"

"I suggested it."

"Why?"

Elena shifts in her seat, facing her. "Because you're looking for something, Addie. I can see it. You've built this incredible career, and you're good at it—better than good. But it's not enough. You want meaning. You want purpose."

"I have purpose."

"You have work. That's not the same thing."

Addison opens her mouth to argue, then closes it. Because Elena's right. She's always right about this kind of thing.

"The Order isn't just about charity," Elena continues. "It's about being part of something bigger than yourself. It's about faith in action. And I think—if you let yourself—you could find something there."

"What if I'm not ready?"

"You don't have to be ready. You just have to be willing."

Addison looks at her, this woman who's become one of the most important people in her life. Elena, who sees her not as a crisis manager or a board member or a partner, but as a person still figuring out who she is.

"I'll think about it," Addison says.

Elena smiles. "Good."

---

That night, alone in her hotel room, Addison sits on the edge of the bed with her laptop open. She Googles the Equestrian Order of the Holy Sepulchre, reading about its history, its mission, its members.

Papal knights. Pilgrimages to Jerusalem. Supporting Christians in the Holy Land.

It sounds impossible. Medieval. Like something out of a novel.

But then she thinks about tonight—the dinner, the conversation, the way those people talked about money and mission like they were inseparable. The way Margaret looked at her, not like she needed to prove herself, but like she already belonged.

She picks up her phone and texts Hawk.

**Addison:** You ever feel like your life is about to change and you don't know if you're ready?

He responds almost immediately.

**Hawk:** All the time.

**Addison:** What do you do?

**Hawk:** I say yes anyway.

She stares at the message, smiling despite herself.

**Addison:** Even when it's terrifying?

**Hawk:** Especially then.

Addison sets the phone down and closes her laptop. She's not ready to say yes. Not yet. But she's not saying no, either.

And maybe that's enough for now.
