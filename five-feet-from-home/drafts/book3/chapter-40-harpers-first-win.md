# Chapter 40: Harper's First Win

*POV: Harper*

*Location: Miami, FL*

---

The wire transfer confirmation came through at 4:47 PM on a Friday.

Harper stared at her laptop screen, at the clean string of numbers that meant Ricardo Santiago was finally, completely free. Three months of careful maneuvering, quiet negotiations, strategic positioning—all of it condensed into a single digital transaction that severed a businessman from cartel entanglement without a drop of blood spilled or a whisper of exposure.

Elegant work, if she said so herself.

She sat back in her desk chair, watching the Miami skyline turn gold through the floor-to-ceiling windows of her new office. The BSS Miami office wasn't much yet—just her, a rented executive suite in Brickell, and a phone that had been ringing more than she'd expected. But it was hers. Her territory, her cases, her wins.

And this one was a win.

Harper picked up her phone and texted Ricardo: *It's done. You're clear.*

The response came back almost immediately: *Thank you. I mean it. You saved my family.*

She allowed herself a small smile. This was why she did the work. Not the adrenaline of crisis management—though she was good at that too—but the satisfaction of solving impossible problems with precision and patience. Of building solutions that served everyone's interests without anyone having to lose.

The Santiago case had been exactly that kind of problem.

Ricardo had made the classic mistake: accepting financing from people he hadn't fully vetted. The loan looked legitimate on paper—competitive rate, standard terms, professional documentation. But the money had come from a shell company connected to a Colombian cartel, and when Ricardo tried to refinance through legitimate channels, the cartel made it clear that walking away wasn't an option.

Pay up. Or face consequences that went well beyond financial.

When Ricardo called BSS, he'd been desperate and out of options. Lawyers couldn't help—this wasn't a legal problem. Law enforcement couldn't help without putting his family at risk. He needed someone who could navigate the gray areas, who understood how to negotiate with people who operated outside normal frameworks.

So Harper had done what she always did: mapped the landscape.

Who were the actual decision-makers? What did they care about beyond the money? What leverage points existed that could make letting Ricardo go more valuable than keeping him trapped?

It had taken weeks of research, quiet conversations with contacts in Miami's development world, careful cultivation of relationships that might lead somewhere useful. And then she'd found it: the cartel's Miami operations were managed by a man named Esteban Ruiz, who had his own problems. Specifically, he was trying to legitimize his business interests, to build a portfolio of clean assets that could survive scrutiny from both law enforcement and his superiors in Colombia.

Ricardo's properties weren't particularly valuable as income-generating assets. But they were valuable as collateral for the legitimate financing Ruiz needed to expand his clean operations.

So Harper had built a deal: Ricardo would transfer the properties to a new LLC controlled by a legitimate investment group that Harper had carefully vetted. That LLC would pay off the cartel's loan at a negotiated discount—not the full value, but enough to satisfy the original investment. In exchange, the cartel got clean paper on properties they could use as collateral, and Ricardo walked away free and clear.

No violence. No exposure. No trail that would lead law enforcement back to anyone involved.

The kind of solution that required understanding not just the legal and financial frameworks, but the human psychology underneath.

Her phone rang. Jasper.

"I heard," he said without preamble. "Santiago's out. Clean exit. Hell of a piece of work, Harper."

"Thank you." She stood and walked to the window, looking out at Biscayne Bay. "It was the right kind of case. Strategic, patient. Not firefighting—architecture."

"That's exactly what you should be doing down there," Jasper said. "Not crisis response. Long-term strategy. The kind of chess game that takes months to play out."

Harper felt warmth spread through her chest. Jasper didn't give praise lightly. When he did, it meant something.

"You built something real down there," he continued. "Three months ago, BSS Miami was just an idea on paper. Now you've got a major win, a reputation forming, and more inquiries than you can handle alone. That's what I sent you down there to do."

"I had a good teacher," Harper said.

"Don't sell yourself short. This was all you." She heard him typing in the background, multitasking as always. "What's next?"

"There's a medical clinic operator who reached out. Similar situation—accepted funding from the wrong people, needs extraction. But I'm not sure I want to take it. Not yet."

"Why not?"

Harper considered the question. "Because I want to make sure I'm building this office right. Not just taking every case that comes in, but being strategic about what kinds of problems we solve and how we position ourselves in this market."

Jasper was quiet for a moment. "That's smart thinking. You're not just executing anymore—you're leading. That's the difference."

After they hung up, Harper stood at her window for a long moment, watching the boats drift across the bay. The sun was setting now, painting everything in shades of amber and rose. Miami at golden hour looked like a dream someone had about success—beautiful and slightly unreal.

Her phone buzzed with a text from Ethan: *Heard you closed the case. Congratulations, brilliant woman.*

Harper smiled. *Thank you. How did you hear?*

*Jasper told Elena who told me. You know how the network works.*

*Apparently I'm the last to know anything.*

*You've been busy saving people. We forgive you.*

Harper was typing a response when another text came through: *I'm at MIA. Gate D7. Surprise.*

She stared at the message. *You're WHERE?*

*Miami International Airport. I flew down to celebrate with you. If that's okay. I probably should have asked first.*

Harper felt something catch in her chest. Ethan had flown to Miami. Without being asked. Without her having to choose between work and relationship, between celebrating her victory and maintaining her professional distance.

He'd just... come.

*It's more than okay,* she typed. *It's perfect. I'll pick you up in forty minutes.*

*See you soon. I have champagne.*

Harper grabbed her bag and keys, locked her office, and headed for the elevator with something that felt dangerously close to joy fizzing in her veins.

---

The traffic from Brickell to the airport was predictably terrible, but Harper didn't care. She used the time to check her reflection in the rearview mirror at stoplights, to wonder if she should have gone home to change first, to feel nervous and excited in equal measure.

Ethan was here. In Miami. To celebrate with her.

When had anyone ever done that?

She found him waiting outside baggage claim, leaning against a pillar with a carry-on bag at his feet and a smile that made her forget about traffic entirely. He was wearing dark jeans and a blue button-down with the sleeves rolled up, looking like he'd just stepped out of a catalog for men who had their lives together.

"Hey," he said as she pulled up to the curb.

"Hey yourself." Harper popped the trunk, and he tossed his bag in before sliding into the passenger seat.

Then he kissed her—soft and sweet and tasting faintly of coffee—and Harper felt three months of Miami isolation crack open like a shell.

"You flew here," she said when they pulled apart.

"I did." Ethan grinned. "You closed your first major case in a new city. That deserves more than a phone call."

"You're insane."

"Probably. But you like me anyway."

Harper laughed and pulled back into traffic. "Where are we going?"

"I made a reservation," Ethan said. "But not at a restaurant."

"Cryptic."

"Trust me."

Twenty minutes later, they were at a marina in Coconut Grove. Ethan led her down the dock to where a sleek white yacht was moored, maybe forty feet long, with CELEBRATION painted in elegant script on the stern.

"You chartered a yacht," Harper said flatly.

"I chartered a yacht," Ethan confirmed. "With champagne, dinner catered from that place you mentioned liking, and a sunset cruise around the bay. Because you deserve to celebrate properly."

Harper looked at him—at this man who'd listened when she'd mentioned offhand that she liked a particular restaurant, who'd remembered, who'd planned this entire thing without making a big production out of it.

"I don't deserve you," she said quietly.

"Wrong," Ethan said. "You deserve exactly this. Someone who shows up. Someone who celebrates your wins. Someone who knows that you're brilliant and driven and sometimes you need someone to remind you to actually enjoy your victories instead of just moving on to the next problem."

Harper felt her throat tighten. "I'm not good at this. At letting people celebrate me."

"I know." Ethan took her hand. "But I'm very persistent. Come on. We have champagne to drink and a sunset to watch."

---

The yacht was intimate and perfect—a main cabin with white leather seating, a small galley where their dinner was already laid out under warming covers, and an upper deck with cushioned loungers facing the bow.

The captain was a weathered man in his sixties who nodded at them and disappeared to the wheelhouse. Within minutes, they were untying from the dock and gliding out into the bay.

Ethan opened the champagne—proper French champagne, not prosecco—and poured two glasses as they stood on the upper deck watching Miami recede behind them.

"To Harper Chen," he said, raising his glass. "Who solved the unsolvable, built something from nothing, and made it look easy."

"It wasn't easy," Harper said.

"That's what makes it impressive." Ethan clinked his glass against hers. "To you."

They drank, and Harper let herself feel it—the satisfaction of the win, the warmth of being celebrated, the strange joy of having someone who cared enough to fly across the country on a Friday night.

"Tell me about it," Ethan said, settling onto one of the loungers and pulling her down beside him. "The case. How did you do it?"

So Harper told him. About Ricardo and his impossible situation. About mapping the landscape and finding Ruiz's pressure points. About building a solution that gave everyone what they actually needed instead of what they said they wanted.

Ethan listened the way he always did—fully present, asking smart questions, understanding the parts she couldn't quite articulate.

"That's brilliant," he said when she finished. "Not just the solution, but the patience. Three months of work to find the right angle."

"That's what Jasper said. That I'm not firefighting anymore—I'm building."

"He's right." Ethan topped off her champagne. "Is that what you want? To keep building in Miami?"

Harper looked out at the water, at the Miami skyline lit up against the darkening sky. "I think so. It's lonely sometimes. But it's also mine in a way that nothing in Charlotte ever was. Even at BSS, even as COO, I was always executing Jasper's vision. Here, I get to build my own."

"Then I'm glad you're here," Ethan said. "Even though I miss you. Even though it's hard sometimes."

"You could move here," Harper said, surprising herself. "I know that's a big ask. I know your architecture firm is in Charlotte. But—"

"Harper." Ethan turned to face her fully. "I'm not moving to Miami. Not yet, anyway."

Her stomach dropped. "Oh."

"Let me finish." He took her hand. "I'm not moving to Miami because you're not staying in Miami. Not forever. You're building BSS's southern hub, proving the expansion model works, and then you're coming back to Charlotte to do the same thing nationally. That's the trajectory. That's what Jasper sees. That's what you're capable of."

Harper blinked. "I hadn't thought that far ahead."

"I have." Ethan smiled. "Because I know you, brilliant woman. You're not going to stay in one city solving one type of problem. You're going to build something bigger. And when you do, when you're ready to come back and run the national expansion from Charlotte, I'll still be there. Waiting. Supporting. Celebrating every win."

"What if it takes years?" Harper asked quietly.

"Then it takes years. I'm patient." Ethan squeezed her hand. "I'm not asking you to choose between me and your career, Harper. I'm asking you to let me be part of both. To let me show up for your wins. To let me be the person you call when you need to talk through a problem or when you just want to hear a friendly voice. That's all."

Harper felt tears prick her eyes. "That's not a small thing."

"No," Ethan agreed. "But it's what you deserve. What we both deserve."

The yacht was rounding the southern tip of Miami Beach now, and the sun was setting in earnest—a spectacular display of orange and pink and gold that turned the water into liquid fire.

They sat in silence for a while, Harper's head on Ethan's shoulder, his arm around her waist, both of them watching the sky burn.

"Thank you," Harper said finally. "For flying down. For this. For understanding what it means that I need to be here right now."

"Always," Ethan said. "Every win. Every loss. Every moment in between. That's the deal."

"I didn't know that was the deal."

"It is now."

Harper laughed and tilted her head up to kiss him. He tasted like champagne and certainty, like someone who meant every word he said.

When they pulled apart, the sun had dipped below the horizon, leaving just a band of light at the edge of the world.

"Come on," Ethan said, standing and pulling her to her feet. "We have dinner to eat. And I want to hear about every other case you're considering, every problem you're solving, every plan you're making."

"That could take all night," Harper warned.

"Good thing I booked us a hotel room then."

They went down to the main cabin, where Ethan had arranged a full spread from Harper's favorite Miami restaurant—fresh ceviche, grilled mahi, plantains, and key lime pie for dessert. They ate and talked and laughed as the yacht cruised slowly around the bay, the lights of Miami sparkling on all sides.

Harper told him about the medical clinic case, about her plans for hiring additional staff, about the office space she was considering. Ethan told her about his latest architecture project, about mutual friends in Charlotte, about the life that was continuing there without her.

It should have made her sad—hearing about what she was missing. But instead it felt grounding. Like proof that she hadn't disappeared, that she still had ties to people and places that mattered.

"Kendra asked about you," Ethan said over dessert. "She's deep in training for the Games. Barely sleeping, apparently."

"That sounds like her." Harper smiled. "Tell her I said good luck when you see her."

"Tell her yourself. She'd probably appreciate hearing from you."

Harper made a mental note to text Kendra later. It was too easy to disappear into work, to let relationships atrophy from neglect. Having Ethan here reminded her that maintaining connections took effort—but it was effort worth making.

After dinner, they went back up to the deck. The yacht was anchored now, rocking gently in the dark water. The city lights reflected on the surface like scattered stars.

Ethan wrapped a blanket around both of them and pulled Harper close. She fit against him perfectly, her back to his chest, his arms circling her waist.

"This is nice," she murmured.

"It is." She felt him kiss the top of her head. "You should do more of this. Celebrating. Taking time to enjoy what you've built instead of immediately moving to the next thing."

"You sound like Jasper."

"Smart man, Jasper."

Harper laughed softly. "I'm not good at celebrating. It feels like wasted time. Like I should be working on the next problem."

"That's exactly why you need to practice," Ethan said. "Because the problems never end, Harper. There's always going to be another case, another crisis, another impossible situation to solve. If you don't stop to acknowledge the wins, you'll burn out. Or worse—you'll forget why you're doing this in the first place."

Harper was quiet, turning that over. "Why am I doing this?"

"You tell me."

She thought about Ricardo Santiago, about the relief in his voice when she'd texted him. About building solutions that served everyone's interests. About the satisfaction of patient, strategic work.

"Because I'm good at it," she said slowly. "Because I can see patterns other people miss. Because I like solving problems that other people think are unsolvable."

"And?" Ethan prompted.

"And because it matters. Because people like Ricardo deserve to be free of situations they can't escape on their own. Because someone should be fighting for them." Harper paused. "Is that too earnest?"

"It's perfect," Ethan said. "That's exactly why you should be doing this. Just don't forget to let yourself enjoy it too."

They stayed on the deck for another hour, talking and watching the lights, until Harper's eyes started to drift closed and Ethan suggested they head to the hotel.

The captain motored them back to the marina. Ethan settled the bill while Harper waited on the dock, breathing in the salt air and feeling pleasantly exhausted.

"Ready?" Ethan asked, rejoining her with his bag.

"Ready."

They drove to the hotel—a boutique place in South Beach that Ethan had chosen specifically because it was near the restaurant he'd ordered from. The room was elegant and understated, with white linens and a balcony overlooking Ocean Drive.

Harper showered while Ethan ordered room service coffee for the morning, then they collapsed into bed together, still talking, still processing the day.

"Thank you," Harper said again, her head on Ethan's chest. "Really. This was exactly what I needed."

"I know," Ethan said. "That's why I came."

"How did you know?"

"Because I know you. And because Jasper may have mentioned that you'd been working yourself into the ground for three months without a break."

Harper lifted her head to look at him. "Jasper called you?"

"He cares about you. We all do." Ethan tucked a strand of hair behind her ear. "You're allowed to be taken care of sometimes, Harper. You don't have to do everything alone."

Harper felt her throat tighten again. "I'm working on that."

"I know you are." Ethan kissed her forehead. "Get some sleep. We can sleep in tomorrow, get breakfast on the beach, and I'll fly home tomorrow evening."

"You're only staying one night?"

"I have a client meeting Monday morning. But I wanted to be here for this. For your first win in Miami. For the celebration you deserved."

Harper kissed him—slow and deep and full of gratitude and something that might have been love, though she wasn't quite ready to name it yet.

"You're a good man, Ethan Reed."

"I try," he said, smiling against her lips.

They fell asleep tangled together, the sounds of South Beach drifting up from the street below—music and laughter and the pulse of a city that never quite stopped.

And for the first time since she'd moved to Miami, Harper didn't feel lonely.

She felt celebrated. Supported. Seen.

She felt like she'd built something real—not just professionally, but personally too.

Like maybe she could have both.

---

The next morning, they had breakfast at a beachside cafe—strong Cuban coffee, fresh fruit, and tostada that made Ethan groan with pleasure.

"I could get used to this," he said, watching the ocean.

"Don't," Harper said, but she was smiling. "Charlotte needs you. I need you there, holding down the fort until I come back."

"Is that the plan? You're coming back?"

Harper considered the question. "Eventually. When I've built what I came here to build. When BSS Miami can stand on its own. Then yeah—I think I want to come back to Charlotte. Run the national expansion from there. Be closer to..." She trailed off.

"To?" Ethan prompted.

"To the people I care about," Harper finished. "To you."

Ethan's smile could have powered the city. "Good. Because I'll be waiting."

They spent the rest of the morning walking the beach, talking about nothing important, just enjoying being together. Then Ethan had to head to the airport, and Harper drove him back, both of them quieter now as the visit came to an end.

"Thank you for coming," Harper said at the departures curb.

"Thank you for letting me," Ethan said. He kissed her one more time—quick and sweet. "Next win, I'm doing this again. Fair warning."

"I'll hold you to that."

She watched him disappear into the terminal, then drove back to her apartment with the windows down and music playing, feeling lighter than she had in months.

Her phone buzzed with a text from Jasper: *Heard Ethan surprised you. Good man. Enjoy the rest of your weekend. Miami office is officially on the map. Proud of you.*

Harper smiled and typed back: *Thank you. For everything. Including sending Ethan.*

*I didn't send him. He went on his own. But I may have mentioned you could use the support.*

*Noted. I'll try to accept help more gracefully in the future.*

*That would be a first.*

Harper laughed and set her phone down.

The Santiago case was closed. Her first major Miami win was in the books. She had a reputation forming, inquiries coming in, and a clear path forward.

But more than that, she had people who showed up for her. Who celebrated her wins. Who reminded her that success wasn't just about the work—it was about building a life worth celebrating.

That was the real victory, she thought.

Not just solving the impossible problem.

But learning to let herself be celebrated when she did.

---

*End of Chapter 40*
