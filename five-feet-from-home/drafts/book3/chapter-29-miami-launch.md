# Chapter 29: Miami Launch

**POV:** Harper
**Location:** Miami
**Book:** Book 3 - The Expansion

---

The Miami office doesn't look like much yet.

Harper Caldwell stands in the middle of the empty reception area—white walls, polished concrete floors, floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking Biscayne Bay—and tries to feel something other than the hollow ache that's been sitting in her chest since she landed three days ago.

This is everything she wanted. Corner office on the fourteenth floor. Regional head. Her name on the door, on the letterhead, on the business cards that arrived yesterday in a box she hasn't opened yet. Barrett Security Solutions - Miami. Harper Caldwell, Managing Director.

She should be elated.

Instead, she's standing alone in an empty office at 7:00 AM on a Monday, drinking gas station coffee because she hasn't figured out where the good coffee is yet, watching cruise ships drift past her window like slow-moving clouds.

Her phone buzzes. A text from Jasper.

**Jasper:** First day. You good?

She types back quickly.

**Harper:** All set. Office looks great.

**Jasper:** Liar. It's empty and you're freaking out.

Harper laughs despite herself. He knows her too well.

**Harper:** Maybe a little.

**Jasper:** You built this. Now fill it. First hire by end of week.

**Jasper:** And Harper? You earned this.

She sets the phone down and walks to the window. Miami sprawls below her—palm trees and glass towers and traffic already clogging I-95. It's nothing like Charlotte. Nothing like the rhythm she's known for fifteen years.

But that's the point, isn't it? She wanted something new. Something hers.

The intercom buzzes. She crosses to the reception desk and presses the button.

"Ms. Caldwell?" A young woman's voice, bright and efficient. "Your nine o'clock is here. The Reyes family."

Harper checks her watch. It's 8:47. Early. That's either a good sign or a nervous one.

"Send them up," she says.

She has thirteen minutes. She smooths her navy blazer, checks her reflection in the dark window—hair pulled back in a low bun, minimal makeup, diamond studs she bought herself last year—and takes a breath.

This is it. First client call in the new office. First impression. First chance to prove she can do this alone.

When the elevator dings, Harper's already in the doorway of the conference room, hand extended, smile calibrated to project confidence without arrogance.

"Mr. Reyes. Thank you for coming."

Carlos Reyes is in his late fifties, silver-haired and sharp-eyed, wearing a guayabera shirt and expensive shoes. Behind him: a younger man who has to be his son, and a woman in her thirties with the kind of polished composure that comes from years of managing family politics.

"Ms. Caldwell." Carlos's handshake is firm, assessing. "My daughter, Isabela. My son, Mateo."

Harper shakes their hands in turn, gestures them into the conference room. It's sparse—glass table, six chairs, a whiteboard she hasn't written on yet. But the view is spectacular, and she knows how to use that.

"Coffee? Water?" Harper offers.

"Water is fine." Carlos settles into the chair at the head of the table like he owns it. Harper doesn't challenge the power play. Not yet.

She pours four waters from the pitcher she'd set out earlier, distributes them, then takes the seat across from Carlos. Not at the head. Not deferential. Equal.

"So," Harper says, pulling out her tablet. "Your message said you need crisis management services. Discrete, fast, and clean. Why don't you tell me what we're dealing with?"

There's a pause. Isabela and Mateo exchange glances. Carlos leans back, studying Harper like she's a horse he's considering buying.

"How much do you know about our family?" he asks.

"I know you own Reyes Capital Group. Real estate development, hospitality, import-export. Three hotels in South Beach, two in the Keys. Revenue in the mid nine figures." Harper doesn't blink. "I also know your son was arrested in 2019 for assault. Charges dropped. And your daughter was named in a lawsuit involving a property dispute in Coral Gables. Also settled."

Isabela's jaw tightens. Mateo looks impressed. Carlos smiles.

"You do your homework."

"Always." Harper sets down her tablet. "What I don't know is why you need BSS. You have lawyers. You have a PR team. What can I give you that you don't already have?"

Another pause. Then Carlos leans forward.

"Fifteen years ago, I made a deal with some people. Bad people. I was young, needed capital, didn't ask enough questions." His voice is steady, but there's an edge to it. "The business grew. I paid them back. With interest. But they don't want money anymore."

"What do they want?" Harper asks.

"A foothold. Legitimate businesses to run product through. Access to our hotels, our shipping routes." Carlos's hands are flat on the table. "I said no. They said that wasn't an option."

Harper's pulse ticks up, but her face stays calm. This is cartel adjacent. Maybe cartel direct. Exactly the kind of thing BSS exists to handle—but also exactly the kind of thing that can blow up spectacularly if mismanaged.

"When you say 'they,' who are we talking about?" Harper asks.

"Does it matter?" Isabela's voice is cool, controlled. "You know what they are. The question is whether you can help us sever the connection without anyone getting hurt."

Harper looks at her. Isabela Reyes is wearing a cream silk blouse and gold hoops, her nails manicured, her makeup flawless. But there's steel underneath. This is a woman who's been managing family crises for years.

"It matters," Harper says, "because the approach depends on who we're dealing with. Some groups respond to financial incentives. Others require political pressure. Some need to be given an exit that lets them save face."

"And some," Mateo says quietly, "don't respond to anything but force."

Harper holds his gaze. "BSS doesn't use force. We use strategy. If you need force, you need different help."

"We don't want force," Carlos says quickly. "We want out. Clean. Permanent."

Harper picks up her tablet, opens a new file. "Okay. Let's start with specifics. Who approached you initially? What was the original deal? And who's been handling contact since then?"

For the next ninety minutes, Harper takes them through it. Step by step. Detail by detail. Carlos does most of the talking—names, dates, money trails. Isabela fills in the political connections, the local officials who've been bought or compromised. Mateo provides the operational details, the logistics of how the cartel has been using their properties.

Harper listens, asks questions, takes notes. She doesn't react when Carlos mentions a name she recognizes from a DEA briefing she sat in on last year. She doesn't flinch when Isabela describes a threat that was delivered to her daughter's school.

By the time they finish, Harper has a mental map of the situation. It's ugly. Complicated. Dangerous. Exactly the kind of thing Jasper would call a "ten-alarm fire."

But it's also solvable.

"Here's what I'm thinking," Harper says, setting down her tablet. "We need to give them a reason to let you go that's more valuable than keeping you locked in. That means finding an alternative—another business they can use, another partner who's more compliant. We create that option, we broker the handoff, and we make sure everyone walks away whole."

"And if they don't want an alternative?" Mateo asks.

"Then we make staying more expensive than leaving." Harper's voice is calm, almost casual. "We identify their vulnerabilities—financial, legal, operational—and we apply pressure in ways they won't see coming. We make it clear that continuing to target your family will cost them more than it's worth."

Carlos is nodding slowly. "You've done this before."

"Variations of it. Different players, same dynamics." Harper meets his eyes. "But I need to be clear about something. This is high-risk. If we move wrong, if we push too hard or too soft, people could get hurt. I can't guarantee a clean exit. I can only guarantee that we'll use every tool we have to get you there."

"And what tools do you have?" Isabela asks.

Harper smiles. It's not a warm smile. It's the smile of someone who's spent fifteen years learning how power really works.

"I have relationships with federal agencies who would love to build a case against the people you're dealing with. I have contacts in finance who can freeze assets without leaving fingerprints. I have political connections who can apply pressure in ways that don't show up in the news." She pauses. "And I have a team that's very, very good at making problems disappear."

The room is quiet.

Then Carlos extends his hand across the table.

"When can you start?"

Harper shakes his hand. "I already have."

---

They leave twenty minutes later, after Harper walks them through next steps, timeline, and fees. The fees make Isabela blink, but Carlos doesn't hesitate. He writes a check for the retainer—two hundred fifty thousand dollars—and slides it across the table.

"I'll have contracts to you by end of day," Harper says. "In the meantime, I need you to do two things. First, business as usual. Don't change your patterns, don't avoid contact if they reach out. We need them comfortable."

"And second?" Mateo asks.

"Second, I need access to everything. Financial records, communication logs, any documentation of your dealings with them. The more I know, the better I can protect you."

"You'll have it," Isabela says.

After they're gone, Harper stands in the empty conference room, holding the check. Two hundred fifty thousand dollars. Her first client. Her first major case in Miami.

Classic Miami, she thinks. Cuban business family with cartel connections they want to sever. High-stakes, high-risk, high-reward.

She should call Jasper. Update him. Loop in Addison and Cole for strategy support.

But first, she just stands there for a moment, looking out at the bay.

This is where she belongs. Not in Charlotte, managing other people's accounts, sitting in meetings where her voice gets softered by seniority and politics. Here. Building something from scratch. Taking cases that matter. Making decisions that are hers alone.

It's lonelier than she expected.

But it's also exactly what she wanted.

Harper pulls out her phone and texts Jasper.

**Harper:** First client signed. Cartel-adjacent family business. Needs full extraction and threat neutralization.

**Harper:** I'm going to need Cole on this one. And maybe Rafe.

The response comes fast.

**Jasper:** Already texted Cole. He's booking a flight.

**Jasper:** And Harper? Welcome to Miami. Now go build an empire.

Harper smiles. She tucks the check into her bag, grabs her coffee, and walks back to her office. There's a hire to make, a case to plan, and a whole city to conquer.

She's not lonely. She's focused.

And for the first time in months, that feels like enough.

---

By noon, Harper has a list.

She's sitting at her desk—minimalist, glass-topped, facing the window—with her laptop open and three browser tabs running. One has job listings she's posted on LinkedIn and Indeed. One has background checks running on candidates she's identified from her network. One has a tactical breakdown of the Reyes case, color-coded by risk level.

Her phone rings. Unknown number, Miami area code.

"Harper Caldwell."

"Ms. Caldwell, this is Detective Sarah Vega, Miami-Dade PD." The voice is professional, clipped. "I understand you recently opened a regional office for Barrett Security Solutions."

Harper's instincts prick up. "That's correct. How can I help you, Detective?"

"I'd like to have a conversation. Off the record. About some mutual interests."

"What kind of mutual interests?"

"The kind that involve families trying to get out from under very bad arrangements." A pause. "I heard you took a meeting this morning with the Reyes family."

Harper's jaw tightens. Word travels fast in Miami. Too fast.

"I don't discuss client matters," Harper says carefully.

"I'm not asking you to. I'm offering to help." Vega's tone shifts, becomes less formal. "Look, I've been working organized crime in Miami for eight years. I know the players. I know the patterns. And I know the Reyes family has been trying to extricate themselves for the last two years without success."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because if you're serious about helping them, you're going to need someone who understands the local landscape. Someone who can tell you which federal agents are worth talking to and which ones are on the take. Someone who knows where the bodies are buried." Another pause. "Literally."

Harper considers. This could be a trap. It could also be exactly what she needs.

"Coffee," Harper says. "Public place. Tomorrow morning."

"Panther Coffee. Wynwood. 8:00 AM."

"I'll be there."

The line goes dead.

Harper sets down her phone and stares out at the bay. This is how it works in a new city. You build your network fast, or you drown. The question is whether Detective Vega is someone she can trust, or someone who's going to make this case exponentially more complicated.

She makes a note to have Cole run a background check. Then she goes back to her hiring list.

By 3:00 PM, she has her first interview scheduled. A former FBI analyst named Marcus Chen who left the bureau after a decade to do private consulting. His resume is impressive—counterintelligence, financial crimes, fluent in Mandarin and Spanish. Harper found him through a mutual contact in DC.

She spends an hour prepping for the interview, reviewing his file, formulating questions. At 4:00, her assistant—a temp she hired through an agency until she can find someone permanent—buzzes her.

"Ms. Caldwell? Mr. Chen is here."

"Send him in."

Marcus Chen is mid-thirties, wearing slacks and a button-down with the sleeves rolled to his elbows. He has an easy smile and sharp eyes. Harper likes him immediately.

"Mr. Chen. Thanks for coming in on short notice."

"Call me Marcus." He shakes her hand, settles into the chair across from her desk. "And thank you for the opportunity. I've been following BSS for a while. Jasper Barrett does good work."

"He does," Harper agrees. "Which is why I need someone equally good here. Talk to me about your time at the bureau. Why'd you leave?"

Marcus doesn't hesitate. "Bureaucracy. Red tape. Cases that should have taken three months stretched to three years because of interagency politics. I got tired of being effective on paper and useless in practice."

"And what do you want to do here?"

"Be useful." He leans forward slightly. "I'm good at finding things people don't want found. Money trails, communication patterns, vulnerabilities. I'm also good at building cases that hold up under scrutiny. If you're building a Miami office, you're going to need both."

Harper studies him. "What do you know about the local landscape? Cartels, organized crime, political corruption?"

"Enough to know it's a minefield. Enough to know you can't navigate it without local contacts and federal cooperation." Marcus's expression is serious now. "I still have friends at the bureau. Some at DEA. They'd talk to me in ways they won't talk to a private firm."

"And if I hired you, you'd use those relationships on behalf of BSS?"

"That's exactly what I'd do."

Harper makes a decision. "You're hired. Pending background check and references. Salary negotiations can happen tomorrow. But I need you to start immediately. Today, if possible."

Marcus blinks. "Today?"

"I have a case that needs your skill set. High-risk extraction, cartel-adjacent. I need research, financial analysis, and federal contacts. Can you do it?"

Marcus grins. "I can absolutely do it."

"Then welcome to BSS Miami." Harper extends her hand. "Let's get to work."

---

That night, Harper sits alone in her apartment.

It's a corporate rental—furnished, bland, located in Brickell because she hasn't had time to find something permanent. The view is nice. The furniture is forgettable. The kitchen is stocked with takeout containers and bottled water.

She orders Thai food and eats it standing at the counter, scrolling through her phone. There are texts from Jasper—congratulations on the hire, requests for updates. A message from Addison asking how she's settling in. One from Elena inviting her to a fundraiser next month in Charlotte.

Nothing personal. Nothing that isn't tied to work or professional obligation.

Harper thinks about calling someone. A friend. A former colleague. Anyone who might want to hear about her first day, her first client, the strange exhilaration and terror of starting over at forty-three.

But the list of people who fit that description is shorter than it should be.

She thinks about Charlotte. About the rhythm of her life there—early morning runs with Maddie and the wives' circle, Sunday dinners at the Barrett house, the easy familiarity of knowing which coffee shop had the best espresso and which dry cleaner didn't over-starch her shirts.

She thinks about the things she left behind when she took this job. The safety of the known. The comfort of proximity to people who cared about her.

But she also thinks about the things she gained. Autonomy. Authority. The chance to prove she could build something on her own terms.

Harper finishes her Thai food and throws away the container. She pours a glass of wine and stands at her window, looking out at the Miami skyline.

This is everything she wanted.

And it's lonelier than expected.

But Harper Caldwell didn't build a career by backing down when things got hard. She built it by showing up, doing the work, and proving she belonged in rooms where people didn't think she should be.

Miami is just another room. And she's going to own it.

Her phone buzzes. A text from an unknown number.

**Unknown:** This is Sarah Vega. Looking forward to coffee tomorrow. You're going to need all the help you can get.

Harper reads the message twice, then saves the number.

She doesn't respond. Not yet.

Tomorrow, she'll have coffee with a detective who might be an ally or a liability. She'll dive deeper into the Reyes case. She'll keep building her team, her network, her presence in this city.

But tonight, she stands at her window and lets herself feel it. The loneliness. The uncertainty. The terrifying, exhilarating truth that this is hers now. All of it. The wins and the losses. The successes and the failures.

Harper smiles.

This is where she belongs.

Classic Miami.

And she's ready.
