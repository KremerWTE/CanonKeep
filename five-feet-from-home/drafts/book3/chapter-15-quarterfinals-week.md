# Chapter 15: Quarterfinals Week

**POV: Kendra Barrett**

The garage gym at 5:47 AM was already warm. Not the oppressive heat of mid-summer, but the creeping warmth that promised another ninety-degree day in Charlotte. Kendra stood in front of her phone, positioned on a tripod at the regulation distance, and tried to slow her breathing.

Quarterfinals. Day one. Workout one.

Four workouts over four days. All filmed. All submitted for judging. The top seventy-five from North America would advance to Semifinals. After that, only thirty would make it to the Games.

She'd been preparing for three months. Every morning at dawn. Every evening after work. Her body was the strongest it had ever been. Her nutrition dialed in. Her sleep optimized.

But standing here, alone in the garage with nothing but a barbell and a camera, Kendra felt the familiar whisper of doubt.

What if she wasn't good enough?

What if three months of perfect training still left her short?

What if—

Her phone alarm went off. 5:50 AM. Time to start the recording.

"Get your head right," she muttered. Then louder, channeling Coach Sarah: "You've done the work. Now do the workout."

She hit record.

The workout was brutal in its simplicity: A twelve-minute AMRAP — as many rounds as possible. Ten snatches at 95 pounds. Ten burpees over the bar. Repeat until time runs out.

Kendra had done this workout twice in practice. She knew she could get eight rounds. Maybe nine if everything clicked.

She needed nine to be competitive.

The timer beeped. Kendra approached the barbell, chalk dust coating her hands. First rep. The bar felt light. Good. She moved through the snatches with the form she'd drilled ten thousand times. Hips back. Bar close. Drive through the heels. Lock out overhead.

Ten reps. Smooth.

Burpees next. Over the bar and back. Keep the pace. Don't redline too early. This was a sprint disguised as a marathon — twelve minutes felt short until you were seven minutes in and tasting copper.

Round one done. 10:47 remaining.

Round two. The snatches were still smooth. Muscle memory. Her body knew this movement so deeply that her brain could almost detach, watch from a distance.

Except she couldn't afford to detach. Not with a camera recording every rep. Every lockout had to be clean. Every burpee had to clear the bar. One questionable rep and the judges would no-rep her, invalidate the whole workout.

Round three. 8:23 remaining.

The breathing got harder. The chalk on her hands felt gritty, mixed with sweat. She wiped her palms on her shorts between snatches, trying to maintain grip.

Round four. Round five.

Halfway through round six, her legs started to burn. Not the good burn of muscles working. The bad burn. The you're-going-too-fast-and-you're-going-to-pay-for-it burn.

Kendra gritted her teeth and kept moving. Six rounds done. 4:12 remaining.

Three more rounds would put her at nine. Three more rounds would be competitive.

Except her grip was slipping. Literally. The barbell felt slick despite the chalk. She had to reset between reps, shake out her hands.

Precious seconds ticking away.

Round seven was ugly. The snatches were still technically sound, but they looked effortful now. No more smooth lockouts. Each rep was a negotiation with gravity.

The burpees were worse. Her chest hit the ground like deadweight. She had to push herself up with everything she had.

Round seven done. 2:03 remaining.

Two minutes. One more full round plus whatever she could get into a ninth round.

Kendra approached the bar for round eight and felt her hands start to slip on the very first rep. She saved it — barely — but the miss cost her momentum.

Focus. Breath. One rep at a time.

Eight snatches. Nine. Ten.

Burpees. She was moving on autopilot now, muscle memory overriding the screaming in her lungs.

Round eight complete. 0:47 remaining.

Forty-seven seconds. Maybe six snatches. Maybe seven if she was fast.

Kendra grabbed the barbell and started moving. No rest. No strategy. Just reps.

One. Two. Three. Four.

Her grip failed on the fifth rep. The bar crashed down. She reset, grabbed it again.

Five. Six.

Twenty seconds.

She went for the seventh rep. Got it overhead. Locked out.

Ten seconds.

She dropped the bar, dove to the ground for a burpee. Hit her chest. Pushed up. Jumped.

The timer beeped.

Eight rounds plus seven reps.

Kendra collapsed on the garage floor, chest heaving, vision spotty. Her heart was trying to escape her ribcage. Her hands were shaking.

But she'd done it. Eight rounds plus seven. Solid. Maybe not podium-worthy, but competitive.

She lay there for ninety seconds, letting her heart rate come down, before pushing herself up to stop the recording. The camera had captured everything. Now she just had to submit it.

And then do it all again tomorrow.

And the next day.

And the day after that.

---

Chris showed up at lunchtime with recovery shakes and foam rollers. He let himself in through the side door, found Kendra on the couch with ice packs on her shoulders.

"How'd it go?" he asked.

"Eight plus seven."

"That's good, right?"

"It's competitive. Not great. But competitive." She accepted the shake he handed her. Some green concoction that tasted like grass and determination. "What's in this?"

"Spinach, banana, protein powder, collagen, tart cherry juice." He sat down beside her. "Sarah sent me the recipe. Said you need the anti-inflammatory properties."

Kendra took another sip. It tasted terrible. She drank it anyway.

"You don't have to do this," she said. "Bring me stuff. Check on me. I can manage."

"I know you can manage. But you don't have to manage alone." Chris picked up her hand, examined her palm. Torn calluses. Broken skin. "Jesus, Kendra."

"It's normal."

"It looks like you've been holding onto razor blades."

"Basically." She pulled her hand back gently. "It'll heal. It always does."

Chris was quiet for a moment. He did that sometimes — went internal, processing thoughts he wasn't ready to articulate. Kendra had learned to wait him out.

"I've been thinking," he said finally. "About what happens if you make it to Semifinals."

"When I make it to Semifinals."

"Right. When." He smiled. "But seriously. Semifinals are in May. In Texas. You'd be gone for a week. And if you make it to the Games after that, you're talking about another week in August."

"I know the schedule."

"I'm not complaining. I just want to understand what we're building toward. Is this a one-time thing? Proving you can do it? Or is this going to be your life for the next few years?"

It was a fair question. One Kendra had been avoiding.

"I don't know," she said honestly. "A year ago, I didn't think I'd even try Quarterfinals. Six months ago, I thought I'd do it once and get it out of my system. But now..."

"Now what?"

"Now I think I might be good at this. Not just gym-good. Actually competitive." She set down the recovery shake. "And I don't know what to do with that."

"You could go all in. Train full-time. See how far you can go."

"I have a job, Chris."

"You could get a different job. Something with more flexibility. Or you could not work for a while. Focus on this completely."

Kendra stared at him. "Are you serious?"

"Why not? We can afford it. And if this is something you want—"

"I can't just quit my job to play CrossFit athlete."

"Why not?"

"Because that's not how adults work. We have responsibilities. Bills. Careers."

"We have money," Chris countered. "And careers can wait. But your athletic prime won't. You're twenty-nine. If you're going to do this, it needs to be now."

Kendra stood up, paced to the window. Outside, the neighborhood was quiet. Weekday afternoon. Everyone at work except her. She'd taken the week off for Quarterfinals — four days of vacation time to chase a dream that might evaporate the moment she submitted her scores.

"What if I'm not good enough?" she asked quietly.

"Then you're not. But at least you'll know." Chris came to stand beside her. "Look, I'm not saying you have to decide right now. But if you make Semifinals, we should talk seriously about what the next year looks like. Because if you're going to compete at that level, you can't do it part-time."

"Your mother would lose her mind if I quit my job to become a CrossFit athlete."

"My mother doesn't get a vote."

Kendra turned to look at him. Chris was usually so careful about family dynamics, so aware of his position as the heir apparent. Hearing him dismiss Elena's opinion so casually was startling.

"Okay, who are you and what did you do with my husband?"

He smiled. "I'm the guy who's watching his wife become something extraordinary. And I don't want to be the reason she doesn't get there."

Kendra felt something crack open in her chest. Not fear. Not doubt. Something else.

Permission.

"I need to get through this week first," she said. "One workout at a time."

"Agreed. But after? We talk."

"After, we talk."

---

Workout two dropped at 8 PM. Kendra was on her phone the moment the announcement went live, reading the description with growing dread.

Twenty-minute AMRAP. A chipper-style workout — a long list of movements, done once through, as many rounds as possible.

40 wall balls. 30 toes-to-bar. 20 box jump-overs. 10 thrusters at 95 pounds. Then repeat.

This was her weakness. Long, grinding workouts that required sustained aerobic capacity. She was strong. She was powerful. But twenty minutes of constant movement? That was a different game.

"You've got this," Chris said, reading over her shoulder.

"I hate wall balls."

"Everyone hates wall balls."

"I especially hate wall balls."

She went to bed at nine, set her alarm for 5:30 AM, and lay awake staring at the ceiling until midnight, mentally rehearsing the workout. Pacing strategies. Breathing patterns. Where to push. Where to back off.

When the alarm went off the next morning, she felt like she'd been hit by a truck.

But she got up anyway. Set up the camera. Loaded the barbell. Put the medicine ball in position.

This was day two. Three more to go.

She could do hard things.

She'd been doing hard things her whole life.

---

The workout was worse than she'd anticipated. Not because the movements were difficult — they were, but she'd expected that. Worse because the volume was crushing. Forty wall balls felt like a hundred. Thirty toes-to-bar destroyed her grip. The box jumps turned her legs to concrete.

By the time she got to the thrusters — the very last movement before the round repeated — Kendra was moving through mud. Every rep was a battle.

She finished one full round with 13:42 remaining.

Six and a half minutes left. Enough time for most of another round if she could keep moving.

Except her body was staging a revolt. Her shoulders were screaming from the wall balls. Her abs were shredded from the toes-to-bar. Her legs were threatening to quit entirely.

Kendra picked up the medicine ball and started the second round.

Forty wall balls. She broke them into sets of ten, resting between each set longer than she should have. But if she didn't rest, she'd fail reps. And failed reps meant wasted energy.

Thirty toes-to-bar. Her grip was already compromised. She got through fifteen before having to drop from the bar. Shook out her hands. Jumped back up. Ten more. Rest. The last five felt like hanging from broken glass.

Twenty box jump-overs. Simple. Brutal. Just keep jumping. Don't think about the burning in your quads. Don't think about the time ticking away.

She finished the box jumps with 3:47 remaining.

Three minutes and forty-seven seconds for ten thrusters.

Doable. Barely.

Kendra approached the barbell and nearly cried when she tried to lift it. Her shoulders had nothing left. The bar that had felt manageable fifteen minutes ago now felt like it weighed three hundred pounds.

She got the first thruster. Barely locked it out overhead.

Second rep. Third.

Her legs were shaking. The front squat portion of the thruster was the easy part — it was the overhead press that was killing her. Every rep required a full-body effort just to get the bar overhead.

Fourth rep. Fifth.

Two minutes remaining.

Six. Seven.

Her vision was tunneling. She could hear her heartbeat in her ears, a frantic drum that drowned out everything else.

Eighth rep. Ninth.

Thirty seconds.

She went for the tenth thruster, got it to her shoulders, drove out of the squat, pressed overhead—

And the bar stopped six inches from lockout.

Her shoulders had quit. Just completely given up.

Kendra lowered the bar, reset, tried again.

Drove it up. Pressed. Locked out.

The timer beeped.

One round plus twenty-nine reps.

Kendra dropped the barbell and collapsed. Not dramatically. Not like in the movies. She just sat down hard on the garage floor and put her head between her knees, trying not to vomit.

One round plus twenty-nine.

That was bad. Really bad. Most competitive athletes would get at least one full round, maybe into a third round. She'd barely scraped through round two.

"Fuck," she said to the empty garage.

Her phone buzzed. Chris: *How'd it go?*

Kendra stared at the message for a long time before responding. *Not good. I'll tell you later.*

She submitted the score anyway. What else was she going to do? Quit? After one bad workout?

But as she showered and got ready for work — she'd burned through her vacation days and had to go in today — Kendra felt the dream slipping away.

One bad workout could tank her entire Quarterfinals. One bad workout could be the difference between advancing and going home.

And she had two more workouts to go.

---

Workout three was a couplet: power cleans and ring muscle-ups. Eight-minute AMRAP.

Kendra's specialty. Barbell cycling and gymnastics. This was where she could make up ground.

She attacked it with everything she had. The power cleans felt smooth, her grip recovered from yesterday's disaster. The ring muscle-ups were crisp, efficient.

She finished with six rounds plus fifteen reps. Solidly competitive. Maybe top twenty in her region if she was lucky.

One good workout. One disaster. One solid performance.

Everything came down to workout four.

---

The final workout was announced on Thursday night. Kendra read the description three times, not quite believing it.

A one-rep max snatch. Followed immediately by a max-effort handstand walk.

Two separate tests. Strength and skill. Combined score.

This was her wheelhouse. This was everything she'd been training for years.

Friday morning, Kendra woke up at 5 AM without an alarm. Her body knew. This was the day.

Chris was already awake, sitting in the kitchen with coffee.

"Big day," he said.

"Biggest."

"You nervous?"

"Terrified."

"Good. That means it matters." He handed her a protein shake. "What's your target?"

"185 on the snatch. Maybe 190 if everything clicks."

"And the handstand walk?"

"As far as I can go without falling." Kendra smiled. "Probably a hundred feet. Maybe more."

"You've got this."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it's true." Chris stood, kissed her forehead. "Go prove it."

---

The snatch felt good from the first warmup rep. Kendra worked up slowly: 95 pounds. 115. 135. 155. 175.

At 185, she paused. This was her current max. She'd hit it twice in training. Anything beyond this was new territory.

She set up for the lift. Took three deep breaths. Grabbed the bar.

Pull. Catch. Stand.

185 locked out overhead. Clean. Solid.

The judges would count it.

But Kendra wasn't done.

She loaded 190 on the bar. Five more pounds. A new personal record.

This rep felt different. Heavier. The bar speed was slower on the pull. She had to fight for the lockout.

But she got it. 190 pounds.

New PR.

She tried 195. Missed it. Tried again. Missed again.

190 was her max for today.

Good enough.

The handstand walk was next. Kendra kicked up into a handstand and started walking. Fifty feet. Seventy-five. One hundred.

Her shoulders were burning. The garage floor seemed to stretch on forever.

One hundred twenty feet. One hundred thirty.

She felt herself starting to wobble. Overcorrected. Lost balance.

Came down at 137 feet.

Kendra stood up, checked the measurement. 137 feet. Combined with the 190-pound snatch, that was a competitive score. Maybe not elite. But competitive.

She'd done everything she could.

---

The scores posted on Sunday. Kendra refreshed the leaderboard every thirty seconds from 8 AM until they went live at noon.

When the results finally loaded, she couldn't look. Made Chris check first.

"Where am I?" she asked, eyes closed.

"Twenty-third."

Kendra's eyes snapped open. "What?"

"Twenty-third. In North America. You're going to Semifinals."

She grabbed the phone. There it was. Her name. Twenty-third place. Well inside the top seventy-five cutoff.

Semifinals bound.

"I did it," she said quietly. Then louder: "I fucking did it."

Chris picked her up, spun her around. "You did it."

"I'm going to Semifinals."

"You're going to Semifinals."

Kendra started laughing. Then crying. Then laughing again. Four days of brutal workouts. Four days of doubt and pain and wondering if she was good enough.

And she was. She was good enough.

Her phone started buzzing. Texts from Coach Sarah. From gym friends. From people she'd competed against in local competitions.

But the text that made her pause was from Elena: *Congratulations on making Semifinals. I'd like to take you to lunch this week to discuss what comes next.*

Kendra stared at the message. Elena never reached out to her directly. Everything went through Chris.

What comes next.

That was the question, wasn't it?

She'd made Semifinals. Proven she could compete at a regional level. But did she want to keep going? Could she keep going?

And if she did — what would that cost?

"Chris," she said slowly. "We need to have that talk."

"About going all in?"

"Yeah. About going all in." She looked at him. "Because I think I want to. I think I want to see how far I can go."

"Then let's figure out how to make that happen."

Kendra nodded. Pulled up her calendar. Started thinking about what the next six months would look like if she actually committed to this.

Semifinals in May. If she made the Games, competition in August. Which meant six months of full-time training. Six months of meal prep and recovery protocols and video analysis.

Six months of choosing this over everything else.

It was terrifying.

It was exactly what she wanted.

"Okay," she said. "Let's do it. Let's actually do this."

Chris smiled. Pulled her close. "You're going to be amazing."

"I'm going to try to be amazing. There's a difference."

"Not from where I'm standing."

Kendra leaned into him, let herself feel the weight of what she'd just decided. This wasn't just about CrossFit. It was about choosing herself. Choosing her goals. Choosing to take up space and demand support and go after something that mattered to her.

For years, she'd been Chris's wife. Elena's daughter-in-law. The woman who fit into the Barrett family structure.

But starting today, she was also something else.

An athlete. A competitor. Someone who belonged on that leaderboard.

And she wasn't done yet.

Not even close.
