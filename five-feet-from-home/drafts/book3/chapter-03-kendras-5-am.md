# Chapter 3: Kendra's 5 AM

**POV: Kendra Mills**

The alarm went off at 4:45 AM.

Kendra silenced it before the second beep, already awake, already alert. She'd learned years ago that the difference between good athletes and great athletes wasn't talent—it was the willingness to get up when everyone else was sleeping.

She slid out of bed, moving quietly through her apartment in the pre-dawn darkness. Sports bra, workout shorts, CrossFit shoes. Hair pulled back in a tight ponytail. Water bottle filled. Pre-workout mixed and ready.

By 4:55, she was in her car, driving through empty Charlotte streets toward the gym.

The CrossFit Open started in six weeks. Six weeks to prove she belonged at the next level. Six weeks to show that all the early mornings, all the sacrifices, all the pain meant something.

Six weeks to qualify for the Games.

---

The gym was already lit up when she arrived, warm light spilling out into the cold February morning. Kendra parked and grabbed her bag, feeling the familiar anticipation build in her chest.

This was her favorite time. Before the world woke up, before the office, before the emails and the meetings and the endless demands. Just her, the equipment, and the work.

Inside, a handful of other athletes were already warming up. Kendra recognized most of them—the core group of competitive CrossFitters who trained here, the ones who showed up at ungodly hours because they wanted more than casual fitness.

"Kendra!" Coach Marcus called from across the gym. He was setting up the rowing machines, programming workout stations. "You ready to suffer?"

"Always."

Marcus grinned. He was in his forties, a former Navy SEAL who'd found CrossFit after retirement and never looked back. He trained a small group of competitive athletes separately from the regular class schedule, pushing them toward Games-level performance.

Kendra was his latest project.

"Okay, listen up," Marcus said, gathering the group. Eight athletes total, ranging from early twenties to late thirties. All lean, all focused, all here for the same reason. "Open starts in six weeks. That means we're shifting from strength building to competition prep. We're working on speed, efficiency, mental toughness. Every workout from now until the Open is designed to simulate competition conditions."

He gestured to the whiteboard where today's workout was written:

*5 Rounds For Time:*
*500m Row*
*15 Thrusters (95/65 lbs)*
*15 Pull-ups*

Kendra read it and felt her stomach tighten. It looked simple on the board. It wouldn't be.

"Goal time is under fifteen minutes," Marcus continued. "That's the standard. If you want to compete with the best, you have to hit these numbers."

Fifteen minutes. Kendra did the math in her head. That meant each round in under three minutes. Possible, but brutal.

"Questions?" Marcus asked.

No one spoke. They all knew what was coming.

"Good. Five minutes to warm up, then we go."

---

Kendra moved through her warm-up routine methodically. Dynamic stretching, activation drills, practice reps with the barbell. She'd done this thousands of times, but she never skipped steps. Warm-up was where you prevented injury, where you prepared your body for the violence about to come.

She loaded her barbell—sixty-five pounds plus the bar. The weight felt manageable in her hands during warm-up. It wouldn't feel manageable during the workout.

At 5:30 exactly, Marcus called time.

"Stations ready. On my count."

Kendra positioned herself at her rowing machine, gripped the handle, set her feet in the straps.

"Three, two, one... go!"

She exploded into the first row, pulling hard and fast. The monitor lit up—split time, distance, calories. She focused on the meters, watching them tick down. 500, 450, 400.

Her breathing was controlled. Powerful pulls, legs driving, core engaged. This was the easy part. Row before the barbell, before the pull-ups, before the fatigue set in.

She hit 500 meters in 1:42. Good time. She jumped off the rower and moved to the barbell.

Thrusters. The movement that separated the tough from the toughest. Squat down with the bar, stand up, press overhead. Fifteen times. Simple and devastating.

Kendra gripped the bar and began. One, two, three. The weight moved smoothly at first. Five, six, seven. Her breathing got heavier. Ten, eleven, twelve. Her shoulders started burning. Thirteen, fourteen, fifteen.

Bar down. Move to pull-ups.

She jumped to the bar, started pulling. Chin over, drop down, pull again. Her arms were already tired from the thrusters, and she felt it in every rep. But she kept moving. Fifteen pull-ups, unbroken.

Round one complete. Time: 2:48.

Four more rounds to go.

---

By round three, Kendra was in the pain cave.

Her legs felt like concrete during the rows. Her shoulders screamed during the thrusters. Her grip was failing on the pull-ups.

But she didn't stop.

This was the point where mental toughness mattered more than physical strength. The point where you either pushed through or gave up. The point where champions were forged.

She thought about Tia-Clair Toomey, the seven-time CrossFit Games champion. She'd watched hours of Tia's competition footage, studying her movements, her efficiency, her mental game. Tia never looked defeated, never looked like she was suffering even when she had to be dying inside.

Kendra tried to channel that. Tried to keep her face neutral, her movements smooth, her breathing controlled.

Row. Thruster. Pull-up. Repeat.

Round four. Her vision started to tunnel. She could hear Marcus shouting encouragement, could hear the other athletes grunting and breathing, but it all felt distant. Everything narrowed to the next rep, the next movement, the next breath.

By round five, she was running on pure will.

The row felt like swimming through sand. The thrusters felt like moving mountains. The pull-ups felt impossible.

But she finished.

She dropped from the pull-up bar, collapsed onto the floor, chest heaving.

"Time!" Marcus called. "Fourteen-oh-three!"

Kendra lay on her back, staring at the ceiling, trying to remember how to breathe normally. Fourteen minutes, three seconds. Under the goal time.

Marcus appeared above her, grinning. "That's the standard right there. Good work."

She managed a thumbs up.

Around her, other athletes were finishing, some under fifteen minutes, some over. Everyone looked destroyed. Everyone had given everything.

This was why they came at 5 AM. Not for the glory or the recognition, but for moments like this. The moments that proved they could do hard things.

---

By seven AM, Kendra was showered, dressed in business casual, and sitting in a coffee shop reviewing market research for BSS.

This was her double life. The one most people didn't see.

From 5 to 7 AM, she was an athlete. Training, pushing, suffering. From 8 AM to 6 PM, she was an analyst. Reading financial reports, building models, supporting client engagements.

The two worlds didn't overlap. At the gym, no one knew she worked in strategy consulting. At the office, most people just knew her as "the CrossFit girl."

She'd tried to integrate them, tried to be fully herself in both places. But it never quite worked. The athletes didn't understand the corporate world. The corporate people didn't understand the athletic obsession.

So she compartmentalized. Gym Kendra and Office Kendra. Two different people sharing one exhausted body.

Her laptop was open to a market analysis she was building for a healthcare client. She'd been working on it for three days, analyzing competitive positioning, identifying growth opportunities. It was good work—detailed, thorough, strategically sound.

But her mind kept drifting back to the workout. To the feeling of finishing under time. To Marcus's nod of approval.

At the gym, success was measurable. Fifteen minutes or fourteen minutes. Forty pull-ups or fifty. First place or tenth.

At work, success was murkier. Client satisfaction. Quality of analysis. Potential for advancement. All important, all valuable, but less tangible somehow.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Marcus: *Solid work this morning. Keep that pace through the Open and you're qualifying.*

Kendra smiled, felt warmth spread through her chest. Then immediately felt guilty for caring more about that text than the email from her boss praising her recent client work.

This was the tension. The constant pull between two identities, two definitions of success.

She forced herself back to the laptop, to the market analysis. She had a meeting at nine to present her findings. She needed to be sharp, prepared, professional.

Office Kendra. Not Gym Kendra.

She took a sip of coffee and got back to work.

---

The presentation went well.

Kendra walked the client through her analysis, highlighted key opportunities, answered questions with confidence. The senior strategist leading the engagement—a guy named Ryan who'd been with BSS for five years—nodded approvingly throughout.

After the client left, Ryan pulled her aside.

"Good work in there. Really solid analysis."

"Thanks."

"You're picking this up fast. Most analysts take six months to get comfortable with client-facing work. You're doing it in three."

Kendra felt a flush of pride. "I'm just trying to add value."

"You are. Jasper's noticed too. He mentioned you might be ready for more independent work soon."

"Really?"

"Really. Keep this up, and you could be looking at junior strategist in a year or two instead of three."

After Ryan left, Kendra sat at her desk, processing this. Junior strategist. Faster track than normal. Recognition for her work, not just her athletic achievements.

This was what she wanted, right? To be valued for her mind, not just her body. To prove she could compete in the business world the same way she competed in CrossFit.

So why did it feel complicated?

She pulled out her phone, opened Instagram. Her feed was full of CrossFit athletes—people she followed, people she competed against, people she aspired to be like. Tia-Clair Toomey posted a video of a heavy snatch session. Another Games athlete posted their Open prep strategy.

Kendra scrolled through, feeling the familiar pull. The desire to be there, fully committed, chasing the Games with everything she had.

But she was also here. In an office. Building a career. Doing work that mattered.

Could she really do both? Or was she just doing two things at seventy percent instead of one thing at a hundred?

---

She stayed late at the office that night, catching up on work she'd missed while in client meetings. By the time she left, it was past seven. The gym's evening class would be wrapping up soon.

Kendra drove there anyway.

She found Marcus in his office, reviewing programming for the next week.

"Didn't expect to see you back tonight," he said.

"I wanted to ask you something."

"Shoot."

Kendra leaned against the doorframe. "You think I can qualify for the Games?"

Marcus looked at her for a long moment. "Based on what I've seen? Physically, yes. You have the strength, the endurance, the skills. Your numbers are competitive."

"But?"

"But the Games isn't just about physical ability. It's about commitment. The athletes who make it are the ones who make it their whole life. Training is their job. Recovery is their priority. They structure everything around performance."

"And I don't."

"You can't. You have another job. You're splitting focus." Marcus leaned back in his chair. "That doesn't mean you can't compete at a high level. But if you're asking if you can qualify for the Games while working full-time as an analyst? Honestly, I don't know. I haven't seen anyone do it."

Kendra nodded slowly. She'd known this, deep down. But hearing it out loud made it real.

"What if I committed fully?" she asked. "What if I quit the analyst job and just trained?"

"Then you'd probably qualify. You'd also be broke and unemployed if the athletic career didn't pan out."

"So I'm stuck."

"You're not stuck. You're choosing. There's a difference." Marcus stood, walked over to her. "Look, Kendra. You're one of the hardest workers I've ever coached. You show up, you push yourself, you don't make excuses. But at some point, you're going to have to decide what you want more. Not because you can't do both, but because excellence in either requires sacrifice. And right now, you're sacrificing excellence in both to be good at both."

The words hit harder than any workout.

"What would you do?" Kendra asked.

"I can't answer that. But I can tell you what I've seen: The people who are happiest are the ones who know what they're chasing and commit to it fully. They don't hedge. They don't keep backup plans. They choose and they go all in."

---

Kendra drove home in silence, Marcus's words echoing in her head.

*You're sacrificing excellence in both to be good at both.*

Was that true? Was she settling for good instead of pursuing great?

At home, she made dinner—chicken and vegetables, precisely measured for her macros. She ate while watching videos of Tia-Clair Toomey competing. Studied her movement patterns, her transitions, her efficiency.

Tia made it look effortless. But Kendra knew the truth: Tia trained eight hours a day. She had sponsors, coaches, a whole team supporting her. CrossFit was her job, her life, her entire focus.

Kendra had two hours in the morning and maybe two at night. She had a full-time job that paid her bills and built a future. She had responsibilities beyond the gym.

She couldn't be Tia. The math didn't work.

But maybe she didn't have to be Tia. Maybe she could be Kendra—the athlete who also had a career, who competed at a high level while building something sustainable.

It wouldn't get her to the Games. But it might get her somewhere else. Somewhere uniquely hers.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Harper: *How was your day? Still alive after deadlifts?*

Kendra smiled, typed back: *Barely. But I'm still standing.*

*That's all that matters.*

Was it? Was just standing enough?

Kendra looked at the video still playing on her laptop. Tia finishing a workout, arms raised, victorious. The best in the world.

Then she looked at her own reflection in the dark window. Tired eyes. Shoulders that ached. A body that had already done a full workout and a full workday and was expected to do it all again tomorrow.

Not the best. But still fighting.

She closed the laptop, turned off the lights, and went to bed.

Tomorrow, she'd get up at 4:45. She'd train at 5 AM. She'd work from 8 to 6. She'd keep doing both, keep pushing both, keep refusing to choose.

Maybe Marcus was right. Maybe she was sacrificing excellence.

But maybe excellence wasn't the only thing worth chasing.

Maybe showing up, day after day, in both worlds—maybe that was its own kind of victory.

Kendra set her alarm and closed her eyes.

In seven hours, she'd do it all again.
