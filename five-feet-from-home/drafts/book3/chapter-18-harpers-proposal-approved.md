# Chapter 18 – Harper's Proposal Approved

---

Harper stood outside the conference room door, breathing.

Not because she was nervous — she'd stopped being nervous about presentations somewhere around her hundredth pitch to a Fortune 500 board. But because this one mattered differently. This wasn't someone else's crisis. This was hers.

Her expansion. Her vision. Her risk.

Miami.

Inside, she could hear voices — Jasper's low and measured, Addison's sharper. They were reviewing financials, probably. Jasper never went into a decision meeting without running the numbers three times.

Harper glanced down at her tablet. The presentation was locked and loaded: market analysis, client pipeline, operational structure, revenue projections for Years 1 through 3. She'd spent six weeks building it, stress-testing every assumption, war-gaming every objection.

If they said no, it wasn't because she hadn't done the work.

The door opened. Addison leaned out, eyebrow raised.

"You planning to stand out here all day, or are you coming in?"

Harper smiled. "Just making sure you had time to miss me."

"We didn't."

"Liar."

Inside, the conference room was all glass and whiteboard, the kind of space that made everything feel official. Jasper sat at the head of the table, sleeves rolled to his elbows, a mug of coffee cooling beside his laptop. He looked tired — they all did — but his eyes were sharp.

"Harper," he said, nodding toward the chair across from him. "Let's hear it."

No preamble. No small talk. That was Jasper's way — efficient, direct, borderline surgical. Harper liked it. It meant he took her seriously.

She connected her tablet to the screen and pulled up the first slide: a map of the southeastern United States with Miami highlighted in red.

"Miami," she began. "Population 6.1 million in the metro area. Eighth-largest economy in the U.S. Gateway to Latin America. And right now, a crisis management blind spot."

Jasper leaned back, arms crossed. "Explain."

"Every major firm with a Latin American practice runs it out of New York or Houston. That's a twelve-hundred-mile gap between decision-makers and the clients who need them. When a crisis hits in São Paulo or Bogotá, they're coordinating across three time zones and two languages. We can cut that in half."

Addison was taking notes on her laptop, fingers flying. She didn't look up. "Market size?"

"Conservatively? Thirty billion in corporate crisis exposure across finance, pharma, and energy sectors. We're not chasing all of it. We're chasing the top ten percent — the clients who need someone on the ground in six hours, not six days."

"Who's your competition?" Jasper asked.

Harper pulled up the next slide: a competitor matrix. "Three firms. Kline Partners out of Houston — good reputation, slow response time. Vanguard Solutions in New York — expensive, bureaucratic. And a local shop called Tropical Risk Advisors — small, undercapitalized, can't handle anything bigger than a local PR flare-up."

"So we'd be the fast, expensive, competent option," Addison said.

"We'd be the only option," Harper corrected. "If a pharma exec in Caracas needs a crisis team tomorrow, they're not calling New York. They're calling us."

Jasper tapped his pen against the table, thinking. "Staffing?"

"I'd start with three people. A senior consultant — someone bilingual, someone who knows Latin American corporate culture. An analyst. And me."

"You're moving to Miami?" Addison looked up, surprised.

Harper nodded. "For the first year, at least. Maybe longer. You can't build a market from a distance."

Jasper's expression didn't shift, but Harper could see the calculation happening behind his eyes. She was COO. She ran operations for the entire firm. If she moved to Miami, someone would have to backfill her role in Charlotte.

"That's a risk," he said.

"It's an investment," Harper replied. "And if it works, we'll have a foothold in a market none of our competitors can touch."

She pulled up the revenue projections. "Year One, I'm projecting $8 million in billings. Year Two, $15 million. Year Three, $25 million. That's conservative. If we land two anchor clients early, we could double that."

Jasper studied the numbers. Addison was still typing, her face unreadable.

"What's your client pipeline?" Jasper asked.

Harper pulled up a list. "Three warm leads. All referrals from existing clients. One pharmaceutical company with regulatory exposure in Brazil. One energy firm with labor disputes in Colombia. And one private equity group with reputational issues in Argentina."

"Warm isn't closed," Addison said.

"No," Harper agreed. "But warm is a hell of a lot better than cold. And once we land the first one, the rest will follow."

Jasper set his pen down. "What's your ask?"

Harper took a breath. This was the moment. "Two million in operating budget for Year One. That covers salaries, office space, travel, and contingency. I'll run it as a separate P&L. If we hit revenue targets, we reinvest. If we don't, we shut it down and I come back to Charlotte."

"And if you hit targets?" Addison asked.

"Then Miami becomes permanent. And we start looking at São Paulo."

Jasper didn't respond immediately. He looked at Addison, some silent conversation passing between them. Harper had seen them do this a hundred times — the unspoken shorthand of two people who'd been in the trenches together long enough to communicate in glances.

Finally, Jasper nodded.

"Miami is yours," he said. "Build it."

Harper felt the breath leave her chest. "Yeah?"

"Yeah." He stood, extending a hand. "But if you blow through that two million in six months, I'm flying down there and dragging you back myself."

She shook his hand, grinning. "Deal."

Addison closed her laptop. "Congratulations, Harper. Try not to get eaten by an alligator."

"That's the Everglades. I'll be downtown."

"Same difference."

They walked out together, into the hallway where the late afternoon light slanted through the windows. Harper felt lighter than she had in months — maybe years. This was hers. Not a project she was managing for someone else. Not a crisis she was cleaning up. Her market. Her team. Her success or failure.

"You good?" Jasper asked.

"Yeah," Harper said. "Really good."

"Ethan know yet?"

Harper's smile flickered. Ethan. Her fiancé. The man she'd been with for three years and somehow still couldn't quite figure out how to build a life with.

"Not yet," she admitted. "I'll tell him tonight."

Jasper gave her a look — the kind that said he saw more than she wanted him to. "He's not going to like it."

"He'll understand."

"That's not the same thing."

Harper knew he was right. Ethan had been patient — more patient than she deserved, probably. But patience had limits. And asking him to wait another year while she built something a thousand miles away might be the limit.

"I'll figure it out," she said.

Jasper nodded, but he didn't look convinced. "Just don't lose yourself in the work, Harper. I've done that. It's not worth it."

She watched him walk away, back toward his office. Jasper Barrett, the man who'd built an empire on eighteen-hour days and missed birthdays, telling her not to lose herself in the work.

The irony wasn't lost on her.

But maybe that's what made the advice worth listening to.

That night, Harper sat in her apartment, staring at her phone.

She'd been staring at it for twenty minutes, trying to figure out what to say. How to tell Ethan that she was leaving. Not forever. Just for a while. Maybe.

Finally, she pressed call.

He picked up on the second ring. "Hey, babe. How'd the meeting go?"

His voice was warm, easy. He always sounded like that — like nothing could rattle him, like the world was manageable as long as they were in it together. It was one of the things she loved about him. And one of the things that terrified her.

"It went well," she said. "Really well, actually."

"Yeah? They approved it?"

"They did."

There was a pause. She could hear him moving around — probably in his kitchen, making dinner. He liked to cook. She liked to order takeout. It was one of a hundred small incompatibilities they'd learned to navigate.

"That's great," he said. "I'm proud of you."

"Thanks."

Another pause. Longer this time.

"You don't sound excited," he said.

Harper closed her eyes. God, he was perceptive. It was infuriating.

"I am," she said. "It's just... it's a big move. Literally."

"How long?"

"A year. Maybe more."

Silence.

She could picture him now — standing in his kitchen, wooden spoon in one hand, phone in the other, trying to process what she'd just said. Trying to figure out if this was a negotiation or a done deal.

"Harper," he said slowly. "We talked about this."

"I know."

"You said you'd think about it. You said you weren't sure."

"I wasn't sure. Now I am."

"So you're just... leaving."

"It's not leaving. It's work."

"It's a thousand miles away for a year. That's leaving."

She heard the edge in his voice now — not anger, exactly. Hurt. Which was worse.

"Ethan," she said carefully. "This is important to me."

"I know it is. That's the problem."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

He sighed, the kind of sigh that carried the weight of a hundred arguments they'd never quite finished. "It means I'm always going to come second to your work, Harper. And I don't know if I can keep doing that."

Her chest tightened. "That's not fair."

"Isn't it?"

She didn't have an answer. Because the truth was, he was right. Work had always come first. Not because she didn't love him — she did, in her own complicated, half-committed way — but because work made sense. Work had rules. Work had outcomes. Love was messier.

"I don't want to fight," she said quietly.

"I don't either." Another pause. "But I don't want to be the guy who's waiting for you to come home, either."

"So what are you saying?"

"I don't know," he admitted. "I need to think."

"Okay."

"Okay."

They sat in silence for a moment, the phone line crackling softly.

"I love you," Harper said, and she meant it. She just didn't know if it was enough.

"I love you too," Ethan replied. "But I don't think that's the problem."

After he hung up, Harper sat in the dark, staring at the city lights outside her window. Charlotte looked small from up here. Manageable. Like maybe she could hold it all in her hands if she just gripped tight enough.

But she knew better.

You couldn't hold everything.

Eventually, you had to choose.

The next morning, Harper started packing.

Not the big stuff — the furniture, the books, the life she'd built in Charlotte over the past five years. Just the essentials. Laptop. Notebooks. The coffee mug Jasper had given her when she made COO. She wrapped it carefully in a sweater and tucked it into her suitcase.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Ethan: *Can we talk tonight?*

She stared at the message for a long time before replying: *Yeah. I'll call you.*

But she didn't know what she'd say. Because the truth was, she'd already made her choice. She'd made it the moment Jasper said, "Miami is yours."

Everything else was just details.

At noon, she met Addison for coffee.

They sat in the corner booth at the café they'd claimed as their unofficial office, laptops open, lattes cooling. Addison was working on a pitch deck for a healthcare client. Harper was drafting a recruiting email for her senior consultant role.

"You talk to Ethan?" Addison asked without looking up.

Harper didn't bother asking how she knew. Addison always knew.

"Yeah."

"And?"

"And I think we're breaking up."

Addison finally looked up, eyebrow raised. "You think?"

"It's complicated."

"It's always complicated." Addison closed her laptop. "You want my advice, or are you going to tell me to stay out of it?"

"Your advice is always to leave," Harper said, half-smiling.

"That's because I'm usually right."

Harper laughed, but it felt hollow. "I don't want to be the person who chooses work over love."

"Then don't be."

"But I am."

Addison leaned back, studying her. "You know what I think?"

"What?"

"I think you're scared that if you choose love, you'll lose yourself. And I think you're scared that if you choose work, you'll end up alone. And I think you're so busy being scared that you're not actually choosing anything."

Harper stared at her. "Damn, Addie. Tell me how you really feel."

"You asked."

"I didn't, actually."

Addison smiled. "You did. You just didn't use words."

They sat in silence for a moment, the café buzzing around them. Outside, Charlotte moved at its usual pace — busy, efficient, indifferent to the small dramas unfolding inside.

"What did you choose?" Harper asked. "When you had to pick between Hawk and the work?"

"I didn't pick," Addison said. "I found someone who didn't make me choose."

"Not everyone gets that."

"No," Addison agreed. "But not everyone asks for it, either."

Harper thought about that as she walked back to her apartment that evening. The streets were quieter now, the rush hour traffic thinning out. She passed the park where she and Ethan used to walk on Sunday mornings, back when Sunday mornings were still a thing they did together.

When had that stopped?

She couldn't remember. That was the problem with slow endings — they didn't announce themselves. They just accumulated, one missed dinner at a time, until you looked up and realized you were living separate lives in the same city.

Her phone buzzed. Another text from Ethan: *I'm sorry about last night. Can we start over?*

Harper stopped walking. She stood there on the sidewalk, people streaming past her, and tried to imagine what starting over would look like.

Would she stay in Charlotte? Cancel Miami?

Would he move with her?

Would they try long distance and pretend it wasn't doomed?

She didn't know. But she knew this: if she had to give up Miami to keep him, she'd resent him. And if he had to follow her to keep her, he'd resent her.

And resentment was just love turning cold.

She typed back: *Let's talk tonight. I'll come over.*

That night, she sat across from Ethan in his living room, the same living room where they'd watched a hundred movies, fought a dozen fights, made up and broken up and made up again.

"I got the approval," she said. "I'm going to Miami."

He nodded slowly. "I figured."

"I'm sorry."

"Me too."

They sat in silence for a while, the weight of three years pressing down between them.

"I don't want to lose you," Ethan said finally.

"I don't want to lose you either."

"But you're going anyway."

"Yeah."

He smiled, but it was sad. "I wish I could be the guy who's okay with this. The guy who says, 'Go build your empire, babe, I'll be here when you get back.' But I can't."

"I know."

"And I wish you could be the person who stays. Who says, 'This is enough. You're enough.' But you can't."

"I know."

He reached across the couch and took her hand. His grip was warm, familiar. Safe.

"I hope Miami's everything you want," he said.

Harper felt tears sting her eyes. "I hope you find someone who stays."

"I hope you find someone who goes with you."

They held each other that night, one last time. And in the morning, Harper packed the rest of her things.

Two weeks later, she was on a plane to Miami.

The city spread out below her like a grid of lights and possibilities. She didn't know what was waiting for her down there — success, failure, loneliness, triumph. Maybe all of it.

But she knew this: it was hers.

And maybe that was enough.

Her phone buzzed as the plane descended. A video call from Ethan.

She almost didn't answer. Almost let it go to voicemail. But something made her pick up.

His face filled the screen — tired, but smiling.

"You got it?" he asked.

"I got it."

"Good."

She could see the strain in his face, the dark circles under his eyes. He hadn't been sleeping. Neither had she.

"Ethan—"

"Don't," he said gently. "We said everything we needed to say."

"I know. I just... I'm sorry."

"I know. Me too."

They looked at each other through the screen, across the miles that were about to become permanent.

"Good luck, Harper," he said.

"You too."

After he hung up, she stared out the window at the lights below. Miami, glittering and endless. Hers.

But the ache in her chest didn't feel like victory.

It felt like cost.

And maybe that was the price of choosing. Not that you made the wrong choice. But that you made one at all.

The plane touched down.

Harper grabbed her bag and stepped into the terminal, into the heat and noise and chaos of a city that didn't know her yet.

But it would.

She'd make sure of it.
