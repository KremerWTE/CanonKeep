# Chapter 42: Games Day 1

**POV: Kendra**
**Location: Madison, Wisconsin**

The air smells like sunscreen and lake water and nervous sweat. I stand in the athlete staging area at the Alliant Energy Center, surrounded by the best CrossFit athletes in the world, and try to remember how to breathe.

This is it. The CrossFit Games. The pinnacle. The thing I've worked toward for years.

And I'm absolutely terrified.

"Williams!" My coach, Mariana, appears at my elbow with a water bottle and the kind of stern look that means business. "Stop spiraling. I can see it happening."

"I'm not spiraling," I lie.

"You're doing that thing with your jaw. The clenching thing." She puts her hands on my shoulders, forces me to look at her. "You belong here. You earned your spot. Now stop thinking and start doing."

Around us, athletes are going through their warm-ups, shaking out limbs, visualizing the course. I recognize faces from Instagram, from YouTube videos I've watched a hundred times while eating dinner alone in my apartment. There's Amanda Barnhart, all power and precision. Danielle Brandon, fierce and focused. And there — stretching near the water's edge — Tia-Clair Toomey.

Seven-time champion. Back less than a year after having a baby. A living legend.

I'm in the same competition as Tia-Clair Toomey.

The thought makes me want to throw up and cry and laugh all at once.

"Kendra." Mariana snaps her fingers in front of my face. "Where are you right now?"

"Here," I say. "I'm here."

"Then be here. Not in your head. Not worried about who else is competing. Just you, the course, the work."

I nod, shake out my arms, try to channel the nervous energy into something useful.

My phone buzzes in my bag. I shouldn't check it — we're fifteen minutes from the start — but I can't help myself.

Chris: *You've got this, babe! Watching the livestream with your parents. We're all so proud of you. Love you!*

Mom: *Breathe, baby girl. You were born for this.*

Dad: *Show them what Barrett girls are made of.*

Jasper: *Grace made you a good luck sign. I'll send a picture after. Kill it out there, Ken.*

And then, at the bottom of the notifications, one more:

Addison: *You belong there. Trust your training. I'm watching.*

My chest tightens. We haven't spoken much since the barbecue three weeks ago. Polite texts about logistics, surface-level exchanges. Nothing real. But she's watching.

I slip the phone back into my bag and focus on the water ahead.

Lake Monona stretches out, deceptively calm in the early morning light. The course is brutal: a 500-meter open water swim, immediately into a trail run with two sandbag carries at specific stations. Two miles total, plus the swim, plus the bags.

"Two minutes!" someone calls.

Athletes begin moving toward the water's edge. I join them, feeling the ground shift from grass to sand to the rocky shoreline. The water is cool against my feet, then my calves as I wade in.

Find your people, I think, scanning the starting line. Don't get boxed in. Stay smooth through the swim. Save your legs for the run.

"Thirty seconds!"

I close my eyes. Breathe in. Breathe out.

I think of every early morning in the gym. Every burpee, every thruster, every moment I wanted to quit and didn't. I think of Grace's face when I told her I qualified. I think of my parents driving ten hours to be here in Madison, somewhere in the stands with cowbells and signs.

I think of Addison saying: You belong there.

"Ten seconds!"

The crowd roar builds. I open my eyes, set my stance.

"Three... two... one..."

The horn blares.

And we're off.

---

The water is chaos.

Bodies everywhere, arms and legs and splashing, everyone fighting for position. I get kicked in the ribs, elbowed in the shoulder, but I push through, finding my rhythm. Stroke, stroke, breathe. Stroke, stroke, breathe.

The first buoy comes into view. I round it, feeling bodies start to spread out. I've lost track of my position — somewhere in the middle of the pack, maybe? — but it doesn't matter. Just swim. Just move.

The water is cooler than I expected, and I'm grateful for it. My heart is hammering, but my breathing stays controlled. This is just another swim. Just another workout.

Except it's not. It's the CrossFit Games.

The second buoy. My arms are starting to burn, but I push harder. I can see the exit point now, the timing mats on the beach, the athletes already sprinting up toward the trail.

Stroke, stroke, breathe.

I'm not first. Not even close. But I'm not last either.

My feet find the bottom. I stand, water streaming off me, and run for the beach. My legs feel like rubber after being horizontal, but I push through it, hitting the timing mat and veering toward the trail.

The crowd noise is deafening. I catch a glimpse of the leaderboard as I pass — I'm 15th out of the water. Fifteen. Not bad for my first Games swim.

The trail is packed dirt and rocks, winding uphill through trees. My lungs scream. My legs remember they know how to run. I pass someone — fourteen — and settle into a pace I hope I can maintain.

The first sandbag station appears at the half-mile mark. Forty-pound bag, carry it a hundred meters, drop it and keep running. I hoist it onto my shoulders, and the weight is both familiar and shocking. We train with sandbags all the time, but never after a swim, never with legs already shaking.

Don't think. Just move.

I carry, focused on each step. Other athletes are all around me — some passing, some falling back. I drop the bag at the marker and immediately feel lighter, even though my shoulders are on fire.

Keep running. Don't stop.

The second sandbag station. This one is sixty pounds. I grab it, heave it up, and nearly stumble. Someone passes me — back to fifteen — and I grit my teeth.

You belong here.

I find my footing, adjust the bag, and move. One step. Another. The crowd along the trail is screaming, but it's just noise now. All I can hear is my breath, my heartbeat, the thud of my feet on packed earth.

Drop the bag. Final stretch.

My legs are lead. My lungs are fire. But I can see the finish line now, that final timing mat, and I know — I know — that I have more to give.

I sprint.

Someone is next to me, another athlete in the same final push. We're stride for stride, and I reach down for something extra, some reserve I wasn't sure I had.

The finish line. I throw myself across it, hands on my knees immediately, gasping.

"Eleventh," someone says. "Williams, eleventh place."

Eleventh. I went from fifteenth out of the water to eleventh overall.

I look up at the leaderboard, still trying to catch my breath, and there it is: my name. 11th place, Event 1.

Tia-Clair Toomey is first, of course. She finished nearly a minute ahead of everyone else, looking like she barely broke a sweat. That's the level. That's what dominance looks like.

But I'm eleventh. At the CrossFit Games. On my first event ever.

Mariana appears with water and a towel and a smile that's almost proud. "Not bad, Williams."

"Not bad?" I'm still gasping. "I'm eleventh."

"You're eleventh," she agrees. "At the Games. On a course that favored swimmers and you held your own on the run. That's solid."

"Tia made it look easy."

"Tia is Tia. You're you." She hands me the water. "And you just proved you belong here. Now hydrate, cool down, and get ready for tomorrow."

I drink the water, let the reality sink in. I'm at the CrossFit Games. I just completed my first event. And I didn't embarrass myself.

Actually, I kind of killed it.

---

The athlete area is buzzing after the event. Everyone is analyzing their performance, talking splits and transitions and what they could have done better. I find a quiet corner to stretch and check my phone.

The messages are flooding in.

Chris: *THAT WAS AMAZING! You looked so strong! I'm showing everyone here the replay!*

Mom: *I screamed so loud I think I scared the people next to us. SO PROUD!*

Dad: *That's my girl. Hell of a run.*

Jasper: *Grace says you were "super fast like a cheetah." Also she wants to know if you got a medal yet. I explained that's not how it works. She's skeptical.*

I smile at that one, already typing back: *Tell Grace cheetahs are WAY faster but I appreciate the comparison. No medal yet but working on it.*

I scroll through more messages — friends from BSS, old college teammates, people I haven't heard from in years coming out of the woodwork to congratulate me.

And then I see it. A text from Addison, sent about ten minutes ago:

*That final sprint was incredible. You looked strong. I knew you would.*

My finger hovers over the message. I should reply. Should say thank you, send a smiley face, keep it light and friendly like we've been doing.

Instead, I just stare at it.

I knew you would.

She believed I could do this. Even with all the weirdness between us, even with the distance I've put there by choosing Chris, by choosing easy — she still believed.

I start typing three different responses and delete them all. Finally, I settle on: *Thanks for watching. Means a lot.*

It sends before I can overthink it. Three dots appear immediately, then disappear. Then appear again.

Addison: *Always.*

One word. It shouldn't hit me the way it does.

"Kendra!" One of the other athletes — a woman named Rachel who I met briefly at a competition last year — waves me over. "We're grabbing food. You coming?"

I pocket my phone. "Yeah. Definitely."

I join the group, let myself get absorbed into talk of the event, of tomorrow's workouts, of the insanity of competing at this level. These are my people now — elite athletes, competitors, the ones who understand what it takes to get here.

But when I check my phone later, between bites of the biggest burger I've ever seen, I find myself looking at Addison's message again.

Always.

---

That night, in my hotel room, I watch the replay of the event. It's surreal seeing myself on the screen, one athlete among many, fighting through the water and up the trail. The commentary team mentions my name a few times — "Strong showing from rookie Kendra Williams" — and I feel a stupid surge of pride.

I made them notice me. On day one, I made them say my name.

I watch Tia's finish, study the way she moves, the efficiency of every step. She's on another level, and everyone knows it. Seven-time champion, back after a baby, still untouchable. But she's also proof that it's possible — that you can be the best in the world and also be a whole person with a whole life.

My phone rings. Chris, FaceTiming from Charlotte.

"There's my CrossFit Games athlete!" he says when I answer, his face filling the screen. "How does it feel?"

"Exhausting," I admit, but I'm smiling. "And amazing. And terrifying."

"You were incredible to watch. Your parents and I were losing our minds."

"Were they embarrassing?"

"Your mom definitely tried to start a wave. Your dad kept explaining your strategy to everyone around us like he's a coach." Chris grins. "It was adorable."

We talk for a while — about the event, about tomorrow's workouts, about how weird it is to be in Madison while he's in Charlotte. He tells me about his day, about a work meeting that ran long, about how he's already planning what we'll do when I get back.

"I'm thinking we do a big celebration dinner," he says. "Invite everyone from BSS. Really make it a thing."

"I haven't even finished competing yet," I protest.

"Doesn't matter. You're at the Games. That's worth celebrating."

He's sweet. Supportive. Everything I should want.

"I should get some sleep," I say eventually. "Tomorrow's a heavy day."

"Okay. Hey, Kendra?"

"Yeah?"

"I'm really proud of you. You know that, right?"

"I know," I say softly. "Thank you for being there."

"Wouldn't miss it. Love you."

"Love you too."

We hang up, and the room feels very quiet suddenly. I set my phone on the nightstand, turn off the lights, and lie in the dark.

Tomorrow is Event 2: a classic triplet. Heavy deadlifts, handstand push-ups, and box jumps. It's going to hurt in entirely different ways than today did.

But I'm ready. I proved today that I belong here.

I close my eyes and try to sleep, but my mind keeps replaying moments from the day. The water. The run. The final sprint to the finish.

Tia-Clair Toomey, looking untouchable.

My name on the leaderboard: 11th place.

And Addison's text, still glowing on my phone: Always.

I drift off thinking about what it means to belong — to a competition, to a relationship, to a moment. And wondering if you can belong somewhere while still feeling like part of you is somewhere else entirely.

In Madison, I'm a CrossFit Games athlete. I'm living my dream.

So why do I still feel like I left something important back in Charlotte?
