# Chapter 39: Boston Financial Crisis

**POV: Addison**

---

The conference room on the thirty-eighth floor of One International Place had floor-to-ceiling windows overlooking Boston Harbor, but Addison Price hadn't looked at the view once in the three hours she'd been sitting here.

Her focus was entirely on the man across the table—Gerald Hutchins, managing partner of Hutchins Capital, a mid-sized private equity firm that had just received a Wells Notice from the SEC indicating imminent enforcement action.

Translation: they were about to get sued for securities fraud, and Addison had three weeks to either prevent it or manage the fallout so thoroughly that the firm survived.

No pressure.

"Walk me through the timeline again," Addison said, her voice calm despite the adrenaline thrumming through her system. "When did you first learn about the accounting irregularities?"

Gerald was in his early sixties, silver-haired and expensively suited, with the kind of polished confidence that came from three decades of making money in markets most people couldn't understand. But right now, that confidence had cracks running through it.

"March," he said. "Our internal audit flagged some discrepancies in how we were valuing certain portfolio companies. Nothing egregious, we thought. Just... aggressive accounting assumptions."

"Aggressive enough to inflate your reported returns by what percentage?"

Gerald's jaw tightened. "Twelve to fifteen percent over three years."

Addison didn't react outwardly, but internally she was recalculating her strategy. Twelve to fifteen percent wasn't a rounding error. It wasn't a good-faith difference in valuation methodology. It was material misrepresentation, and the SEC was going to treat it as such.

"And when did you disclose this to your investors?"

Silence.

"Gerald," Addison said, her voice still calm but with steel underneath. "When did you disclose?"

"We haven't. Not formally. We wanted to understand the full scope before triggering panic."

Addison closed her eyes briefly, then opened them. "So you've known about material misrepresentations for eight months, failed to disclose to your LPs, and are now facing SEC enforcement. Is there anything else I should know? Any other bodies buried that are going to surface in the next seventy-two hours?"

"No. I've told you everything."

"Let me be very clear about what everything means, Gerald. If I'm going to represent you through this—if Barrett Security Solutions is going to put our reputation behind managing this crisis—I need complete transparency. No surprises. No additional revelations three days before the hearing. Because if that happens, I will withdraw, and you will be facing this alone. Do you understand?"

Gerald nodded, looking smaller than he had when she'd arrived. "I understand."

"Good." Addison opened her laptop and pulled up the draft strategy document she'd been building since Jasper had sent her this case four days ago. "Here's what we're going to do."

For the next ninety minutes, she walked him through the plan. Immediate disclosure to limited partners with carefully crafted language that acknowledged the issue without triggering breach of contract claims. Proactive outreach to the SEC demonstrating cooperation and remediation. Media strategy that positioned this as accounting methodology differences rather than intentional fraud. Internal restructuring that showed genuine commitment to compliance.

It was a good plan. Solid. The kind of crisis management that BSS had built its reputation on.

But as she talked, Addison was acutely aware that Jasper wasn't here. That for the first time in her career with the firm, she was running point on a major case without him providing cover, making the final calls, taking responsibility if things went sideways.

This was her show. Her reputation on the line.

The thought was terrifying and exhilarating in equal measure.

---

After the meeting, Addison stood at those floor-to-ceiling windows and finally let herself look at the view.

Boston Harbor stretched out below, boats cutting white lines through dark water, the airport visible in the distance. She'd been in Boston for four days, would be here for at least two more weeks, possibly three depending on how the SEC negotiations went.

Three weeks away from Charlotte. From the office. From Elena and RCIA sessions and the life she'd been building.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Jasper: *How'd the client meeting go?*

She typed back: *As well as could be expected. He buried the lede on disclosure timeline, but we have a path forward.*

*Need backup?*

Addison hesitated, her fingers hovering over the keyboard. The old version of herself would have said yes immediately. Would have wanted Jasper there to validate her strategy, to share the weight of the decisions, to be the one ultimately responsible if things went wrong.

But she wasn't the old version of herself anymore.

*I've got it,* she typed. *Going to loop in Harper for the media strategy piece, but the regulatory and client management is mine.*

There was a pause, then: *Good. You don't need me for this. You've got every tool in the arsenal. Trust yourself.*

She smiled despite the anxiety coiling in her chest. *Trying.*

*Not trying. Doing. Call me if you need to talk through anything, but otherwise—this is yours. Show them what you're made of.*

Addison pocketed her phone and turned back to the harbor view, feeling the weight of what Jasper had just given her. Not just permission to lead the case, but confidence that she could. Recognition that she'd evolved from partner who executed his strategy to strategist who could build her own.

It was what she'd been working toward for years. And now that she had it, it was absolutely terrifying.

---

The next two weeks were a blur of eighteen-hour days.

Addison set up a temporary war room in the firm's Boston office—a converted conference room with whiteboards covering three walls, timeline charts, stakeholder maps, regulatory filing deadlines color-coded by urgency.

She assembled a team: two junior associates from the Boston office who knew the local SEC staff, a forensic accountant from a firm she'd worked with on previous cases, a crisis communications specialist Harper had recommended who'd handled similar situations.

Every morning started at 5:30 with coffee and a review of the overnight developments. Every evening ended after 11:00 with a debrief and planning session for the next day. In between was a relentless cycle of witness prep, document review, regulatory meetings, and client management.

Gerald Hutchins called her at least twice a day, sometimes in panic, sometimes in denial, occasionally in anger. Addison handled each call with the same calm patience, redirecting his energy toward productive actions, managing his anxiety while maintaining clear boundaries about what was and wasn't possible.

The limited partner disclosure went out on Day 6. The blowback was immediate and intense—angry phone calls, threats of litigation, demands for Gerald's resignation. Addison had war-gamed every scenario, prepared response scripts, coordinated with the firm's general counsel to ensure they were protected.

By Day 9, the initial fury had cooled enough for substantive conversations. Some LPs were still furious, but they were talking rather than just threatening. That was progress.

The SEC meetings were harder. The enforcement staff were professional but skeptical, clearly unconvinced that this was innocent accounting misjudgment rather than intentional inflation of returns. Addison walked them through the remediation plan—new valuation methodology, third-party oversight, enhanced disclosure protocols—while her forensic accountant provided technical backup.

"You understand," the lead SEC attorney said during their third meeting, "that cooperation and remediation don't make the underlying violation go away. Your client materially misstated returns for three years. That's fraud."

"With respect," Addison replied, her voice steady, "the question isn't whether there were misstatements. We've acknowledged that. The question is whether those misstatements were intentional and whether the firm took adequate steps to correct them once discovered. We're arguing they were the result of aggressive but good-faith assumptions, and that the firm's response once the issues were identified demonstrates genuine commitment to compliance."

"Eight months to disclose to investors isn't exactly immediate corrective action."

"Eight months to fully investigate the scope, implement new controls, and prepare accurate corrected disclosures. We could have rushed out a half-formed disclosure in Week 1 and triggered complete collapse of investor confidence based on incomplete information. Instead, we did the work to understand the full picture and present it accurately. That's responsible, not evasive."

The SEC attorney didn't look convinced, but she made notes. That was something.

---

On Day 12, Addison's carefully constructed control started to slip.

She was in her hotel room—a corporate suite at the Mandarin Oriental that was nicer than her apartment in Charlotte—when her phone rang. An unknown number.

She almost didn't answer. But some instinct made her pick up.

"Is this Addison Price?" A woman's voice, unfamiliar.

"Yes. Who's calling?"

"My name is Rebecca Hutchins. I'm Gerald's daughter. I need to talk to you about my father's case."

Every alarm bell in Addison's head started ringing. "Ms. Hutchins, I appreciate you reaching out, but I can't discuss your father's case without his consent. Attorney-client privilege and all that."

"I don't want to discuss the case. I want to discuss my father." Rebecca's voice cracked slightly. "Please. Just five minutes. I'm in the hotel lobby. I can come up, or we can meet somewhere else, but I need to talk to someone who understands what's happening."

Addison closed her eyes, weighing the risks. This could be a setup—Rebecca recording the conversation, trying to get Addison to say something that could be used against Gerald later. Or it could be exactly what it sounded like: a scared daughter trying to understand why her father's world was falling apart.

"The bar," Addison said finally. "I'll meet you in the hotel bar in fifteen minutes. Public space, no recording, strictly off the record. And if you violate that, I walk."

"Agreed. Thank you."

Addison changed out of the sweatpants she'd been working in and into tailored pants and a silk blouse—armor disguised as professional wear. She found Rebecca in the bar, sitting at a corner table with a glass of wine she didn't appear to be drinking.

She was early thirties, dark hair in a simple ponytail, wearing jeans and a Georgetown Law sweatshirt. Her eyes were red.

"Thank you for meeting me," Rebecca said as Addison sat down.

"Five minutes," Addison replied. "And I meant what I said—I can't discuss your father's case."

"I know. I'm not here about the legal strategy. I'm here because..." Rebecca took a shaky breath. "My father called me yesterday. He was drunk, which is not like him. And he said that he might go to prison. That everything he built is going to collapse. That he's destroyed his legacy and betrayed everyone who trusted him."

Addison felt something tighten in her chest. "What did you say to him?"

"I didn't know what to say. I'm a corporate lawyer—I understand the legal framework, but I've never dealt with something like this on a personal level. And he wouldn't talk to me about the details, just kept saying he'd ruined everything."

She looked at Addison with desperate eyes. "Is that true? Is he going to prison?"

"I genuinely don't know," Addison said, and it was the truth. "The SEC enforcement action could result in civil penalties, injunctions, possibly a temporary bar from the industry. Criminal charges are a different division, and we haven't seen any indication those are coming. But I can't promise you they won't."

"And if they do? What happens to him? To our family?"

Addison thought about deflecting, about giving a safe non-answer that protected her position. But looking at Rebecca's face—the fear and confusion and desperate need to understand—she found herself giving the honest answer instead.

"If criminal charges come, he'll face trial. If convicted, he could go to prison—probably minimum security, probably not for more than a few years given his age and lack of prior record. Your family would face financial consequences, reputational damage, social isolation. It would be hard. Really hard."

"But we'd survive?"

"People survive worse. The question isn't whether you'd survive. It's whether your father can find a way to make meaning out of what comes next—whether he can rebuild his life around something other than the identity he's losing."

Rebecca was quiet for a long moment. "You sound like you've thought about this a lot. Not just professionally."

"I think about survival a lot," Addison admitted. "What it costs. What it requires. What it looks like to rebuild after everything falls apart."

"Have you had to rebuild?"

Addison considered the question. Thought about the version of herself who'd existed before Elena's collapse, before RCIA, before the slow dismantling of all the walls she'd built around her heart.

"I'm in the process," she said finally. "Turns out you don't have to lose everything to need rebuilding. Sometimes you just realize the thing you built isn't sustainable, and you have to take it apart and start again."

Rebecca nodded slowly, then finished her wine in one long swallow. "Thank you. For being honest. For treating this like it's about people and not just legal strategy."

"It's always about people," Addison said. "The legal strategy is just the framework."

After Rebecca left, Addison stayed in the bar, nursing a glass of wine she didn't really want and thinking about Gerald Hutchins drunk-dialing his daughter. About the human cost of the crisis she was managing. About the fact that no matter how brilliant her strategy was, someone was going to suffer.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Elena: *How are you holding up?*

Addison stared at the message for a long moment, feeling the distance between Boston and Charlotte like a physical weight.

*Tired,* she typed back. *But okay. Case is progressing. Should have resolution in the next week or so.*

*I miss you.*

The words hit harder than they should have. Addison felt tears prick her eyes—from exhaustion, from stress, from the overwhelming loneliness of sitting in this beautiful hotel bar managing someone else's crisis while her own life waited in a city five hundred miles away.

*I miss you too,* she replied. *More than I should probably admit.*

*No such thing as too much when it's true. Call me when you have time? I want to hear your voice.*

*Tonight. After I finish prep for tomorrow's SEC meeting.*

*I'll be up. Lucas is on a sleep strike anyway.*

Addison smiled despite everything and pocketed her phone. Then she went back to her room, back to the war room documents spread across the desk, back to the work of managing crisis and containing damage and building strategy out of chaos.

This was what she was good at. What she'd built her career on.

But increasingly, it felt like the wrong measuring stick for her life.

---

The resolution came on Day 18.

The SEC agreed to a settlement: civil penalty of $4.2 million, enhanced compliance monitoring for three years, no admission of intentional wrongdoing. It was better than Addison had hoped, honestly. The kind of outcome that let Gerald keep his firm, rebuild his reputation, avoid criminal exposure.

He cried when she told him. Actual tears in the conference room, this sixty-year-old master of the universe breaking down because he wasn't going to lose everything after all.

"Thank you," he said, his voice rough. "Thank you for saving my life."

"I didn't save your life," Addison corrected gently. "I gave you tools to save yourself. What you do with them from here is up to you."

After the final settlement documents were signed, Addison stood in that conference room one more time, looking out at Boston Harbor.

Three weeks in this city. Three weeks of eighteen-hour days, relentless pressure, carrying the full weight of a major case without backup.

And she'd done it. Not perfectly—there were things she'd change, conversations she'd handle differently, strategic choices that hadn't paid off the way she'd hoped.

But she'd done it.

Her phone rang. Jasper.

"I heard," he said when she answered. "Settlement approved. Hell of a result, Addie."

"It was a team effort."

"Don't diminish it. You led that team. You made the calls. You carried that client through the worst crisis of his professional life. That was you."

Addison felt emotion clog her throat. "I couldn't have done it without the foundation you built. Without everything you taught me about crisis management and client psychology and—"

"Addison." Jasper's voice was firm but warm. "You don't need me anymore. You haven't needed me for a while. You're not my protégé or my deputy or my backup. You're my partner. Actual equal partner. And it's time you started seeing yourself that way."

"I'm trying."

"No," Jasper said, echoing the text from three weeks ago. "You're doing. You just proved it. Three weeks in Boston, major SEC case, client on the edge of collapse, and you brought him home safe. That's not trying. That's succeeding."

Addison closed her eyes, letting the words sink in. "Maybe. But I still want you there. Not because I need you. Because I value your perspective. Your experience. Your judgment."

"That's different. That's partnership, not dependency. And I'll always be here for that. But the next time someone asks who led the Hutchins Capital case, the answer is Addison Price. Not Jasper Barrett. Not BSS. You."

After they hung up, Addison stood at the window for a long time, watching the boats in the harbor, feeling the shift inside herself.

She'd come to Boston as Jasper's partner, running a case he'd assigned her, representing the firm's reputation.

She was leaving as herself. As the strategist who'd navigated impossible complexity and brought a client through to the other side. As someone capable of carrying that weight alone.

The thought was terrifying. It was also liberating.

Her phone buzzed again. Elena: *Flying into Boston tonight. Dinner? I've missed you too much to wait until you get home.*

Addison's heart jumped. *You're coming here?*

*Maison Aurelia has a client meeting in Cambridge tomorrow anyway. Seemed like good timing. Plus I wanted to see you. Hotel bar at 8?*

*I'll be there.*

Addison looked at her watch. Five hours to finish the close-out documentation, debrief the team, pack her war room materials.

Five hours until she saw Elena.

She felt something unclench in her chest—relief, anticipation, the recognition that she'd been holding herself together through pure willpower and now could finally let someone else share the weight.

Not because she needed saving. Because she wanted to share the victory with someone who mattered.

That was different. That was growth.

Addison turned from the window and got to work, counting down the hours until she could stop being the crisis manager and just be Addie again.

Just for one night. That would be enough.

*End of Chapter 39*
