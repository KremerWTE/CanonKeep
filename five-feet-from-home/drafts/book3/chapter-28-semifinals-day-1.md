# Chapter 28: Semifinals Day 1

**POV: Kendra Miller**
**Location: Knoxville, Tennessee**

The lake looked cold. Kendra stood on the shore with forty-nine other women, all of them staring at the gray water of Fort Loudoun Lake like it might offer some kind of mercy. It wouldn't. Semifinals never did.

"Athletes to the start line," the announcer called, and Kendra felt her heart rate spike.

Event one: 400-meter open water swim, 1.5-mile hill run, then a barbell complex for time. It was the kind of workout designed to destroy you in layers—first the swim would wreck your breathing, then the run would shred your legs, and by the time you hit the barbell you'd be operating on pure will.

Kendra had done worse. She'd survived the Open. She'd earned her spot here. But standing on this shore in Knoxville, watching the elite athletes around her—women who'd been to the Games multiple times, women with sponsorships and Instagram followings and CrossFit celebrity status—she felt the gap between where she was and where she wanted to be.

Forty-ninth place last year. This year, she needed top ten to advance to the Games.

"You ready?" The woman next to her was tall, blonde, familiar from Instagram. One of the perennial contenders.

"As I'll ever be," Kendra said.

The horn blared, and they were off.

The water hit like a slap. Cold enough to steal breath, murky enough that Kendra couldn't see more than a few feet ahead. She fell into her stroke—not her strongest skill, but she'd trained for this. Hours in the pool with Chris watching her form, drilling breathing patterns, building the kind of endurance that would carry her through 400 meters even when her lungs were screaming.

Bodies churned around her. Someone's foot caught her shoulder. She adjusted course, found clearer water, focused on the buoy marking the turnaround point.

The swim portion of CrossFit workouts was always mental warfare. You couldn't see the finish, couldn't measure your progress, couldn't do anything except trust your training and keep moving. Kendra counted strokes, regulated her breathing, and tried not to think about the forty-nine other women who were probably faster swimmers.

When her hand finally hit sand, she scrambled out of the water and onto the beach. Her legs felt heavy, waterlogged. She had maybe three seconds to transition before she had to start running.

She kicked off her swim cap, pulled on her running shoes—pre-laced, ready to go—and took off toward the hill course.

The first quarter mile was flat, and Kendra used it to find her rhythm. Her breathing was ragged from the swim, but it started to even out as her legs remembered what they were supposed to do. Around her, other athletes surged past or fell behind. She couldn't worry about them. She had to run her own race.

The hill started gradually, then got steep. Really steep. The kind of incline that turned running into something closer to climbing, where every step required conscious decision-making and your quads burned with the specific agony of fighting gravity.

Kendra leaned into it. She'd trained hills with Chris for weeks specifically for this. He'd taken her to every steep road around Charlotte, made her run repeats until her legs felt like jelly, taught her how to manage the mental game of climbing when every instinct said to stop.

"Hills are where you separate yourself," he'd told her. "Most people slow down, try to conserve energy. You speed up. You attack."

So she attacked. Pushed through the burn, lengthened her stride, found the rhythm that would carry her up without completely destroying her for the barbell complex waiting at the top.

She passed two women on the climb. Small victories.

At the crest of the hill, volunteers directed them toward the final running portion—a winding descent back toward the competition floor. Kendra's legs wanted to give out on the downhill, wanted to buckle and collapse. She locked her knees and ran through it, focusing on the transition area coming into view.

Her barbell was waiting. 135 pounds. The complex: three power cleans, two front squats, one shoulder-to-overhead. Seven rounds for time.

Kendra chalked her hands, approached the bar, and lifted.

The first round felt manageable. Her arms were tired from swimming, her legs were wrecked from the run, but the weight moved. Clean, clean, clean. Squat, squat. Press overhead.

Round two was harder. The barbell felt heavier, her breathing more labored.

Round three, she had to pause between reps. Just for a second. Just to convince her body it could keep going.

Around her, other athletes were finishing. She heard the sound of bars dropping, the announcer calling out names and times. She couldn't think about them. She had to finish.

Round four. Round five. Her vision was starting to narrow, that particular kind of tunnel vision that came from maximum effort. Everything reduced to the barbell, the movement pattern, the desperate math of reps remaining.

Round six. Her arms were shaking. The press overhead on the last rep took three attempts—she got the bar halfway up, lost it, had to re-clean and try again.

Final round. Seven reps between her and the finish.

Kendra thought about Addison, about their last conversation. "I'm still invested in your career." Thought about Chris, waiting at the athlete area, believing she could do this. Thought about herself a year ago, struggling through the Open, barely qualifying for semifinals.

She cleaned the bar. Once. Twice. Three times.

Front squats. Down, up. Down, up.

One more rep. Shoulder to overhead. She pressed with everything she had left, felt the bar lock out overhead, heard the timer beep as she dropped it.

Finished.

Kendra bent over, hands on her knees, trying to remember how to breathe normally. Her coach appeared—not Chris, who couldn't coach her officially during competition, but the BSS trainer they'd assigned for the weekend.

"Time?" Kendra managed.

"17:43," the coach said. "Good work."

17:43. She had no idea if that was good enough. Wouldn't know until all fifty athletes finished.

She stumbled to the athlete recovery area, found water, found a place to sit. Other women were scattered around, all of them looking equally destroyed. This was the thing about semifinals that the spectators didn't see—the aftermath. The way elite athletes became human again after pushing beyond every reasonable limit.

Chris found her twenty minutes later, once the athlete area opened to coaches.

"You were amazing," he said, crouching down next to where she was stretched out on the grass.

"I was slow," Kendra said.

"You were strategic," Chris corrected. "You didn't blow up on the swim, you attacked the hill, you finished the barbell complex. That's exactly what we planned."

"What place?"

"They're still processing times. But I counted—you weren't in the last ten to finish."

That was something. Not great, but something.

The leaderboard updated an hour later. Kendra checked her phone and felt her stomach drop.

Eighth place.

She'd finished eighth in event one.

"Holy shit," Chris said, looking over her shoulder at the screen. "Kendra. Eighth place."

"It's just one event," Kendra said, but she couldn't stop the smile spreading across her face. "There are six more."

"It's a statement," Chris said. "You just announced that you belong here."

Kendra looked at the leaderboard again. Her name sat in eighth place, sandwiched between athletes she'd been watching on YouTube for years. Above her in first place: Laura Horvath.

Laura Horvath. The reigning CrossFit Games champion. The woman who'd won the Games last year and was the overwhelming favorite to win again. The athlete that every woman in this competition was chasing.

Kendra had watched Laura finish the event—she'd come out of the water first, run the hill like it was flat ground, and moved through the barbell complex with a speed and efficiency that looked effortless. Her time: 14:22. Three minutes faster than Kendra.

Three minutes. That was the gap between eighth place and first place. Between being a competitive athlete and being a champion.

Kendra watched the video replay of Laura's heat on the big screen. The way she moved was different—more economical, more explosive, more dialed in. It wasn't just that she was stronger or faster. It was that she was more complete. More refined. More championship-level.

"That's what I'm chasing," Kendra said, nodding toward the screen.

Chris followed her gaze. "Laura Horvath."

"That level," Kendra said. "That's where I need to get."

"You will," Chris said. "But not today. Today you focus on staying consistent, staying in the top ten, qualifying for the Games. The championship-level stuff—that comes after."

He was right. Kendra knew he was right. But watching Laura move, seeing the gap between good and great made visible in split times and leaderboard positions—it crystallized something for her.

This wasn't just about making the Games anymore. It was about becoming the kind of athlete who could compete at the highest level. Who could stand on the competition floor with Laura Horvath and not just survive, but contend.

She had a long way to go.

The rest of the day was recovery and preparation for tomorrow's events. Kendra foam-rolled, stretched, ate the carefully planned meals that BSS's nutritionist had prepared, and tried not to obsess over the leaderboard.

That night, lying in her hotel room, she got a text from Addison: "Saw the results. 8th place. Incredible."

Kendra smiled and typed back: "First event. Long way to go."

"You've got this. Trust your training."

"Thanks. How's Charlotte?"

"Busy. Working on a new project. But watching your competition feed when I can."

Kendra felt something warm spread through her chest. Even with everything that had shifted between them, Addison was still there. Still watching. Still invested.

"Means a lot," Kendra typed. "Really."

"Go get some sleep. Big day tomorrow."

Kendra set her phone aside and closed her eyes. Tomorrow was event two and three—a heavy deadlift ladder, then a gymnastics-heavy chipper. Different challenges, different opportunities to separate herself from the pack or fall behind.

But tonight, she let herself feel good about eighth place. About proving she belonged here. About being forty-two spots better than last year.

The gap between eighth and first was still huge. But the gap between forty-ninth and eighth had seemed impossible too, and she'd closed it.

Laura Horvath was the standard, the benchmark, the level that defined championship-caliber. Kendra watched her from eight spots back and thought: someday.

Not this year, probably. Maybe not next year. But someday, she'd close that gap. She'd stand on the competition floor and not just survive against the best—she'd compete with them.

For now, eighth place was enough. It was proof that all the training, all the sacrifice, all the complicated choices she'd made over the past year were building toward something real.

She fell asleep thinking about tomorrow's events, about the forty-nine other women chasing the same dream, about the long road from where she was to where she wanted to be.

And for the first time in months, she slept without doubting she'd get there eventually.

The alarm would go off at 5:30 AM. Day two of semifinals would test her in different ways. But tonight, she was eighth place, and that was victory enough.
