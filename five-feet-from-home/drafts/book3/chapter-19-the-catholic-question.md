# Chapter 19 – The Catholic Question

---

The church was older than Addison expected.

Not old in the European sense — no Gothic arches or stained glass saints. But old for Charlotte: red brick, white trim, a bell tower that looked like it had been standing since before the city learned to build upward. The sign out front read *St. Catherine of Siena Catholic Church*, and below it, in smaller letters: *All Are Welcome*.

Addison stood in the parking lot, staring at the doors.

She'd been standing there for five minutes.

This was ridiculous. She'd walked into boardrooms full of hostile investors. She'd negotiated with cartel lawyers in São Paulo. She'd stared down activist shareholders who wanted to gut companies for parts.

But a church? That made her want to get back in the car and drive away.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Grace: *Auntie Addie, did you go yet?*

Addison smiled despite herself. Grace had been asking about this for weeks — ever since Addison had mentioned, in passing, that she was thinking about becoming Grace's sponsor for Confirmation. Grace had lit up like someone had told her Christmas was coming early.

"You have to do RCIA first," Grace had explained, very seriously. "That's how you learn all the stuff."

"What stuff?" Addison had asked.

"You know. Jesus stuff. And saints. And why we eat the bread."

Addison had laughed. But Grace hadn't been joking.

And Elena — Elena had looked at her with something Addison couldn't quite name. Hope, maybe. Or relief. Like this was a door Addison had been standing outside for years, and she was finally thinking about opening it.

So here she was.

She typed back: *Going in now.*

Grace replied immediately: *YAY! Tell Father Thomas I said hi!*

Addison pocketed her phone and walked toward the doors.

Inside, the church was cool and quiet. The kind of quiet that felt intentional, like the building itself was holding its breath. Rows of wooden pews stretched toward the altar, where candles flickered in red glass holders. The air smelled like incense and old paper.

A woman in her sixties sat in the front office, typing at a computer that looked older than Addison. She glanced up and smiled.

"Can I help you?"

"I'm here to see Father Thomas," Addison said. "I have an appointment."

"Oh, you must be Addison. He's expecting you. I'll let him know you're here."

She disappeared down a hallway, leaving Addison alone in the waiting area. There were pamphlets on a side table — *What is the Mass?*, *Understanding the Sacraments*, *Why Be Catholic?* — and a framed photo of Pope Francis smiling like someone's friendly grandfather.

Addison picked up the pamphlet on sacraments and flipped through it. Baptism. Confirmation. Eucharist. The words were familiar in the way that half-remembered songs were familiar — she'd heard them before, maybe as a kid, maybe in passing. But they didn't mean anything.

Not yet.

"Addison Price?"

She turned. The man standing in the doorway was younger than she expected — maybe forty, with dark hair going gray at the temples and the kind of calm expression that made you think he'd heard worse than whatever you were about to say.

"Father Thomas," he said, extending a hand. "Thanks for coming."

His grip was firm but not performative. Addison liked that.

"Thanks for meeting with me," she said.

He led her down the hallway to a small office that smelled like coffee and old books. The walls were lined with shelves — theology, history, a few novels that looked well-read. A crucifix hung above the desk, simple wood, no ornamentation.

"Sit, please," he said, gesturing to a chair. "Can I get you anything? Coffee? Water?"

"I'm fine."

He sat across from her, hands folded on the desk. He didn't say anything right away, just looked at her with the kind of patience that felt unnerving.

Finally, Addison broke the silence. "I don't know if I believe."

Father Thomas smiled. "Most people who walk through that door don't. That's why they're here."

"Grace asked me to be her sponsor," Addison said. "For Confirmation. And I said yes. But I don't want to do it unless I mean it."

"That's a good instinct."

"So I figured... I should learn what I'm saying yes to."

"Also a good instinct." He leaned back in his chair. "What do you know about the faith?"

Addison shrugged. "Not much. I know there's Mass. And sacraments. And a lot of rules."

"True," Father Thomas said. "But that's like saying music is notes on a page. Technically accurate, but it misses the point."

"So what's the point?"

He thought for a moment. "The point is relationship. Everything else — the sacraments, the prayers, the rules — those are just the language we use to talk about God. But the point is the conversation."

Addison frowned. "I don't know if I'm good at that kind of conversation."

"Why not?"

"Because I'm good at things I can control. I'm good at strategy, at solving problems, at making plans. But faith?" She shook her head. "Faith is just... trusting something you can't see. And I'm not good at that."

Father Thomas nodded slowly. "You know what I think?"

"What?"

"I think you're better at it than you realize."

"How do you figure?"

"You said yes to Grace," he said. "You didn't have to. You could've told her no, told her to find someone who already believes. But you didn't. You said yes, even though you weren't sure. That's faith."

Addison looked at him, surprised. "That's not faith. That's just... love."

"What do you think faith is?"

She didn't have an answer for that.

Father Thomas pulled a binder off the shelf and opened it. "RCIA stands for the Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults. It's the process for people who want to join the Church — or people who want to understand what they're joining. It usually runs from fall to Easter."

"How does it work?"

"We meet once a week. We talk about scripture, theology, the sacraments. We explore what the Church teaches and why. And we ask questions. Lots of questions."

"What kind of questions?"

"Any kind. Why does God allow suffering? What's the point of the Eucharist? Why do Catholics care so much about Mary? Nothing's off-limits."

Addison felt a small knot of tension loosen in her chest. "So I don't have to pretend I believe things I don't?"

"Not at all," Father Thomas said. "In fact, I'd rather you didn't. Doubt is part of faith, Addison. If you never question, you never really choose."

She looked at the crucifix on the wall. "What if I go through this whole thing and I still don't believe?"

"Then you don't," he said simply. "No one's going to force you. But at least you'll know what you're saying no to."

That was fair.

"Okay," Addison said. "I'm in."

Father Thomas smiled. "Good. We start next Tuesday. Seven p.m."

They talked for another twenty minutes — logistics, mostly. What to bring (nothing), what to expect (conversation), whether she'd be the only one (no, there were three others). By the time Addison left, the knot in her chest had loosened a little more.

She sat in her car for a moment, staring at the church. The doors were still open, light spilling out into the evening. She thought about texting Grace, telling her it went well.

But instead, she called Elena.

"Hey," Elena answered. "How'd it go?"

"Good, I think. He's... not what I expected."

"Father Thomas is great. He doesn't push."

"Yeah, I noticed."

There was a pause. Addison could hear Grace in the background, asking if it was Auntie Addie.

"She's been waiting to hear from you," Elena said, amused.

"Tell her I said yes. I'm doing it."

"She's going to lose her mind."

Addison smiled. "Good."

Another pause, longer this time. Then Elena said, "Addie? I'm proud of you."

"For what? I haven't done anything yet."

"Yes, you have," Elena said quietly. "You showed up."

After they hung up, Addison sat in the silence of the car, watching the light fade over the parking lot. Showing up. That was all she'd done. And somehow, it felt like more than she expected.

The first RCIA session was the following Tuesday.

Addison arrived early, because she always arrived early, and found herself sitting in a classroom in the church basement. The chairs were arranged in a circle — no desks, no podium, just chairs. It felt less like school and more like... she wasn't sure what. Therapy, maybe.

There were four other people. A woman in her thirties who looked nervous. A man in his fifties who looked bored. A younger guy, maybe mid-twenties, who was scrolling through his phone. And an older woman who sat with her hands folded, serene.

Father Thomas walked in at exactly seven.

"Good evening, everyone. Welcome to RCIA. I'm Father Thomas, and for the next several months, we're going to be exploring what it means to be Catholic — or what it might mean, if you decide that's what you want."

He didn't use notes. Didn't pull out a PowerPoint. He just sat down in one of the chairs and looked at them.

"Let's start with introductions," he said. "Tell me your name and why you're here."

The nervous woman went first. "I'm Sarah. I'm getting married next year, and my fiancé is Catholic, so I thought I should... you know. Learn."

The bored man was next. "Jim. My wife's been asking me to do this for ten years. Figured I'd run out of excuses."

The younger guy didn't look up from his phone. "Kyle. My mom made me come."

The older woman smiled. "I'm Margaret. I've been curious about the Church for a long time. I figured it was time to stop being curious and start asking."

Then it was Addison's turn.

"I'm Addison," she said. "I'm here because my niece asked me to be her sponsor for Confirmation. And I don't know if I believe. But I want to understand."

Father Thomas nodded. "That's a good reason."

"Is it?" Addison asked. "It feels kind of selfish. Like I'm doing this for her, not for me."

"Why can't it be both?" Father Thomas said. "Love isn't selfish, Addison. And love is usually where faith starts."

That first night, they talked about creation.

Not in the way Addison expected — no arguments about Genesis versus evolution, no debates about seven days. Instead, Father Thomas asked a question: "What does it mean to say that the world is made in love?"

The group was quiet. Then Margaret spoke up.

"It means it's not an accident," she said. "It means we're not random."

"Good," Father Thomas said. "What else?"

"It means we matter," Sarah added quietly.

"Also good. Anything else?"

Addison hesitated, then said, "It means there's a purpose. Even when we can't see it."

Father Thomas looked at her. "Do you believe that?"

"I don't know," Addison admitted. "I want to."

"That's enough for now."

The sessions became a rhythm.

Every Tuesday, Addison drove to the church, sat in the circle, and listened. Sometimes she talked. Sometimes she just absorbed. The topics ranged from scripture to sacraments to the problem of evil. Father Thomas never lectured. He just asked questions and let the group wrestle with them.

One night, they talked about the Eucharist.

"Why do Catholics believe the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ?" Father Thomas asked.

Jim, the bored man, snorted. "Because it's magic?"

"That's one theory," Father Thomas said, smiling. "Anyone else?"

Addison thought about it. "It's not magic. It's transformation. You're saying that something ordinary becomes something sacred."

"Exactly," Father Thomas said. "And where else do we see that?"

"Baptism," Sarah said. "Water becomes holy."

"Marriage," Margaret added. "Two people become one."

Addison felt something shift in her chest. Transformation. That was the whole point, wasn't it? Not that you added something on top of what already existed, but that what already existed became something new.

She thought about Elena. About Kendra. About the way love had taken something ordinary — friendship, attraction, companionship — and made it sacred.

Maybe that was what faith was. Not believing impossible things, but recognizing the sacred in the ordinary.

One night, Addison stayed after the session.

Father Thomas was stacking chairs, and she helped him without asking.

"You've been quiet the last few weeks," he said. "Everything okay?"

"Yeah," Addison said. "I'm just... thinking."

"About?"

She hesitated. "Do you think faith is supposed to make sense?"

Father Thomas laughed. "Absolutely not."

"Then what's the point?"

"The point," he said, setting down a chair, "is that some things are true even when they don't make sense. Love doesn't make sense. Forgiveness doesn't make sense. Sacrifice doesn't make sense. But we do them anyway, because they matter."

"So faith is just... doing things that don't make sense?"

"No," he said. "Faith is trusting that the things that don't make sense are still worth doing."

Addison thought about that as she drove home. Trust. That was the word that kept coming up. Not certainty. Not proof. Just trust.

And maybe that was the hardest thing.

Because trust meant letting go.

One Tuesday in late November, Father Thomas asked a question that stopped Addison cold.

"Why are you here?"

"What do you mean?" she asked. "We've been over this."

"No," he said. "I mean right now. Tonight. You could've left weeks ago. You've learned enough to be Grace's sponsor. But you keep coming back. Why?"

Addison didn't answer right away. The rest of the group was watching her, curious.

"I don't know," she said finally. "I guess... I want to see where this goes."

"That's not an answer," Father Thomas said gently.

"Fine." Addison exhaled. "I keep coming back because I'm tired of being in control. I'm tired of managing everything, of fixing everything, of pretending I have all the answers. And I don't know if faith is real. But I know that sitting here, talking about things I can't control — it feels like breathing."

The room was quiet.

Then Margaret said, "That's the most honest thing I've heard all night."

Father Thomas nodded. "Faith isn't about having answers, Addison. It's about being okay with questions."

"I'm not okay with questions," Addison said. "Questions make me anxious."

"Then maybe," Father Thomas said, "that's what you're here to learn."

By December, Addison had stopped thinking of RCIA as something she had to do.

It became something she wanted to do. She looked forward to Tuesdays, to the circle of chairs, to the questions that didn't have easy answers. She even started reading the Bible — not cover to cover, but in pieces. The Gospels, mostly. She liked the stories. They felt real.

One night, she asked Father Thomas, "Do you think I'll ever believe?"

He smiled. "You already do."

"No, I don't."

"You show up every week. You ask questions. You care. That's belief, Addison. It just doesn't look the way you expected."

She thought about that as she drove home. Maybe he was right. Maybe belief wasn't a switch that flipped from off to on. Maybe it was a muscle you built, one question at a time.

The week before Christmas, Father Thomas announced that the group would attend Midnight Mass together.

"It's not required," he said. "But it's a good way to see the faith in action."

Addison agreed before she could talk herself out of it.

Midnight Mass was overwhelming.

The church was packed — standing room only. Candles flickered everywhere, and the air smelled like pine and incense. The choir sang in Latin, voices rising and falling like waves.

Addison stood in the back, watching. She didn't know the prayers. Didn't know when to kneel or stand or cross herself. But she watched the people around her, the way they moved through the rituals with the ease of long practice.

And she felt something.

Not certainty. Not proof. Just a sense that she was part of something bigger than herself. Something old and enduring and sacred.

When the priest raised the Eucharist and said, "This is my body, given for you," Addison felt tears sting her eyes.

She didn't know if she believed it.

But she wanted to.

Afterward, Father Thomas found her in the parking lot.

"How was it?" he asked.

"Overwhelming."

"Good overwhelming or bad overwhelming?"

Addison smiled. "I'm not sure yet."

He nodded. "Take your time. Faith isn't a race."

"Father?" Addison said. "Do you think Grace knows what she's asking for? When she asked me to be her sponsor?"

"Probably not," Father Thomas said. "But I think she knows what she's giving you."

"What's that?"

"A reason to try."

Addison drove home in the dark, the city quiet and still. She thought about Grace, about Elena, about the crucifix on Father Thomas's wall.

She thought about transformation — how ordinary things became sacred, how questions became faith, how doubt became trust.

And she thought, Maybe this is enough. Maybe showing up is enough.

Maybe that's all faith is.

When she got home, she texted Grace: *Merry Christmas, sweetheart. I went to Mass tonight.*

Grace replied immediately: *DID YOU LIKE IT?*

Addison smiled. *I think so. Ask me again in a few months.*

*OK! Love you Auntie Addie!*

*Love you too.*

Addison set her phone down and looked out the window at the dark street. Somewhere in the distance, church bells were ringing.

She didn't have answers.

But she had questions.

And maybe that was the whole point.

**End of Chapter 19**
