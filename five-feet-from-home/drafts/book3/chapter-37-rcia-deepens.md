# Chapter 37: RCIA Deepens

**POV: Addison**

---

The meeting room at St. Thomas More smelled like old coffee and furniture polish, the kind of scent that seemed to live permanently in every church building Addison Price had ever entered. She sat in one of the folding chairs arranged in a circle, her leather portfolio balanced on her lap like armor she wasn't quite ready to put down.

Seven weeks into RCIA—the Rite of Christian Initiation for Adults—and she still felt like an imposter.

Around her sat five other candidates, each at various stages of their own journeys toward Catholicism. Maria, the young nurse who worked nights at Carolina Medical and came to sessions with shadows under her eyes. David, the retired engineer who'd been married to a Catholic woman for forty years and was finally taking the plunge. Keisha, whose teenage daughter had started attending youth group and who'd decided to investigate what all the fuss was about.

And then there was Addison Price, crisis management specialist, managing partner at Barrett Security Solutions, woman who built her career on controlling chaos and containing disasters. Woman who had no idea what she was doing here.

Except she did know. She knew exactly why she'd walked through those doors seven weeks ago.

Because the people she loved had something she didn't. Faith. Community. A framework for understanding suffering that didn't involve compartmentalizing it into strategic advantages.

Because Elena had collapsed and almost died, and Addison had stood in that hospital room feeling utterly helpless for the first time in her adult life.

Because Grace had looked at her with those wide, perceptive eyes and asked if she wanted to come to Mass sometime, and Addison had said yes before her brain could catch up with her mouth.

Father Thomas entered the room with his usual unhurried grace, carrying a worn Bible and a thermos of what was almost certainly terrible coffee. He was in his mid-sixties, with silver hair and the kind of calm presence that suggested he'd heard every possible human confession and still managed to believe in redemption.

"Good evening, everyone," he said, settling into his chair. "Tonight I want us to talk about why we're here. Not the surface answer—not 'my spouse is Catholic' or 'I'm curious about the Church.' I want the real answer. The one that wakes you up at three in the morning."

Addison felt her stomach tighten.

Father Thomas looked around the circle, his gaze landing on each person with the kind of attention that made it impossible to hide. "Maria, why don't you start?"

Maria shifted in her seat, her fingers worrying at the strap of her bag. "I see people die," she said quietly. "At work. I'm there for the last moments, and sometimes they're peaceful, but sometimes they're terrified. And I realized I don't have anything to offer them except medical care. I want to be able to offer hope. Even if it's just... the possibility that death isn't the end."

Father Thomas nodded slowly. "Thank you for that honesty."

They went around the circle. David talked about wanting to share his wife's faith after years of politely declining. Keisha spoke about watching her daughter find purpose and wanting that for herself. Each answer was raw and real, stripped of the polish that people usually brought to church.

Then Father Thomas's gaze settled on Addison.

"Addison. Why do you want this? Really?"

She opened her mouth, prepared to give the answer she'd been rehearsing for weeks. Something about intellectual curiosity, about wanting to understand the framework that shaped Western civilization, about appreciating the ritual and tradition.

But under Father Thomas's steady gaze, all those careful words evaporated.

"Because the people I love have faith," she said finally. "And I want to understand what they know. What gives them stability when everything else is falling apart."

"Which people?"

Addison hesitated. The room felt too small suddenly, the air too thick.

"Elena Barrett," she said. "My business partner's wife. She's... she's important to me. When she collapsed, when we thought she might die, I watched her family gather around her. Watched them pray. Watched them find comfort in something I couldn't access. And I felt like I was standing outside looking in."

"That must have been difficult."

"It was terrifying." Addison's hands tightened on her portfolio. "I'm good at fixing things. It's literally my job. But I couldn't fix that. I couldn't control it. And the only people who seemed to have any peace about it were the ones who believed in something bigger than themselves."

Father Thomas was quiet for a long moment. "Is that what you're looking for? Peace?"

"I don't know," Addison admitted. "Maybe. Or maybe I'm just tired of standing outside."

"And what are you afraid of?"

The question landed like a punch. "Excuse me?"

"You heard me." Father Thomas's voice was gentle but firm. "What are you afraid of? Because I've been doing this long enough to know that when someone as accomplished as you walks through these doors, there's usually fear underneath all the intellectual curiosity."

Addison felt her throat tighten. Around the circle, the other candidates watched with varying degrees of sympathy and curiosity. She wanted to deflect, to redirect the conversation, to use any of the dozen techniques she'd mastered for controlling difficult moments.

Instead, she told the truth.

"I'm afraid that if I let myself believe, I'll have to change. That faith will require something from me that I'm not willing to give up." She paused, forcing herself to continue. "I've built my life on control. On being the smartest person in the room, on never being vulnerable, on solving problems through strategy and willpower. Faith seems to require the opposite. It seems to require surrender. And I don't know if I'm capable of that."

The silence that followed was heavy but not uncomfortable. Father Thomas nodded slowly, something like approval in his expression.

"That," he said, "is the most honest thing anyone has said in this room tonight. Thank you."

Addison felt something loosen in her chest—relief, maybe, or the beginning of it.

---

After the session ended, Addison lingered in the church parking lot, leaning against her car and breathing in the cool November air. The sky was dark, stars just beginning to emerge, and she felt wrung out in a way that had nothing to do with physical exhaustion.

She heard footsteps and turned to see Elena approaching, wrapped in a long wool coat, her hair caught in a loose braid.

"You came," Addison said, surprised.

"You asked me to." Elena stopped a few feet away, her expression soft. "How was it?"

"Intense." Addison exhaled slowly. "Father Thomas doesn't let you hide."

"No, he doesn't. That's what makes him good at this."

They stood in comfortable silence for a moment, the kind that only came from months of knowing each other beyond the surface level. Elena had started accompanying Addison to RCIA sessions three weeks ago, not as a candidate but as a sponsor—someone to walk alongside her through the process.

At first, Addison had resisted. She didn't need a babysitter, didn't need someone holding her hand through what felt like a deeply personal journey.

But Elena had insisted, gently and firmly, and Addison had found herself grateful. Having Elena there made the whole thing feel less like stepping into foreign territory alone and more like exploring something together.

"He asked me why I wanted this," Addison said finally.

"What did you tell him?"

"The truth. That I want to understand what you have. What gives you and Jasper and Grace that... foundation. That sense of being anchored to something bigger than yourselves."

Elena moved closer, close enough that Addison could smell her perfume—something floral and subtle. "And what did he say?"

"He asked me what I was afraid of."

"That's Father Thomas for you. Always going straight for the jugular." Elena's voice was warm with affection. "What did you tell him?"

Addison looked up at the stars, trying to find the words. "That I'm afraid faith will require me to surrender control. To stop relying on strategy and willpower and actually trust something I can't manage or manipulate."

"That's a fair fear."

"Is it?" Addison looked at her. "You don't seem afraid. None of you do. You make it look easy."

Elena laughed—soft and genuine. "Oh, Addie. It's not easy. It's never been easy. Faith isn't about having all the answers or never being afraid. It's about choosing to trust even when you can't see the outcome. Even when control is stripped away and all you have left is hope."

"That sounds terrifying."

"It is. But it's also freeing." Elena reached out and took Addison's hand, her fingers warm despite the November chill. "You've spent your whole life trying to control everything, to anticipate every variable, to never be caught off guard. How's that working for you?"

Addison thought about the sleepless nights, the constant anxiety, the way her mind never stopped running scenarios and contingencies. Thought about standing in that hospital room watching Elena fight for her life and feeling utterly, completely powerless.

"Not as well as I'd like," she admitted.

"Then maybe it's time to try something different." Elena squeezed her hand. "You don't have to have it all figured out tonight. You don't have to make any grand declarations. Just keep showing up. Keep being honest. Keep asking the hard questions."

"And if I can't do it? If I can't make that leap?"

"Then you can't. And that's okay too. But I don't think you'd still be here, seven weeks in, if some part of you wasn't ready to try."

Addison felt tears prick her eyes and blinked them back furiously. She didn't cry. Crying was a loss of control, a vulnerability she couldn't afford.

But Elena just pulled her into a hug, and Addison let herself lean into it. Let herself be held by this woman who had somehow become essential to her life in ways that terrified and comforted her in equal measure.

"Thank you," Addison whispered against Elena's shoulder. "For doing this with me. For not making me feel stupid or weak for not understanding."

"You could never be stupid or weak." Elena pulled back enough to look at her. "You're one of the strongest people I know, Addie. And strength isn't about never needing help. It's about knowing when to ask for it."

---

They went to dinner after—a quiet Italian place near the church that Elena swore made the best gnocchi in Charlotte. The restaurant was warm and dimly lit, with exposed brick walls and candles on every table.

Over wine and pasta, they talked about everything except faith. Work gossip from BSS, Grace's latest art project, Lucas's first attempts at rolling over. The kind of easy conversation that filled the space between deeper moments.

But when the check came and they were walking to their cars, Elena brought it up again.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Always."

"What you said tonight—about wanting to understand what we have. Is it just about faith? Or is it about something more?"

Addison stopped walking. The parking lot was nearly empty now, the streetlights casting long shadows across the pavement.

"What do you mean?"

Elena's expression was careful, gentle. "I mean that faith is part of it. But I wonder if what you're really seeking is permission. Permission to be part of something. Permission to belong in a way you've never let yourself belong before."

Addison felt her breath catch. "I don't—"

"You're not just Jasper's business partner, Addie. You're family. You're Grace's aunt, Lucas's godmother, my..." Elena paused, searching for the word. "My person. One of my people. And I think maybe part of what draws you to the Church is that it gives you a framework for being part of our family in a way that feels legitimate. Official."

"I already am part of your family."

"I know that. And you know that. But do you believe it?" Elena stepped closer. "Do you believe that you belong with us, not because of what you do for Jasper's company or how useful you are in a crisis, but just because we love you?"

The question hit harder than Father Thomas's had. Because it was true. All of it was true.

Addison had spent her entire life proving her worth through achievement, through competence, through being indispensable. She'd never learned how to just... be loved. How to accept that she was wanted for who she was rather than what she could do.

"I'm working on it," she said finally, her voice rough.

"Good." Elena smiled. "Because you are loved, Addie. By me, by Jasper, by Grace and Harper and everyone else in our crazy extended family. You don't have to earn it. You don't have to prove anything. You just have to show up and let yourself receive it."

"That might be harder than anything Father Thomas asks me to do."

"I know. But I think you can do it. I think you're already doing it."

They stood in the parking lot for another minute, just breathing together in the cold November air. Then Elena hugged her again—quick and fierce—and got in her car.

Addison drove home with the windows cracked, letting the cold air clear her head. She thought about Father Thomas's questions, about Elena's observations, about the parallel journeys she seemed to be on—toward faith and toward family, toward surrender and toward belonging.

Building something from nothing. That's what she did for a living—took crisis situations and constructed solutions out of chaos. Maybe that's what she was doing here too. Taking the empty spaces in her life and building something new. Something that looked like faith, like family, like home.

It was terrifying. But Elena was right—if she wasn't ready to try, she wouldn't still be showing up.

And Addison Price always showed up.

---

Later that night, lying in bed in her downtown apartment, Addison opened her laptop and started typing an email to Father Thomas.

*Dear Father Thomas,*

*Thank you for your question tonight. It's one I'll be thinking about for a while.*

*You asked what I'm afraid of, and I gave you the honest answer—that I'm afraid faith will require surrender, and surrender feels like weakness.*

*But there's another fear underneath that one. I'm afraid that if I let myself believe, I'll discover I've been wrong about everything. That all the control I've built my life around is actually just a prison. That the strength I'm so proud of is actually just a defense mechanism to keep people at a safe distance.*

*I'm afraid that faith will change me in ways I can't predict or manage. And I'm afraid that I'm not brave enough for that kind of transformation.*

*But I'm also afraid of staying exactly where I am. Of building a life that looks impressive from the outside but feels hollow on the inside. Of being the smartest, most capable person in every room and also the loneliest.*

*So I'm going to keep coming. I'm going to keep showing up, keep asking questions, keep letting you push me toward answers I'm not sure I'm ready for.*

*Because the people I love have something worth having. And if there's even a chance I could have it too—that I could belong the way they belong, believe the way they believe—then it's worth the fear.*

*Thank you for creating space for honesty. For not settling for easy answers. For pushing all of us toward the hard, true things underneath our careful defenses.*

*See you next week.*

*— Addison*

She read it over twice, deleted three sentences, added two more, then hit send before she could second-guess herself.

Her phone buzzed almost immediately. A text from Elena:

*Get home safe?*

*Just got in,* Addison replied.

*Good. I'm proud of you, you know. For doing this. For being brave enough to ask the hard questions.*

*I don't feel brave.*

*You never do. That's what makes it brave.*

Addison smiled and set her phone on the nightstand. Outside her window, the city lights glowed against the dark sky. Somewhere in the distance, church bells chimed the hour.

She thought about Father Thomas's question. About Elena's observation. About the journey she was on—toward faith, toward family, toward a version of herself that wasn't defined entirely by competence and control.

It was terrifying. It was also, maybe, the most important thing she'd ever done.

She turned off the light and let herself drift toward sleep, thinking about gnocchi and church parking lots and the slow, steady work of building something from nothing.

Tomorrow there would be cases to manage, crises to contain, fires to put out. Tomorrow she would be Addison Price, managing partner, problem solver, woman who had all the answers.

But tonight, she was just Addie. Woman with questions. Woman learning to surrender. Woman trying, against all her instincts, to believe.

And maybe—just maybe—that was enough.
