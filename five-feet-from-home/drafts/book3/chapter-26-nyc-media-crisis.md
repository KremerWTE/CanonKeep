# Chapter 26: NYC Media Crisis

**POV: Addison Barrett**
**Location: New York City**

The view from the forty-third floor of the Meridian Building looked out over Manhattan like a promise and a threat. Addison stood at the floor-to-ceiling windows of the executive suite, watching the city shimmer in the late April heat, and wondered if this was what power actually looked like—glass and steel and the ability to be untouchable even when everything was burning down around you.

"Addison." Jasper's voice pulled her back from the window. "We need to review the timeline before the board meeting."

She turned away from the view and crossed to where her uncle had spread a series of documents across the massive conference table. Seven days in New York City. That's what Jasper had told her when he'd pulled her aside two weeks ago with the Meridian brief. "This is your project," he'd said. "I'll be there for counsel, but you're running point."

Now, on day five, she was beginning to understand the full scope of what she'd been handed.

Marcus Webber, CEO of Meridian Media Group, was facing allegations. The first story had broken in Vanity Fair three weeks ago—three women, all former employees, describing a pattern of inappropriate behavior spanning fifteen years. Within seventy-two hours, two more publications had run similar stories. Within a week, the board was calling for his resignation.

But the situation wasn't as clean as the headlines suggested.

"Walk me through what we know versus what we suspect," Jasper said, settling into one of the leather chairs that probably cost more than most people's monthly rent.

Addison pulled up her tablet and began. "Confirmed: Marcus had affairs with at least two of the women who've come forward. Both were consensual at the time, both occurred when the women were subordinates. Clear ethics violations, clear abuse of power dynamics."

"But not illegal," Jasper noted.

"Not illegal," Addison agreed. "Fireable, reputation-destroying, morally indefensible—but not criminal."

"And the other allegations?"

"That's where it gets complicated." Addison swiped through her notes. "Three of the women describe behavior that crosses into harassment—unwanted advances, inappropriate comments, quid pro quo implications. But the timelines are inconsistent, the details don't match up with verified facts, and at least two of the accusers have documented connections to Caine Media."

Jasper's expression didn't change, but Addison saw the slight shift in his posture. Caine Media was Meridian's primary competitor. If they were orchestrating false allegations to destroy Webber's credibility and tank Meridian's stock price...

"Show me the connections," Jasper said.

Addison pulled up the file she'd been building for three days. "Accuser number four—Jessica Raines. According to her LinkedIn, she left Meridian eighteen months ago. But her financial records show a payment of fifty thousand dollars from an LLC that traces back to Caine's VP of Strategic Communications."

"That's not proof of fabrication," Jasper said. "Could be a settlement."

"Could be," Addison agreed. "Except the payment was made six months after she left Meridian, and two weeks before the Vanity Fair story broke. And there's this—" She pulled up a series of text messages her team had obtained through methods she'd learned not to ask too many questions about. "Correspondence between Raines and a journalist at Vanity Fair. 'Make sure you emphasize the pattern.' 'Connect it to the other stories.' This isn't someone coming forward reluctantly. This is someone with a narrative to sell."

Jasper studied the messages, his expression unreadable. "And accuser number five?"

"Sarah Chen. She does have a legitimate complaint—Marcus made comments about her appearance at a company event, asked her to dinner in a way that was clearly not professional. But the story she told to the press escalated those incidents into something more serious. The timeline she describes has her alone with Marcus in his office on a night when we have security footage showing he was in Los Angeles."

"So some of the allegations are true," Jasper said slowly. "And some are fabricated or exaggerated. Potentially orchestrated by a competitor looking to destroy Meridian's leadership and depress their stock value."

"That's my assessment," Addison said.

"Which creates a problem."

"Several problems," Addison corrected. She stood and began pacing, the way she always did when she was working through complex variables. "Problem one: Marcus is guilty of serious ethical violations and abuse of power. He doesn't deserve to be protected from the consequences of his actual behavior."

"Problem two?" Jasper prompted.

"Problem two: if we expose the fabricated allegations, we risk undermining all the accusers, including the ones telling the truth. We feed into the narrative that women lie about harassment."

"Problem three?"

"Problem three: Meridian's stock has dropped twenty-three percent since this started. If we don't stabilize the situation, you're looking at a hostile takeover situation within six months. Probably by Caine, who's perfectly positioned to swoop in and acquire Meridian at a discount."

Jasper nodded slowly. "So we need to remove Marcus from power in a way that holds him accountable for his actual misconduct, protect the legitimate accusers, expose the fabricated allegations without discrediting the real ones, and stabilize Meridian's stock price before Caine can make a move."

"Right," Addison said. "Simple."

Jasper almost smiled. "Tell me your strategy."

This was the moment. The strategy she'd been developing for five days, running scenarios and war-gaming outcomes until she'd found the narrow path through this minefield.

"We split the difference," Addison said. "Marcus resigns, effective immediately. Not because of the allegations—because of 'personal reasons' and a 'desire to spend time with family.'"

"He'll never agree to that," Jasper said.

"He will if we give him the right incentive. We structure his exit package to include full pension, continued health insurance, and a non-disparagement clause that protects his reputation from further attacks by Meridian."

"In exchange for?"

"In exchange for his cooperation in our investigation into which allegations are credible and which aren't. We hire an independent investigator—someone with impeccable credentials and no connection to either Meridian or Caine. They review every allegation, interview every accuser, examine all the evidence."

Jasper was tracking her logic now, leaning forward slightly. "And when they find the fabricated allegations?"

"We don't announce it publicly," Addison said. "We quietly share the findings with the journalists who broke the original stories. Give them the evidence of Caine's involvement. Let them decide if they want to run corrections or follow-ups about media manipulation."

"That puts the onus on the press to police themselves," Jasper noted.

"It does. And it might not work. But it's better than Meridian making accusations that look like corporate retaliation against victims."

"What about the legitimate accusers?" Jasper asked.

"We reach out to them directly. Apologize on behalf of Meridian's board for the environment that allowed Marcus's behavior. Offer meaningful settlements that include therapy coverage, career support, and NDAs that protect them from retaliation."

"And if they don't want NDAs?"

"Then we don't require them," Addison said firmly. "This isn't about silencing victims. It's about making genuine amends for real harm while also protecting the company from false allegations."

Jasper was quiet for a long moment, running through the strategy from every angle. Addison waited, her heart rate slightly elevated despite her outward calm. This was the biggest solo operation she'd ever run. If she got it wrong...

"It's good," Jasper said finally. "But there's one piece missing."

"What?"

"Meridian needs new leadership. Not just an interim CEO, but someone who can rebuild credibility and navigate the company through this crisis."

Addison had thought about this too. "What about Rachel Kim? She's currently COO, she's been with Meridian for twelve years, she has a spotless record, and she's publicly committed to diversity and ethical workplace culture."

"The board will want someone with CEO experience," Jasper said.

"Then we make the case that this is exactly the moment to promote from within. Show continuity, demonstrate commitment to change, elevate a woman to the top position as a clear signal that Meridian is serious about reform."

Jasper nodded slowly. "That could work. Especially if we position it correctly in the press."

"I've already drafted talking points," Addison said, pulling up another document. "The narrative is: Meridian takes accountability seriously. Marcus's resignation isn't about denying allegations or escaping consequences—it's about making space for new leadership that can guide the company forward. Rachel Kim represents both continuity and change. She knows the business, but she also represents a fundamental shift in company culture."

"You've been busy," Jasper observed.

"I've been thorough," Addison corrected.

Her phone buzzed. A message from their contact at the New York Times: "Hearing rumors Caine is preparing a hostile takeover bid. Can you confirm?"

Addison showed the message to Jasper. "We're running out of time."

"Then let's execute," Jasper said. He stood and straightened his tie—a small gesture that Addison had learned meant he was shifting into combat mode. "I'll handle the board. You talk to Rachel Kim, make sure she's willing to step up. Then we need Marcus to agree to the exit package."

"I can handle Marcus," Addison said.

Jasper raised an eyebrow. "You sure? He's not going to make it easy."

"I know," Addison said. "But this needs to come from me, not you. If you do it, it looks like BSS forcing him out. If I do it, it's a strategic advisor helping him see his best path forward."

"All right," Jasper agreed. "But I'm on standby if you need backup."

They spent the next hour preparing. Jasper made calls to board members, laying groundwork and testing receptivity to their strategy. Addison reached out to Rachel Kim's assistant and arranged a meeting for later that afternoon.

At 2 PM, Addison sat across from Rachel Kim in a coffee shop three blocks from Meridian's headquarters. Rachel was fifty-two, elegant in a way that suggested confidence rather than vanity, and she'd been navigating Meridian's executive ranks long enough to know when something significant was happening.

"You're here about Marcus," Rachel said before Addison could start her pitch.

"I am," Addison confirmed. "How much do you know about BSS's involvement?"

"I know the board hired you to manage the crisis. I know Marcus is probably going to resign or be fired within the week. I know Caine is circling." Rachel sipped her coffee. "What I don't know is what happens next."

"That depends on you," Addison said.

She laid out the strategy—Marcus's resignation, the independent investigation, the approach to the accusers. Rachel listened without interrupting, her expression neutral.

"And you want me to step up as CEO," Rachel said when Addison finished.

"I think you're the right choice. Not just for optics, but for actual leadership. You know the company, you have relationships with the key players, and you have the credibility to navigate this transition."

"I also have a target on my back the minute I take the job," Rachel said. "Everyone will be watching to see if I'm just a placeholder or if I can actually lead."

"That's true," Addison acknowledged. "But you've been preparing for this role for a decade. And Meridian needs someone who can handle scrutiny and pressure. That's you."

Rachel was quiet for a moment. "What about the accusers? The real ones, the ones Marcus actually hurt?"

"We make it right," Addison said. "As right as we can. Settlements, support, accountability. Not hush money—genuine amends."

"And the fake ones?"

"We expose the manipulation without undermining the credible allegations," Addison said. "It's a narrow path, but it's the right one."

Rachel nodded slowly. "Okay. I'm in. But I want full authority to restructure Meridian's HR policies and implement real accountability measures."

"You'll have it," Addison promised. "The board will support reforms, especially if you're the one leading them."

They shook hands, and Addison felt a small victory click into place. One piece of the puzzle secured.

At 5 PM, she met with Marcus Webber in his office. He looked like he hadn't slept in a week—which, based on the bags under his eyes, was probably accurate.

"You're here to tell me I'm finished," Marcus said flatly.

"I'm here to tell you that you have options," Addison corrected. She sat down across from him without waiting for an invitation. "You can fight this—go to war with the board, contest every allegation, drag this out for months. Or you can take control of the narrative and exit on terms that preserve what's left of your reputation."

"By admitting I'm guilty?"

"By acknowledging that your time at Meridian is over and making a graceful exit that doesn't destroy everything you built."

She walked him through the exit package, the independent investigation, the strategy for separating credible allegations from fabricated ones.

Marcus listened, his expression cycling through anger, resignation, and finally something that looked like relief.

"What happens to the women I actually hurt?" he asked quietly.

It was the first thing he'd said that made Addison think he might actually have a conscience buried under the ego and entitlement.

"They get settlements and support," Addison said. "And you cooperate with the investigation so that their stories are validated and the fake ones are exposed. You don't get to escape accountability for what you actually did. But you also don't get destroyed by lies."

Marcus was quiet for a long time. Then: "When do I resign?"

"Tomorrow morning," Addison said. "Press conference at 10 AM. You announce you're stepping down for personal reasons. Rachel Kim is named interim CEO. The board announces the independent investigation. Clean, clear, quick."

"And then what?"

"Then you disappear for a while," Addison said. "Take the generous exit package, spend time with your family, and stay quiet while the investigation runs its course."

Marcus nodded slowly. "Okay. Let's do it."

Addison left his office feeling the weight of what she'd just negotiated. Not a perfect resolution—there was no such thing in a situation like this. But a managed outcome that protected victims, preserved what was salvageable, and prevented a predatory competitor from exploiting the crisis.

That evening, she and Jasper sat in the hotel bar reviewing the day's progress.

"Rachel's on board," Addison reported. "Marcus agreed to the exit package. The independent investigator starts Monday. And I've got a call scheduled with the Times to give them background on the Caine connection."

"Good," Jasper said. He sipped his scotch, studying her. "How do you feel about it?"

Addison considered the question. "Like I'm complicit in protecting someone who doesn't deserve it."

"Are you?"

"I don't know," she admitted. "Marcus is guilty of real misconduct. Women were harmed because he abused his power. And I just negotiated a deal that lets him walk away with his pension intact."

"You also ensured that the women he hurt get meaningful compensation and support," Jasper pointed out. "And you're exposing the manipulation that was using their pain as a weapon in a corporate takeover fight."

"Is that enough?"

"It's the best outcome available in an imperfect situation," Jasper said. "That's what we do, Addison. We operate in the gray areas where there are no clean solutions. We find the least-bad option and execute it perfectly."

"It feels like compromise," Addison said.

"It is compromise," Jasper agreed. "But compromise isn't always weakness. Sometimes it's wisdom."

Addison thought about that as she finished her drink. Wisdom. The word her mother's inner circle had claimed for themselves. Sapientia Minervae.

"This was a test, wasn't it?" she said suddenly. "The wives. They wanted to see if I could handle complexity at this level."

Jasper didn't deny it. "They're considering you for succession. For my role, eventually. This was your audition."

"Did I pass?"

"You'll find out when we get back to Charlotte," Jasper said. "But for what it's worth? You did good work this week. Better than good."

They spent two more days in New York managing the fallout. The press conference went smoothly—Marcus read his prepared statement, Rachel was introduced as interim CEO, and the independent investigation was announced. The initial media response was cautiously positive. Meridian's stock stabilized and then ticked upward.

The Times ran a story three days later about potential media manipulation in the Meridian allegations, citing anonymous sources but clearly working from the information Addison had provided. It was enough to raise questions without undermining the credible accusers.

On their last night in the city, Addison stood at the window of her hotel room looking out at Manhattan. Forty-three floors up, surrounded by glass and steel and the architecture of power.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Kendra: "How's New York?"

Addison stared at the message for a long moment before typing back: "Complicated."

Three dots appeared, then disappeared, then appeared again. Finally: "Aren't you used to that by now?"

Addison smiled despite herself. "Getting there."

She put her phone away and turned back to the view. Getting there. That was the truth of it. She was learning to navigate the gray areas, to make impossible choices, to carry the weight of power without being crushed by it.

Whether that made her ready for what came next—that was still an open question.

But she was getting there.
